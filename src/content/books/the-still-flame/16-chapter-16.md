Ten minutes earlier, before the lights went out, Romeo had been having the best night of his life.

He would remember that afterwards, with a kind of wonder. He had gone left every single time. Susan had laughed, properly, with her head back, in the middle of her mother's ballroom. He had watched his sister let a boy lead her onto a dance floor with one arm in a sling. Lord Fennimore had not bitten him again. Things, as far as Romeo could tell, had been going extremely well.

Then Susan's father had set his glass down on a footman's tray, and smiled at the Speaker, and walked away towards the kitchens, and Susan's hand had gone cold in his.

"The light on the hill," she'd said. "He's going."

And Romeo had looked at her face, and known exactly what she was going to do, and known that nothing he said would stop her, and so he had not wasted any breath trying.

"Back stairs," he'd said instead. "Then the kitchen garden. I'll tell the horses we're coming."

---

They did not take the horses in the end. Vesper was already gone from her stall when they reached the stables, the door swinging, the saddle missing from its peg. Old Bastiano was asleep in his chair by the tack room stove with his mouth open.

"He's on the mare," Romeo said. "Ten minutes ahead. Maybe less."

"Then we run. Vesper's old and her knees are bad. She'll take the bridle path, all switchbacks, at a walk. The footpath goes straight up the terraces."

They ran. Out through the back gate, the one Vesper had shown him in her tired old memory, and up into the vines.

It was a long climb. The terraces went up and up, row after row, each one a little steeper than the last, the vines heavy with the late harvest and the paths between them narrow and stony. Susan ran in her ballgown with the skirt bunched in one fist and her shoes in the other. She ran well. Better than he did; she had grown up on these slopes and knew every turn. Romeo ran behind her in Matteo's second-best dinner jacket and wished, with his whole heart, that he had been born a goat.

Sergeant was not with them. Sergeant had been sulking on the stable roof since Lord Fennimore had stolen his biscuit that afternoon, and had refused, flatly, to come. So Romeo did what he could without him.

He reached out, as he ran, into the dark around them.

The vineyard was full of small lives. Field mice in the roots. A hedgehog, waddling between the rows, offended by the noise. Moths. A fox on the terrace above, frozen, watching them pass with bright, unfriendly eyes. And higher up, much higher, gliding silent above the top of the hill on great soft wings, an owl.

Romeo touched the owl. Gently. *Please.*

The owl was old, and wise, and had very little time for humans. But it was curious. It let him in, just a little.

And for a moment Romeo was flying.

High over the dark hill. Over the black ranks of vines and the pale scars of the paths between them. Over the last terrace, and the scrub beyond it, and the ruined shrine on the summit: a broken ring of old stone walls, roofless, with an empty archway facing the house. And in the middle of the ruin, a fire. A small one, in a ring of stones. Beside it, a grey mare, tied, her head low.

And two men.

One of them, Romeo knew. Aurelio de Ricci, in his evening clothes, standing very straight with his hands at his sides.

The other one...

The owl did not like the other one. The owl wheeled away from him, high and wide, and Romeo felt it go, and let it, and came back into himself on a stony path halfway up the hill with his heart going like a hammer.

"What?" Susan said. She'd stopped, a few rows above him, breathing hard. "Romeo. What did you see?"

"Your father," he said. "And someone else."

"Who?"

Romeo opened his mouth, and found he did not know how to answer.

He had a theory that everyone was an animal. He'd had it for years. It had never once failed him. Varga was a heron. Teyl was a tortoise. Susan was a cat. Every human he had ever met, he could look at for a minute and see the shape of the creature underneath.

He had looked at the man in the ruin through the eyes of an owl, and he had not seen an animal at all.

He had seen a forest fire.

---

They went the last part slowly. On their bellies, in the end, through the scrub above the last terrace, with the loose stones Vesper hated shifting under their elbows. Susan's ballgown was torn to the knee. She did not seem to have noticed.

They stopped behind a fallen block of the old shrine wall, where a gap in the stones gave them a view into the ruin.

The fire in the ring of stones was very small. Smaller than a campfire. But the light it gave was strange. Too bright for its size, and too steady, and too gold. It lit the inside of the broken walls, and the empty archway, and the faces of the two men standing beside it.

Romeo could see Aurelio clearly. He looked older than he had in the ballroom. His face was grey.

The other man stood with his back half turned. He was very tall, taller than Aurelio by a head, in a long dark coat that hung to his boots. His hair was dark and cut short. Romeo could not see his face. But he could see his hands. The man was holding them loosely at his sides, open, and in the firelight they looked wrong. Too smooth. Shiny, almost, like old polished wood, or like skin that had been burned so many times it had forgotten how to scar.

Romeo felt Susan, beside him, stop breathing.

"...in my house," Aurelio was saying. His voice was low and shaking. Romeo had never heard that voice come out of that man. "You swore to me. Twenty years ago, on this hill. Never in my house. Never near my family."

"I am not in your house," said the tall man.

His voice was not what Romeo expected. It was quiet. Low and warm and even, a voice that had never needed to be raised. It was the kind of voice you would trust instantly if you didn't know better.

"You took the light out of it," said Aurelio. "Every candle. With the Assembly in that room. With the *Zetans* in that room. With my daughter."

"Your daughter was dancing," said the man. "She dances very well. Better than the boy she was dancing with, though he is trying hard."

Romeo, flat on his stomach in the scrub, decided he would think about that later.

"Was it you?" Aurelio's voice cracked. "The knife. Was it yours?"

The man was quiet for a moment. The little fire in the ring of stones burned very straight.

"No," he said.

"Don't lie to me."

"I have never once lied to you, Aurelio. In twenty years. That is the only reason you still come up this hill." The man turned his head slightly, towards the house below, its windows still dark. "The knife was not mine. I knew it was coming. I did not send it."

"You knew, and you put out the lights for it."

"I put out the lights for my own reasons. The knife took advantage." A pause. "And the knife failed, didn't it? Your guest still breathes. I would wager on it. The girl with the wind made sure of that."

Romeo's whole body went cold.

*The girl with the wind.* He knew Jules. He knew what she'd have done in a dark room with an assassin in it. And this man, up here on a hill a mile from the house, knew it too.

"Then whose was it?" Aurelio said. "Whose knife?"

"Someone who wants the truce broken more than I do." The man's voice was very gentle. "Someone who wants the Zetans dead in a human house, so there will be no pipeline and no peace and no choice but war. Someone who would like the whole of the west to be wide open." He tilted his head. "Ask who hired your new footman, Aurelio. Ask through which agency. And then ask your son why he chose it."

Aurelio de Ricci took a step back, as if he had been struck.

Beside Romeo, Susan made a very small sound in her throat.

The tall man turned his head.

Not quickly. Not like someone startled. Slowly, the way a cat turns towards a sound in the grass. And the little fire in the ring of stones leaned, very slightly, in the same direction. Towards the fallen block of wall. Towards the gap in the stones.

Towards them.

"You can come out, Susan," said the man. "You and your friend. Your heart is very loud."

---

Romeo would have stayed down. Every instinct he had, every lesson from every barracks of his childhood, was telling him to stay flat in the scrub, very still, and let the big dangerous thing decide they weren't worth the trouble.

Susan stood up.

She stood up in her torn ballgown with her shoes in her hand and her hair coming down around her face and her ring on her finger, and walked out from behind the wall into the light of the fire, and stopped in the empty archway of the shrine, and looked at her father.

Romeo swore under his breath, and stood up, and followed her.

"Susan," said Aurelio. His face had gone from grey to white. "Susan, *cara*, go back to the house. Now."

"No," said Susan.

She was looking at her father. Not at the man in the coat. At her father.

"I found your ring, Papa," she said. "At the dye works on Tannery Street. In the rubble, after an Ashen charge brought the floor down on my captain. *A. de R.*" Her voice was perfectly level. "You told me you lost it on the terraces."

The silence on the hilltop went on for a long time.

Aurelio did not try to answer. He looked at his daughter, at her torn dress and her ring and her face, and something in his own face slowly came apart, like a wall that had been holding back a great deal of water for a very long time.

The tall man watched them both. Then his eyes moved past Susan, to Romeo, standing a pace behind her in Matteo's ruined jacket.

Romeo looked back.

Up close, in the strange gold light, he could see the man's face at last. He was older than Romeo had thought. Fifty, perhaps. Lean, and dark-skinned, with deep lines carved round his mouth and eyes, and a short grizzled beard. Handsome, in a severe way. And the shape of him...

Romeo had seen that face before. He'd seen them across the mess table every morning for eleven months. He'd seen them on a laundry roof in the Fourth District, wet, listening to a hymn. He'd seen them ten minutes ago in a ballroom, turned towards his sister in a green dress with every candle by the window leaning after them.

The same brow. The same jaw. The same way of tilting his head when he listened.

Only this face was old, and very tired, and had seen a great many fires.

"The beast-speaker," said the man, almost kindly. "Vasquez. The other twin. You came up here on the eyes of an owl, I think. The owl doesn't like me. They never do." He tilted his head. "Your sister is coming up the hill now. Very fast, for a woman with one arm. And she is not alone."

Romeo felt it too, then. Not with his own senses; with the fox's, still crouched on the terrace below, ears flat. Two people coming up through the vines. Running. One of them light and quick and fierce. The other one...

The little fire in the ring of stones jumped. Just once. A sudden bright leap, like a heart missing a beat.

The tall man went very still.

For the first time all night, something moved in his face. Something Romeo could not read. It was there and gone in an instant, like a fish rising and sinking in dark water.

"Not tonight," the man said softly. To himself, Romeo thought. Not to any of them.

Then he turned to Aurelio.

"Ask your son," he said. "Before the Zetans do. They have already started listening."

He raised one hand, open, palm out, the way the figures on the walls of the Undercroft had raised theirs. And the little fire in the ring of stones rose up off the ground in a single gold ribbon and wrapped itself around his wrist like a living thing, and the whole hilltop went dark.

Romeo blinked.

When his eyes cleared, there was nothing in the ruin but Aurelio de Ricci, and Susan, and himself, and the grey mare tied by the wall, trembling all over.

And on the old stone of the shrine's inner wall, just beside the empty archway, a handprint. Five fingers, spread. Burned black into the rock. Still glowing faintly orange at the edges, like the last of a coal.

---

They heard the others before they saw them. Feet on stone. Hard breathing. And then Juliette came over the top of the last terrace and into the ruin like a gust of wind, her green dress torn, her face white with pain, her eyes going round the broken walls in one quick hawk's sweep: Susan. Romeo. Aurelio. The mare. The handprint, still smoking. The empty dark beyond.

"Gone," Romeo said. "Just now. He knew you were coming."

Juliette's eyes came to rest on him. They stayed there for a moment. Checking. Counting. *Hands, feet, can you feel everything.* Then something in her shoulders let go, and she breathed out, and nodded once.

And then Dimitri came through the archway behind her.

He was breathing hard. Matteo's good suit was torn at the shoulder. His hands were in his pockets, exactly where Juliette had told him to keep them, and Romeo could see, in the faint orange glow from the handprint on the wall, that they were shaking.

He stopped. He looked at the handprint. For a long time.

Then he looked up, and across the ruin, and saw Aurelio de Ricci.

And Aurelio saw him.

Romeo would never forget the older man's face. He had seen many faces in his life. Frightened ones and grieving ones and furious ones, at the edges of wars. He had never seen one like this. Aurelio de Ricci looked at Dimitri Gale across the dark ruin on top of his own hill as if he were looking at a ghost. As if someone had reached back twenty years and pulled a door open that he had spent his whole life holding shut.

He took one step towards Dimitri. Then stopped, as if he didn't trust his legs.

"Oh," said Aurelio de Ricci, very quietly, in the voice of a man who has been punched. "Oh, God of the Sown."

Dimitri didn't move. "Sir?"

Aurelio's hand had come up to his mouth. Above it, his eyes were wet.

"You have her eyes," he said.

And nobody on the hilltop said anything at all.
