The Omicron Theory hall was the warmest room on base, which was the only reason anyone stayed awake in it.

It was a long, curved lecture theatre with tiered benches and tall windows, and at the front, behind the lectern, the whole wall was taken up by a map. Not a screen. A real map, painted on plaster a hundred years ago by somebody who had clearly loved the work, with little sea serpents in the margins and a compass rose the size of a cartwheel. Romeo had been staring at it twice a week for four years. He knew it better than he knew his own face.

He was staring at it now, because the alternative was listening.

The great curve of Eta filled the middle of the wall, lying on its side like a sleeping animal. Along its eastern edge, the human coast: a long ribbon of green with the cities strung along it like beads. Haven in the middle, fat and gold, where the Assembly sat and the EDF had its headquarters, and where Romeo was currently sitting on a bench trying not to yawn. North of it, the grey harbour of Varra, which he did not look at for long. South of it, the long green thumb of the southern basin, where the great river came down out of the hills and spread into a delta, and where, if you looked closely, the painter had put a tiny cluster of vines and a little gold key.

West of the coast, the land rose. That was the Reach: a high wild band of hill and scrub and broken forest, painted in browns and dusty greens, with little drawings of beasts in it that the painter had clearly never seen and had made up from descriptions. Somewhere in there, a creature that was meant to be a hill-cat and looked more like a very angry sheep.

At the far western edge of the Reach ran a jagged line of dark hills. Somebody, sometime, had scratched a small cross into the plaster beside them, and nobody had ever painted over it. The Kesh Ridge.

Beyond the Ridge, the colours changed completely. Tan and ochre and pale gold, rolling away westward to the edge of the wall. The Sere. The Zetan deserts. A few cities marked in a script that wasn't human, with careful translations underneath.

The whole northern half of the continent was green so dark it was almost black. Forest, unbroken, all the way to the northern coast, and then a sea painted the same deep colour, as if the forest simply went on under the water. Across it, in faded red letters: *Nuite Territories. No Transit.*

And above everything, at the very top of the wall, where the forest and the desert met, a long white spine of mountains. Snow peaks, painted with tiny strokes of silver. The painter had not labelled them. Somebody later had written in pencil, very small, almost apologetically: *the Spine (Muite)*.

And off the edges of Eta, on all sides, nothing. Blue sea and blank plaster. A few sketchy coastlines at the very corners, half-finished, like a thought somebody had started and lost interest in.

*Psi is wide*, Romeo thought. He had never really looked at those blank corners before.

"...which brings us," said Dr Pell from the lectern, "to the question of affinity. Cadet Vasquez. The male one. Since you seem so fascinated by the map, perhaps you'd remind us what an omicron actually is."

The hall turned to look at him. Beside him, Dimitri very carefully did not laugh.

Romeo stood up.

---

Dr Imre Pell was a very small, very old man with very large spectacles and a voice like a door that needed oiling. He had been teaching Omicron Theory at the EDF for forty years. He was rumoured to have a trace of every single element and to have never once used any of them for anything more dramatic than stirring his tea.

Romeo liked him enormously. He was a heron's grandfather: dusty, patient, and more dangerous than he looked.

"An omicron, Doctor," Romeo said, "is a very small particle that lives in the air of Psi and nowhere else that we know of. We breathe it in. All of us, all our lives, from our first breath. Most of it we breathe straight out again. But some of it stays. It settles into the nerves, and the brain, and the marrow, and it binds there."

"Go on."

"How much of it binds, and how tightly, is your load. That's what they measure when you're seeded, and when you're reassessed at eighteen. Load is how much power you've got. It's a number. A big number means you've crossed the threshold and the EDF sends very polite people to collect you." A few cadets laughed. "But load isn't what you can do. Load is just how big the engine is."

"And what you can do?"

"Is affinity." Romeo shrugged. "The bound omicrons tune themselves to something. Nobody really knows why. Some people's tune to air, some to water, some to animals or plants or the pull of things. Like bells all ringing one note. Your affinity's the note."

Dr Pell regarded him over the top of his enormous spectacles.

"That," he said, "is the first time in four years you've given me a correct answer without a joke in it. Sit down before you ruin it."

Romeo sat. Dimitri elbowed him. He elbowed back.

"Load," said Dr Pell, turning to the board, "we can measure with a machine. Affinity, we cannot. A machine will tell you a cadet has the engine of a cargo hauler. It will not tell you whether that engine drives wheels or wings or propellers." He chalked three words, slowly, in a spidery hand: *LOAD. AFFINITY. TRIAL.* "So when a cadet comes to us with a heavy load and no history of using it, we hold an affinity trial. Yes, Cadet Okoro?"

A girl near the front had her hand up. "Is that the room with the cages?"

"It is not a room with cages," said Dr Pell, with great weariness. "It is a room with seven stations. At each one, the subject is exposed, under controlled conditions, to one domain. A moving current of air. A basin of water. A tray of soil. A living plant. A living animal, *in a box*, Cadet Okoro, not a cage. A set of free-hanging weights. And a flame." He tapped the board. "Bound omicrons respond to their own note. Even in someone who has never used their gift, the instruments will register a sympathy. The air stirs. The water trembles. The flame leans. You read the trial, you know the affinity, and you train the cadet accordingly."

Romeo felt Dimitri go very still beside him.

He didn't look. He kept his eyes on the board. But he could feel it the way you feel an animal freeze in the grass next to you, every muscle locked.

"And if nothing leans?" Romeo said.

He hadn't meant to say it out loud. Dr Pell turned and looked at him with an expression of mild interest.

"Then, Cadet Vasquez, you have a sleeper."

The word dropped into the warm room like a stone into a pond.

"A heavy load," said Dr Pell, "and no affinity at all. Every station dead. It is rare, but not unheard of. Perhaps one in a thousand of our threshold cases. We call them unaligned, officially. Everyone else calls them sleepers." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve. "Most sleepers never wake. They carry an enormous load their whole lives, and it does nothing. They make perfectly good soldiers. Sometimes better ones; they never come to rely on a gift."

"Most?" said someone.

"Most." Dr Pell put his spectacles back on. "The literature records a handful who woke late. Almost always under extreme stress. Danger. Shock. Grief. The bound omicrons are tuned, but something is holding the bell still, and then one day something strikes it hard enough." He shrugged his thin shoulders. "We don't understand it. So the EDF does what it always does with things it doesn't understand. It keeps the sleeper in general training, and it retests them once a year, on the anniversary of their first trial, and it writes down the result."

Once a year.

Romeo did the arithmetic without wanting to. Reassessment at eighteen. Eleven months ago.

He heard Dimitri breathe in, very slowly, the way Jules had taught him. In. Out.

"Doctor," Romeo said. "What's holding the bell still?"

Dr Pell looked at him for a long moment. Then he did something Romeo had never seen him do in four years. He smiled.

"Ah," he said. "Now that is the good question. And the honest answer is that we do not know. Some think it's simply a defect. A bell cast badly, that can't ring." He leaned on the lectern. "There are other ideas. Older ones. The Zetans have a story, and so, I am told, do the Muites, though they don't tell it to us. The story goes that a very strong wielder, a true master, can reach into another person's load and lay a hand on the bell. Hold it still. Hide the note so deep that no trial will ever find it."

The hall was silent.

"Folklore, of course," Dr Pell said. "No human wielder has ever been recorded doing it. Possibly no human could. The Muites, if you believe the stories, can do a great many things with omicrons that we can't, and they've had a great deal longer to practise." He straightened, and was a dusty old man again. "Now. Combustion. Why does a black-market pistol blow up in its owner's hand? Cadet Okoro. You're the one with the eyebrows missing. You'll know."

The hall laughed. Cadet Okoro, who had lost her eyebrows to a chemical lighter on a field exercise the previous term, explained that omicrons crowded around fast reactions like flies around fruit, and the more of them in the air, the more the reaction jittered; and that was why fire on Psi was wild, and why fire wielders were so rare, because fire never held still long enough for a bell to tune to it.

Romeo didn't hear most of it.

He was looking at the white mountains at the top of the map. At the faint pencil words, almost apologetic. *The Spine (Muite).*

---

He caught Dimitri in the corridor afterwards, by the elbow, and steered him into a stairwell that nobody used because it smelled of drains.

"When's your retest?"

Dimitri didn't pretend not to understand. He leaned his back against the wall and looked at the ceiling.

"Twenty-three days."

"And when they light the flame at station seven..."

"It'll lean." Dimitri's voice was very quiet. "It leans now. Every candle I walk past. I can feel all of them, Romeo. The lamps in the corridors. The stove in the mess. Ordo's pipe. I spend all day holding my breath so none of them do anything." He let out a long breath. "Station seven is going to light up like a festival."

Romeo nodded slowly.

"So we've got twenty-three days," he said. "Eight of them before we go and drink expensive wine in a house where somebody's father is doing something he shouldn't. And fifteen after." He thought about it. "That's actually loads of time. People have overthrown governments in less."

Dimitri laughed. It was a cracked, tired sound, but it was a laugh.

"Does Jules know?" Romeo said.

"Not about the date."

"Tell her."

"She's got a broken arm and a Zetan reception and a commander who might be the leak."

"Tell her anyway." Romeo held his eyes. "Trust me. The one thing worse for my sister than more problems is finding out about a problem late."

Dimitri looked at him for a moment. Then he nodded.

"The bell thing," he said. "What Pell said. A hand on the bell."

"Folklore," said Romeo.

"Yeah."

Neither of them said anything else. They didn't need to. Somewhere above them, very far off, a long white range of mountains was sitting at the top of a painted wall, and neither of them was going to say the word *Muite* in a stairwell, out loud, where the Choir could draw a line around their hearts.

---

Susan found him that evening in the motor pool, where he was sitting on the bonnet of a transport with Sergeant, feeding him bits of a meat pie that Ordo had definitely not given him.

"We leave in seven days," she said, without preamble. "My mother wants the squad at the house the day before the reception. To learn the rooms. Meet the staff." She paused. "She asked for you specifically."

Romeo nearly dropped the pie.

"Your mother? Asked for *me*? The woman who looked at Sergeant like he'd been sick on her carpet?"

"She asked for you," Susan said, "because I told her you're good with animals. And the estate has forty horses, twelve hounds, a peacock with a grudge, and a stable master who's eighty years old and refuses to retire. And on the night of the reception, every one of those animals will be within a mile of the house."

She looked at him steadily.

"I want to know where my father goes at night," she said. "He rides out. He has for years. Nobody asks. I never asked. I thought it was just something fathers did." Her voice was perfectly even. "Horses remember where they've been, don't they?"

"Better than people," said Romeo. "They don't lie about it, either."

"Then that's why I asked for you."

She turned to go. He watched her walk away across the oily concrete, straight-backed, neat-haired, her shadow long under the lamps.

"Susan."

She stopped. Didn't turn round.

"Is that the only reason?"

The motor pool was quiet. Somewhere a pipe ticked as it cooled. On the bonnet beside him, Sergeant stopped eating and looked up, very interested.

Susan stood with her back to him for what felt like a long time.

"The peacock," she said finally, "is called Lord Fennimore. He's bitten three ambassadors. He's going to hate you." And then, still without turning: "I'm told that's a quality you find attractive."

She walked on.

Romeo sat on the bonnet of the transport with a meat pie going cold in his hand and an extremely smug crow at his elbow, and grinned at the darkness like an idiot, and didn't care in the least who saw.

---

That night, he lay on his bunk with the lights off and thought about the map.

He thought about the little cross scratched beside the Kesh Ridge. About the blank plaster at the corners of the world, where nobody had bothered to finish painting. About a long white line of mountains where, according to a Zetan medic and a very old man in large spectacles, there lived people who could lay a hand on a bell and hold it still.

He thought about the hill-cat in the gully, and how far it was from Haven to the Reach. Not far. A day by transport. Two on foot.

And he thought about forty horses, twelve hounds, and a peacock called Lord Fennimore.

"Seven days," he said to Sergeant, who was asleep on the chair.

Sergeant did not wake up. But in the dark, very faintly, Romeo felt him dream: a long, slow, gliding dream, high up over a green and gold country, under a sky with no lines in it at all.

He borrowed it for a while. Just a little. Just to see.

Then he gave it back, and slept.
