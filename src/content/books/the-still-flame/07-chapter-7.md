"Five people," Juliette said.

She was standing at the head of the squad room table with her hands flat on it, and nobody was sitting down. It was the morning after the chapel. None of them had slept much. Sergeant sat on top of the cupboard in the corner with his feathers fluffed up, looking at each of them in turn, as if he were also counting.

"Five people knew we'd be on that roof last night," Juliette said. "Varga. And the four of us. The stakeout isn't in the log. It isn't in the duty roster. It isn't anywhere the Choir would carry it, because Varga made sure of that." She looked around the table. "So either one of us told someone, or Varga did, or Corran Vell is a much better guesser than I am."

"Say it," Susan said.

Juliette looked at her.

"Say it," Susan said again. Her voice was very calm. "You think it's me. My brother runs the basin. My family's water is in every one of Vell's cans. If anyone at this table has a reason to warn the Ashen, it's the de Ricci girl. Say it, Captain, so we can all stop pretending you're not thinking it."

The room went very still.

Dimitri watched Juliette's face. Nothing moved in it. He'd seen her look like that in Varga's office, and on Tannery Street with the water coming down. It was the face she used when she was deciding something.

"I think it's five people," Juliette said evenly. "I think if I start with you because of your name, I'm as bad as the people who wrote the charter. And I think the fastest way for the Ashen to win is for the four of us to start watching each other instead of them."

Susan held her eyes for a long moment. Then something in her shoulders let go, just slightly.

"It's not Susan," Romeo said.

Everyone turned to him. He was leaning against the wall with his arms folded, and he didn't look like he was joking.

"How do you know?" Juliette said.

"Because she signed her brother's name in Varga's office," Romeo said. "Nobody who's working for the Ashen does that. They'd have found a reason not to. She didn't look for one." He shrugged. "And Sergeant likes her. Sergeant doesn't like anyone."

On top of the cupboard, the crow made a low, grudging sound.

Susan looked at Romeo with an expression Dimitri couldn't read. Then she looked away, quickly, at the window.

"Then it's Varga," Dimitri said, and the words came out before he'd decided to say them. "Or someone near Varga. Or someone watching Varga."

"Or," Juliette said, "Vell has a way of knowing things we don't understand yet." She straightened. "Until we know which, nobody talks about this outside this room. Not in sent messages. Not in the mess. Not anywhere the Choir can draw a line around it."

They all nodded. Dimitri nodded with them.

He didn't know yet that by nightfall he'd be breaking that order.

---

He hadn't meant to go back.

That was what he told himself afterwards, and it was half true. He'd had a free afternoon, and he'd gone out through the main gate like any cadet on leave, and he'd walked. He hadn't decided where. His feet had just taken him through the market and over the canal bridge and into the narrow streets of the Fourth District, past the shuttered bakery and the dead tram stop, down the stone steps, to the blue door with the painted ship.

In daylight it was smaller. Shabbier. The paint on the ship was flaking. The door was shut, but there was a bell, and while he was still standing there trying to decide whether he was really doing this, the door opened.

It was the old woman in the blue shawl.

Up close she was smaller than he'd thought, and older, with a brown face folded into a thousand fine creases and eyes that were very sharp and very kind at the same time. She looked up at him. Her expression did something he didn't expect. It went still, and then it softened, and then it went still again.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, I wondered."

"I'm sorry," Dimitri said. "I just... I heard the singing, the other night. I wanted to..."

"You've got her eyes," the old woman said.

The street seemed to tip under him.

"You knew my mother?"

"Mira Gale sat on that bench there," the old woman said, pointing past him into the dim room, "every Restday for six years. Second row, by the pillar. She had a voice like a bell and she never once sang loud enough for anyone to hear it properly." She looked at him for a long moment. "You'd better come in, Dimitri. I'm Sister Orla. I'll put the kettle on."

---

The chapel was cold and smelled of candles and wet stone. Sister Orla made tea on a little iron stove in the corner, in two chipped mugs, and they sat on the bench by the pillar. His mother's bench. He put his hand on the wood and felt nothing in particular, and was surprised by how much that hurt.

"She came to us when she was carrying you," Sister Orla said. "Very young. Very frightened. She didn't tell us much and we didn't ask. That's the Wayfarer way. Everyone's on a voyage. Nobody has to explain where they set out from." She blew on her tea. "She found a room in the Fourth, near here. She worked in the laundry, the one you were lying on top of last night."

Dimitri nearly dropped his mug.

The old woman smiled faintly. "The roof's in full view of my kitchen window, boy. You and your captain aren't as quiet as you think. Neither are the two on the tram shelter."

"Then it was you," he said. "You told Corran."

"I told Corran there were soldiers on the roofs. He told me not to worry. He said he already knew." She set her mug down. "I don't know how. I don't ask Corran how he knows things. It's a good rule."

Dimitri sat very still.

"What was she afraid of?" he said. "My mother. You said she was frightened."

Sister Orla was quiet for a long time.

"Fire," she said finally. "She was afraid of fire. She wouldn't have candles in her room. She'd sit in this chapel with all of them lit and I'd see her shaking, and she'd stay anyway, all through the singing, because she said the song was worth it." The old woman looked at him with those sharp, kind eyes. "And she was afraid of someone. She never said who. Once, only once, she said that if a man ever came asking for her, someone with burns on his hands that never scarred, I was to say I'd never heard of her."

The tea in Dimitri's mug had gone very still. He realised his hands had gone hot, and that the tea was beginning, very faintly, to steam.

He put the mug down on the bench, carefully.

"Did he?" he said. "Come asking?"

"No," said Sister Orla. "Not then."

"What does that mean?"

She didn't answer. She looked past him, towards the back of the chapel, where a curtain hung over a doorway.

The curtain moved, and Corran Vell stepped through it.

---

He was not carrying water this time. His sleeves were rolled down. In the dim light of the candles he looked even younger than he had on Tannery Street, and more tired, and there was a small pink scar just behind his left ear, a little crescent, the kind of mark you'd only notice if you were standing close.

Dimitri was on his feet before he knew it. His hand went to his hip where his knife should have been, and wasn't, because cadets on leave didn't carry steel.

"Sit down," Corran said mildly. "If I wanted to fight you I'd have done it on the roof. There's a lot of water in this building."

"Sister," Dimitri said, not taking his eyes off Corran. "You need to step away."

"I'm ninety-three years old," said Sister Orla. "I'll step where I please." But she stood, and picked up the mugs, and went to the stove, and stood there with her back to them, the way a person does when they're giving others privacy without leaving the room.

Corran sat down on the bench opposite. After a long moment, Dimitri sat too.

"The Choir can see you," Corran said. "Can you feel it? It knows exactly where you are. It knows your heart's going like a drum. What it can't see is me." He touched the little crescent scar behind his ear. "Burned it out when I was fourteen. It hurt more than anything I've ever felt in my life. Then it stopped hurting, and it was the first quiet I'd ever had." He smiled slightly. "A hollow, they call it. Like that's an insult."

"How did you know we were on the roof?"

"Same way I knew there'd be one of you with the soldiers that first night." Corran leaned forward, elbows on his knees. "Somebody told me. Somebody very high up, in a house you'd never get into. And what they said was: there's a boy with the EDF, a cadet, a late bloomer. Fire in him like nobody's seen in a generation. Watch for him. Don't hurt him." He tilted his head. "Don't hurt him. That's the part I keep thinking about. Nobody in the Ashen says that about anyone. Not about me. Not about anyone."

Dimitri's heart was going so hard he could hear it.

"Who told you?" he said.

"I've never seen his face." Corran's voice was quiet now. "Nobody I know has. We get orders. Sometimes there's a mark with them, burned into whatever they're written on. A handprint." He looked at Dimitri's hands, resting on his knees. "About your size, actually."

The candles along the wall leaned, all together, a fraction of an inch towards the bench. Sister Orla, at the stove, did not turn around. But her shoulders went tight.

Dimitri made himself breathe. In, the way Juliette had taught him. Out. He found the warm place under his ribs, turning over in its sleep, and he did not ask it for anything. He just sat with it until the candles went straight again.

Corran watched all of this with an expression Dimitri couldn't read. Something like respect. Something like pity.

"I'm not going to ask you to come with me," Corran said. "That's not what this is. I just thought you deserved to know that somebody out there knows exactly what you are, and they knew before you did." He stood. "You can tell your captain I was here. I'll be gone before she gets halfway across the canal. Or you can not tell her. And maybe come back next Restday and sit on your mother's bench and hear the song properly."

He went to the curtain. Then he stopped, and looked back.

"Your captain," he said. "Vasquez. You should ask her about Varra."

Then he was gone.

---

It was dark when he got back to base.

He came in through the main gate like a cadet returning from leave, and the Choir confirmed him with its soft green certainty, and he walked across the parade ground in the cold with his hands in his pockets, and the whole way back he told himself that he would go straight to Juliette's quarters and tell her everything.

The bench. Sister Orla. His mother's fear of fire, and the man with burns that never scarred. Corran, hollow, with orders from someone he'd never seen. *Don't hurt him.*

And Varra. A word he had never heard before, said in a tone that meant it was a door he wasn't supposed to open.

She was waiting for him at the bottom of the barracks stairs.

She was leaning against the wall with her arms folded, and her face was unreadable, and he understood with a cold drop in his stomach that of course she was. She was his captain. She could see the whole squad on the Choir any time she chose. She had watched a little marker that was him walk across the city and down into the Fourth District and sit, for an hour and a half, in a building at the bottom of some stone steps.

"Fourth District," she said. "The chapel."

He could tell her now. All of it. He opened his mouth to do it.

And then he thought of Corran saying *ask her about Varra*, and of Romeo on the roof saying *she's had enough people leave*, and of how she had looked in the squad room that morning saying *nobody talks about this outside this room*. He thought about what her face would do if he said the word.

He thought about the fact that she had a secret, and that somebody in the Ashen knew it, and that he didn't yet know which of those things frightened him more.

"I went to hear the singing," he said. "My mother used to go there. The old woman who runs it knew her. I sat on her bench." He swallowed. "That's all."

Every word of it was true.

Juliette looked at him for a long time. In the cold light over the stairs, the thin white line on her chin showed very clearly. He could not tell what she was thinking. He had never been able to.

"That must have been hard," she said at last, quietly.

"It was."

She nodded once. Then she unfolded her arms and stepped aside to let him pass, and as he went by her she touched his elbow, very briefly, the way you might steady someone on a ship.

He climbed the stairs. He went into his room and closed the door and sat down on the bunk in the dark with his heart still pounding.

*I left something out,* she had said to him on the roof, barely a week ago, a lifetime ago. *There's a difference. You'll learn it.*

He had learned it.

He sat there for a long time in the dark and wished he hadn't.
