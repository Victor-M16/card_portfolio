"Alone," Romeo said, at the door of the east wing. "He was very clear about that. I'm to deliver you like a parcel and go away."

"Will you?"

"I'll go as far as the end of the corridor and sit on the floor." Romeo shrugged. "It's a very long corridor. Practically another country." He looked at Dimitri for a moment, and his voice lost its lightness. "He bowed to you, Dimitri. From the waist. I asked Jules what that means. She said she's only ever read about it. In histories."

"Histories of what?"

"She didn't say. She just went quiet." Romeo put a hand on his shoulder, briefly. "Go on. I'll be on the floor."

The guard captain with the white streak down her nose opened the door. She looked at Dimitri, and her second eyelids slid across her eyes, slow, and back. Then she stepped aside.

Ambassador Sath was alone except for Observer Teyl.

The room was lit by a single lamp on a low table. Not a candle. An oil lamp, old-fashioned, with a glass chimney. The Ambassador sat beside it in his plain grey robe, without his gold. Teyl stood by the window with its hands clasped behind its back. Outside, the vineyard climbed into the dark.

"Sit, Dimitri Gale," said Sath.

Dimitri sat. His hands were in his lap. They wanted to go into his pockets. He made them stay.

"You know who your father is," said the Ambassador. It was not a question.

"I know his name. Since last night."

"Idris." The old Zetan said it as if tasting it. "Idris Kane. Captain, once, of the Etan Defence Force. Then nothing, of anything. Then a story, told around fires in the Sere, about a human who walked north into the snow with his seed burned out of his head and came back nine years later with hands that didn't scar." The red eyes rested on him. "He passed through my country, both times. Going and coming. The first time, he asked us for water and a road. We gave him both, because he asked politely, and because we do not refuse water to the dying, even human ones. I was governor of the oasis at Thel, then. I gave him the water myself."

Dimitri found that he was gripping his own knees.

"And the second time?"

"The second time, he did not need water. He did not need anything." Sath was quiet for a moment. "He sat at my well for one night. He lit a small fire to warm his hands, the way any traveller would. And I looked at that fire, and I understood that the man who had walked north was gone, and something else had walked back in his clothes."

The lamp on the low table burned between them. Dimitri looked at it, and felt it look back.

"Light a flame for me," said Sath. "Small. As you did in the ballroom."

Dimitri hesitated. He looked at Teyl, by the window. The Observer's head was tilted, listening.

"There is no Choir in this room," Sath said gently. "My guards carry a small thing that the Assembly agreed we might carry, for our own privacy, under the terms of the truce. You are in a very small dead zone, Dimitri Gale. Nobody is drawing your heart on any screen. Only we are listening. And we have already heard it."

So he opened his hand.

The flame came up the way it always did now. Easy. Too easy. Gold, and the size of a coin, floating above his palm.

Sath leaned forward and looked at it.

Then he picked up the oil lamp from the table, and held it beside Dimitri's hand. Its flame danced. It flickered, and leaned, and jittered in its glass, shivering the way all fire on Psi shivered: restless, hungry, never still for a moment. Dimitri had seen fire do that his whole life. He had never thought about it.

Beside it, the flame above his palm burned perfectly, utterly still.

Not a flicker. Not a tremor. A small gold teardrop, as motionless as if it had been painted on the air.

"You see," said Sath softly.

Dimitri stared at it.

"Fire on Psi is wild," the Ambassador said. "Your Dr Pell will have told you why. The omicrons crowd it. Fire never holds still long enough for a bell to tune to it. That is why your fire wielders are so few, and so weak, and so mad. But there is an old story among my people. Very old. Older than the war. Older than the desert." He set the lamp back down. "It says that the Keepers in the mountains know a way to teach fire to be still. And that a fire which has been taught by them burns like that. Like a painted thing. Like a candle in a room with no air."

"The Keepers," Dimitri said. "The Muites."

"Your word. Yes."

"And my father learned it. In the Spine."

"Your father's fire burned like that at my well." Sath's eyes did not leave the little flame. "So does yours. Which means one of two things. Either you have been to the Spine, which I do not think. Or someone who had been there laid their hand on your fire when it was very new, and taught it stillness, and then laid their hand on your bell, and held it quiet, so that it would wait."

*A hand on the bell.* Dr Pell's dry voice, in the warm lecture hall. *Hide the note so deep that no trial will ever find it.*

"When I was a baby," Dimitri said. His mouth had gone dry. "Someone did that. When I was a baby."

"Or before you were born," said Sath. "The stories are not clear. Stories never are."

Dimitri closed his hand. The flame went out. He sat in the lamplight with his fist clenched in his lap and felt the fire under his ribs, awake, pacing, and for the first time he understood that it had always been like that. Patient. Waiting. Not asleep. *Held.*

"That's why you bowed," he said.

"Partly." Sath was quiet for a long moment. "There is more to the story. My people tell it to children, the way yours tell them about the empty shore. Long ago, when the humans had been on the coast only a few lifetimes, and had grown strong, and had begun to look west, there was a war. The first war. We call it the Long Burning. It went on for generations. The desert was green before it. After it, it was the desert." His voice did not change, but something in the room did. "And it ended because a human came down out of the mountains with a fire that did not flicker. Nobody knows who he was. The stories do not agree on what he did. Some say he burned both armies until they stopped. Some say he stood between them and would not move. Some say both."

The lamp hissed softly.

"And when it was over," said Sath, "he went back up into the mountains. And the Keepers sent word down to both peoples, the only time in all our histories that they ever spoke to us together. They said: *we have given you a shore and a desert, and you have burned them both. We will not give again. But when the shore forgets what it was given, we will send another.*"

Dimitri did not move.

"I am an old man," Ambassador Sath said. "I have spent my life trying to make peace with a people who teach their children that the land was empty. Who paint *Psi is wide* on their walls. Who have, I think, entirely forgotten." The red eyes held his. "And last night, in a dark room, with a knife at my feet, a boy opened his hand and made a fire that did not flicker."

He spread his own hands, slowly.

"So I bowed," he said. "Because I did not know whether I was bowing to the end of a war, or the beginning of one. And in my experience, Dimitri Gale, it is wise to be polite to both."

---

Romeo was sitting on the floor at the far end of the corridor with his back against the wall and Sergeant asleep in his lap.

He took one look at Dimitri's face and got up without a word and walked beside him, all the way through the dark house, down the stairs, through the kitchens and out into the cold kitchen garden, where Dimitri stopped by the wall and put both hands flat against the old brick and stood there breathing.

Romeo waited.

"I'm a story," Dimitri said eventually. "Romeo. I'm a children's story. In the desert."

"Is it a good one?"

Dimitri laughed. It came out cracked and wrong. "I don't know. Nobody knows. That's the whole point of it."

Romeo was quiet. Then he said, "Do you want me to tell Jules, or do you want to?"

"I'll tell her."

"Good." A pause. "For the record, I always thought you were the big dog. The one who knocks things over with his tail. I've been very wrong about animals this week."

"What am I now?"

Romeo looked at him for a long time in the dark.

"I'll let you know," he said.

---

Varga sent for him at dawn.

Not to an office. To a car. A long black EDF transport, idling at the foot of the de Ricci steps with its windows dark, while the rest of the squad loaded into the second car behind it. Juliette watched him go with her bad arm in its sling and her face completely blank, which was how he knew she was afraid.

He climbed in. The door closed. The car pulled away down the long drive between the terraces, past the fountain, where Lord Fennimore sat on the rim with his tail spread, screaming at the morning.

Varga sat across from him with her hands folded in her lap. For the first few miles she didn't speak. She looked out of the window at the passing vines.

Then she said: "Ambassador Sath told you the story."

"Yes, ma'am."

"The still flame."

"Yes."

She nodded slowly.

"I've heard it," she said. "Twenty years ago. From your father. He'd heard it at the oasis at Thel, on his way north, and he wouldn't stop talking about it." She was still looking at the window. "I thought it was nonsense. Desert stories. I told him so." A long pause. "I've had twenty years to change my mind."

The car turned out of the estate gates onto the coast road. The sea was grey and flat under a grey sky.

"You knew," Dimitri said. "From the start. When they brought me in."

"I knew when I saw your file." Varga's voice was very level. "Mother, Mira Gale. Deceased, house fire, Fourth District. Father, unknown. Heavy load, no affinity, sleeper." She turned, finally, and looked at him. "I knew Mira Gale. I was on that estate the year she sang in the kitchens. I knew exactly whose son you were before I'd finished the first page."

"And you put me on Juliette's squad."

"I put you on the best squad I had, under the best captain I had, a girl who would die before she let anyone take one of her people." Varga's mouth moved, very slightly. "I was right about that, at least."

"Why?"

Varga looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked down at her own hands, folded in her lap. Narrow and pale and lined.

"I was twenty-three," she said. "His lieutenant. We were on the southern garrison, the year of the water riots. There was a grain store at Halden Ford, in the basin, and a mob set it alight, with a squad of ours inside, holding the doors. Holding them shut, so the mob wouldn't get at the grain. I was one of them." Her voice did not change at all. "The roof came down. I was under a beam. I remember the smoke, and the heat, and thinking that this was a very stupid way to die. For grain."

The car hummed along the coast road. Dimitri didn't breathe.

"And then the fire opened," Varga said. "Like a curtain. It just... parted. And your father walked through it. Not around it. Through it. The flames went back from him like water from the bow of a boat. He lifted the beam off me with one hand and he carried me out on his shoulder and set me down in the road and went back in for the others. Four times." She looked out of the window again. "He got all of them. Every one. And then he sat down in the road and laughed, and his coat was on fire, and he didn't even notice."

"He saved your life."

"He saved my life. He saved four other lives. And six months later he burned his seed out on a hilltop and walked into the Reach and became the most wanted man on Eta, and I signed the warrant myself." Her hands, in her lap, had gone very still. "I have hunted him for nineteen years, cadet. I've never once come close. I'm not sure I've ever tried as hard as I should." A pause. "And when your file came across my desk, I thought: here is a debt I can actually pay."

Dimitri looked at her. At the heron in the shallows, who had stood still for nineteen years waiting for a fish she did not want to catch.

"Is that why you let me lie?" he said. "In the review. About Tannery Street."

"I let you lie because a lie bought you time." Varga turned back to him. "And time is the one thing I can't buy you anymore."

She touched the panel beside her. A screen woke in the air between them. A news broadcast, the sound off. A small, round, smiling man in a red sash, standing at a lectern in the Assembly hall, speaking with great earnestness to a forest of cameras.

Councillor Bastien Roe.

Under his face, a line of text crawled across the bottom of the screen.

*COUNCILLOR ROE: EDF HARBOURING SON OF ASHEN LEADER. DEMANDS INQUIRY.*

Dimitri felt the floor of the car drop away under him.

"He knows," he whispered.

"He knows," said Varga. "He knew before we did, I suspect. Someone in my own command has been feeding him for months." Her voice was flat and cold. "Last night we found the knife in his hand. This morning, before we could arrest him, he stood up in the Assembly and told the whole of Haven that the EDF is sheltering Idris Kane's son. That the boy was at the reception. That the boy made fire in the dark, in front of the Zetans, an hour before the lights went out." She watched the small smiling man mouth his words. "He's very good. By tonight half the city will believe the knife was yours."

"It wasn't."

"I know it wasn't. Sath knows it wasn't. The Choir can't prove it either way, because the hollow who held it doesn't exist." Varga turned the screen off. "So we won't argue. We'll show them."

"Show them what?"

"You." Her grey eyes held his. "Your affinity retest was due in fourteen days. I've moved it. It's tomorrow morning, at headquarters, in the old trial hall. The Assembly's committee will be in the gallery. Roe will be in the gallery. The Zetans have asked to attend, and I have said yes." She paused. "Every station will be lit. Including the seventh."

The car hummed. Outside, the grey sea rolled.

"You want me to show them," Dimitri said slowly. "That I'm a fire wielder."

"They already know you are. Roe's made sure of that." Varga leaned forward. "I want you to show them what you are *not*. Not an assassin. Not your father's weapon. Not a monster in a ballroom. A cadet of the EDF, who sat a trial in the open, under every eye in Haven, and did exactly what he was asked, and nothing more." Her voice dropped. "Can you do that?"

Dimitri looked down at his hands.

He thought about the four hundred and twelve threads under his ribs, yanked up a hill in the dark. About a voice calling him from the next room. About a flame that did not flicker, and a story told to Zetan children, and a hand he had never seen, laid on a bell before he could remember.

"I don't know," he said honestly.

Varga looked at him for a long time.

"That," she said, "is the first true thing anyone in your family has said to me in twenty years." She sat back. "Get some sleep, cadet. You'll need it."

Juliette was waiting at the gate when the car pulled into headquarters.

She was standing in the cold with her sling and her grey coat, alone, and when he climbed out she didn't say anything. She just looked at his face, the way she looked at everything, quickly, totally. Then she held out her good hand.

He took it.

"Tomorrow," he said.

"I heard." Her fingers tightened. "Station seven."

"In front of everyone."

"In front of everyone." She was quiet for a moment. "I'll be there. In the hall. Close. If it goes wrong, I'll take the air."

He looked at her. At the hawk, with one broken wing, standing at the gate in the cold.

"Juliette," he said. "Last night. On the hill. When he took the candles. Did you feel how much there was?"

She didn't answer for a moment. And in that moment, he saw something cross her face that she would not have let anyone else see.

Doubt.

"I'll take the air," she said again, more quietly.

But she didn't let go of his hand.
