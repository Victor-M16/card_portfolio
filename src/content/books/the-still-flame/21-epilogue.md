Four days after the trial hall, somebody painted a flame on the door of the Long Voyage.

Romeo saw it on his way back from the canal market, with a bag of oranges under one arm and Sergeant on his shoulder eating the peel. He had come the long way round, through the Fourth District, because he had wanted to see. Everybody on base had been talking about what was happening in the Fourth. He had wanted to see it with his own eyes.

It was a simple painting. Gold, on the blue of the door, just under the old picture of the ship rising. A single flame, shaped like a teardrop. No flicker lines around it. No movement. Just still.

And it was not the only one.

They were everywhere. On the walls by the dead tram stop. On the shutters of the bakery. On the side of the laundry, the one where his mother had worked, Romeo thought, and then corrected himself: Dimitri's mother. Little gold teardrops, painted by people who had watched a boy in a ring of stones and seen, in the middle of it, something they wanted to believe in.

He walked further, past the canal bridge, into the Second District, where the chartered houses had their offices and the streets were clean.

There were paintings there too. Different ones. Red paint, sprayed in a hurry across white walls in the night. The same word, over and over, in big careless letters, sometimes with a crude flame drawn beside it and a line struck through.

*MONSTER.*

And on the overpass by the old dye works, where it had always been, the oldest graffiti in the city: *Psi is wide.* Someone had added to it now. Underneath, in fresh black letters, neat, almost elegant.

*And now it is lit.*

Romeo stood looking at that for a long time. Sergeant finished the orange peel and wiped his beak on Romeo's collar.

"I know," Romeo said. "I don't like it either."

---

The news, in the four days since, had gone like this.

On the first day, the city had shouted. Every screen in Haven played the trial hall, again and again, and every person in Haven had an opinion, and every opinion was very loud. The Committee on Western Affairs called for the boy to be locked up. The Wayfarer chapels called for the boy to be left alone. The generals said nothing at all, which Romeo found more frightening than anything the Committee said.

On the second day, Varga arrested Councillor Bastien Roe.

She did it in the Assembly hall, in the middle of a session, with Matteo de Ricci's sworn testimony in one hand and a sealed letter from Ambassador Sath in the other. She walked down the centre aisle in plain grey, alone, and stopped in front of Roe's seat, and said something very quietly that nobody could hear. And Roe, for the first time since anyone could remember, stopped smiling.

He was in a cell under headquarters now. He had answered every question about the knife, the agency, the footman, the money. He had answered politely and in great detail.

He had not answered one.

*Who in the EDF told you about the boy?*

To that, he only smiled again.

On the third day, Ambassador Sath had come back to the table.

Not to sign the five-year treaty. Not the pipeline. Those were gone, at least for now, buried under the shouting. But he had signed something smaller. A truce of one year. A border held. An exchange of observers continued. And a single new clause, added in the Ambassador's own hand, that the Assembly had argued about for six hours and then accepted, because nobody could think of a reason to refuse that they were willing to say out loud.

*The Sere requests that Observer Teyl be attached, at the Observer's own discretion, to the EDF squad of Captain Juliette Vasquez.*

Jules had read that clause four times. Then she had gone up onto the training roof and stood there for an hour in the cold without moving.

And on the fourth day, which was today, Susan had gone to visit her brother.

---

She was sitting on the low wall outside the headquarters cells when Romeo came back with the oranges. Straight-backed. Hands folded in her lap. Ring on her finger. She did not look like someone who had been crying. Romeo had grown up on army bases; he knew exactly what people looked like when they had been crying and had then washed their faces in cold water and done their hair again.

He sat down beside her on the wall and held out an orange.

She looked at it.

"Sergeant's been at the peel," he said. "Only the outside. The inside's had a very sheltered life."

She took it. She didn't eat it. She held it in both hands, the way you'd hold something warm.

"He asked me to forgive him," she said.

"Matteo?"

"He sat on the bench in that room with his hands between his knees and asked me to forgive him. Like when we were children and he'd broken something and wanted me to say it was me." She turned the orange slowly in her fingers. "I said I didn't know how yet. He said that was fair. Then he asked if Vesper was all right." A small, tired sound, almost a laugh. "He's always loved that horse."

"And your father?"

"Resigned the basin. Yesterday. Handed the books to the Assembly himself. All of them. Twenty years of water going out the back door." She was quiet for a moment. "He came to see me after. At the gate. He didn't come in. He just stood there, and said he was sorry he'd made me into something he'd have to pay for. And then he gave me this."

She opened her hand. On her palm, beside the orange, lay a small curl of something pale and dry and very fragile. A single long spiral of apple peel, unbroken, end to end.

"He peeled it on the way here," Susan said. "In the car. All in one piece." Her voice cracked very slightly. "He said I could make a wish."

Romeo looked at the curl of peel on her palm for a long time.

"Did you?"

"Not yet." She closed her hand gently around it. "I'm saving it."

They sat there on the wall in the thin winter sun, not talking. After a while she peeled the orange, carefully, and gave him half, and they ate it, and Sergeant got the pips.

Then Susan said, without looking at him: "Romeo."

"Mm."

"In the training hall. When you kept going right. You said sometimes you mean it."

He went very still.

"Do you mean it now?" said Susan de Ricci, looking straight ahead at the parade ground with her chin up and her heart, he could hear it, going like a bird in a box.

Romeo opened his mouth to make a joke.

He had one ready. He always had one ready. It was something about the peacock.

He closed his mouth again.

"Yes," he said.

She turned her head and looked at him. For a long moment. The cat was gone completely. There was just a girl on a wall with orange juice on her fingers and a curl of apple peel in her pocket, deciding something.

Then she leaned over and kissed him. Quickly. Lightly. Once. Like someone touching a hot stove to see if it was really hot.

It was.

She sat back. Her face was perfectly composed, except for her eyes.

"Good," said Susan. "Because I've been meaning it since the peacock."

On Romeo's shoulder, Sergeant made a long, low, deeply disgusted sound, turned his back on both of them, and flew away to sit on the roof of the cells, where he stayed for the rest of the afternoon, refusing to look.

---

He found Dimitri on the training roof at sunset.

Jules was there too. She was sitting on the parapet with her back against the chimney stack and her sling, a new one, clean, resting in her lap. Dimitri was sitting beside her. Not quite touching. Close enough that it didn't matter. They weren't talking. They were both looking west, towards the line of the hills, where the sun was going down red and gold over the Reach.

Romeo stood at the top of the stairs for a moment and didn't say anything.

He had spent his whole life watching his sister. He knew all her faces. Hawk. Captain. The blank one, which meant she was afraid. The rare small almost-smile, which meant someone had surprised her into being happy.

This was a new one. She was sitting on a roof in the cold beside a boy who had filled a stone hall with fire that she could not stop, and she was looking at the sunset with an expression Romeo had never seen on her before. It was not peace, exactly. It was more like someone standing at the edge of very deep water, who knows she is going to jump, and has stopped pretending otherwise.

He went over and sat down on Dimitri's other side, and held out the last orange.

"Peace offering," he said. "I've been to the Fourth. You're on the walls."

"I know," said Dimitri. "Both kinds."

"Both kinds."

Dimitri took the orange. He didn't peel it. He held it in his hands, and Romeo watched the skin of it go slowly, very gently warm, and smelled the oil of it rising in the cold air.

"Varga says I stay," Dimitri said. "For now. On the squad. Under her command. The Assembly wanted me in a cell. The Zetans wanted me left alone. She told them both no and they both backed down." He turned the orange. "I don't know how she did it."

"She's a heron," said Romeo. "She's been standing in that river for nineteen years. She knows where every fish in it lives."

Jules made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

"And Teyl?" Romeo said.

"Teyl starts on the squad tomorrow," said Jules. "Its own discretion, it says. It wants to observe." She was quiet a moment. "I asked it what it was observing for. It said: *for both of us.*"

"Ominous."

"Very."

They sat for a while in the cold, the three of them, and watched the sun go down.

"Are you going?" Romeo said finally. "North. Where the snow starts."

He felt Jules go still on Dimitri's other side.

Dimitri didn't answer for a long time. He looked west, past the city, past the hills, to the place where the land went up and up into the Reach, and beyond it, somewhere very far off, past the Ridge and the desert and the edge of the map, a long white line of mountains that the painter had never bothered to name.

"Not yet," he said. "Not like he wants. Not alone, on his say-so, because he called." He turned the warm orange in his hands. "But I'm not going to pretend I don't want to know. I want to know who held my bell and why. I want to know what the Keepers want. I want to know who set the fire that killed my mother." He paused. "I want to look at him. Just once. And see what he looks like when I say no."

"And then?" said Jules quietly.

Dimitri turned his head and looked at her.

"And then I want to come home," he said. "Wherever that is."

Jules held his eyes. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. She just reached out with her good hand and took the orange out of his fingers, and peeled it, one-handed, badly, with her teeth, and gave him half.

Romeo looked away at the sunset, so they could have that, and found that he was grinning like an idiot again, and didn't care who saw.

---

Later, when they had gone down to the mess, Romeo stayed on the roof alone.

It was full dark now. The stars were out over the Fourth District, more of them than over the rest of the city, because the streetlights there ran on rationed power. Somewhere down in those streets, in a little chapel at the bottom of some stone steps, a gold flame was painted on a blue door. Somewhere in the Second District, a red word was drying on a white wall. And somewhere far to the west, past the hills, a man who smelled of old smoke and hot stone was walking north through the dark, waiting.

Sergeant came back from the roof of the cells at last and landed on the parapet beside him, and looked at him with his flat black eyes, and clicked.

"I know," said Romeo. "I kissed a de Ricci. It's done now. You'll have to get used to it."

Sergeant did not dignify that with a response.

Romeo looked west.

He thought about the map on the lecture hall wall. The long green ribbon of the coast. The brown and dusty green of the Reach, with its badly painted beasts. The little scratched cross by the Kesh Ridge. The pale gold of the Sere. The dark forest across the whole north, and the dark sea that seemed to go on under it. The long white mountains at the top, unnamed. And all around the edges, blank plaster, where nobody had ever bothered to finish.

He thought about a hill-cat in a gully. About how it had felt to be that, for ten seconds, nine years old. Hungry and furious and completely free.

About how he'd come back, and decided never to want anything that much again.

He had been wrong about a lot of things this month. He had been wrong about animals. He had been wrong about his sister, and about Dimitri, and about a girl who sat very straight and read manuals and turned out to be the bravest person he had ever met. He thought he might have been wrong about that, too.

"Sergeant," he said. "I've worked it out. What Dimitri is."

The crow looked at him.

"He's not an animal," said Romeo. "I kept trying. Dog. Wolf. Nothing fit. Because he's not the creature." He looked out at the dark, at the hills, at the west. "He's the fire on the hill. The one every animal in the Reach is afraid of. The one they all run from."

Sergeant tilted his head.

"And the one they all come back to," said Romeo softly, "when it's cold."

He sat on the roof of the training hall for a long time after that, with his crow beside him and the stars coming out over the city, looking west towards a land that was wide, and wild, and waiting.

Then he went down to the mess, where his sister and his friend and a girl with apple peel in her pocket were saving him a seat.

*End of Book One.*
