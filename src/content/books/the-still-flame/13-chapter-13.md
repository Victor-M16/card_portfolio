Lord Fennimore bit Romeo within forty seconds of the car door opening.

Susan had been counting. She had known it would happen; she had simply wanted to know how long. The peacock came round the side of the fountain in a blaze of blue and green, its tail dragging on the gravel like a train, took one look at the young man climbing out of the back seat with a crow on his shoulder, and went for his ankle like an arrow.

"*Ow!* Why does it... ow! Susan! Your bird!"

"He's not my bird," Susan said, stepping out of the other side. "He belongs to the house. Like the vines, and the debt, and the portraits."

Romeo was hopping on one foot. Sergeant had risen off his shoulder in outrage and was circling overhead, shouting. Lord Fennimore stood his ground on the gravel with his crest raised and his eyes like two small black buttons of pure malice, and screamed back.

Then Romeo stopped hopping. He went very still, and looked down at the peacock, and something happened in his face.

Lord Fennimore lowered his crest. He tilted his head. He took one step forward, and pecked, very delicately, at the toe of Romeo's boot, as if checking whether it was edible. Then he turned around, and walked away across the gravel with enormous dignity, and settled on the rim of the fountain with his back to them all.

"What did you do?" Susan said.

"Told him he was beautiful." Romeo rubbed his ankle. "He's very insecure. It's all for show, the screaming. Underneath he just wants someone to notice the tail." He looked up at her with that grin. "Remind you of anyone?"

Susan turned and walked up the steps of her house without answering, because if she had answered, she would have laughed, and she had decided before they left the base that she was not going to laugh once in the next two days.

She lasted until the front hall.

---

It was strange to see them here.

She had imagined it on the drive down, of course. The squad in her house. But imagining it was nothing like watching Dimitri stop dead in the middle of the hall with his head tipped back, looking up at the living ceiling of the grand staircase, where a hundred years of trained ivy hung in green curtains from the dome and the evening light came through it gold.

"It's growing," he said. "Inside."

"It's been growing inside since before the Assembly existed," Susan said. "Don't touch it. It doesn't know you."

He put his hands in his pockets, quickly, like a child in a shop full of glass.

Juliette didn't stop. Juliette walked into the de Ricci house in a borrowed grey dress with her arm in a black silk sling that Susan had found for her, and looked at the paintings and the marble and the staircase and the ivy with a soldier's quick sweeping eyes, and Susan understood that she was not looking at any of it. She was counting doors. Windows. Stairs. Places a person could stand and not be seen.

It was oddly comforting.

"Susan, darling."

Her mother came down the staircase like a ship coming into harbour, in pale gold silk, with both hands held out. She kissed Susan on each cheek. Then she turned to the squad, and Susan watched her do it: the inventory. Quick and total, like a merchant pricing a cargo.

Juliette. *Officer. Wounded. Dangerous. Pretty, in a severe way. Will need a better dress.*

Dimitri. *Tall. Gentle. Out of his depth. Good bones. Doesn't know what to do with his hands.*

Romeo. *Charming. Insolent. Bird on shoulder. Bitten by the peacock and survived it. Hm.*

"Welcome," said Celestine de Ricci, "to our home. Any friend of Susan's." She smiled, and it almost reached her eyes. "Dinner is at eight. The Zetan advance party arrived this afternoon and will be dining with us. Do try not to start a war."

---

The Zetans had come with Observer Teyl.

Susan had not expected that, and from the small stillness that came over Juliette when the Zetan party entered the dining room, neither had she. There were five of them. Four were soldiers, in pale sand-coloured robes over hard armour, with the plates along their jaws painted in thin lines of white. The fifth was Teyl, in a plain grey coat, who bowed to her mother, and to her father, and then turned and looked straight down the long table at Juliette, and bowed to her too.

Juliette rose. She put her good hand open against her chest, and bowed from the neck, eyes lowered. *I show you I carry nothing.* And then she said something in Zetan.

Susan didn't understand it. But she saw the four soldiers look at each other, and she saw the oldest of them, a woman with deep lines round her eyes and a white streak painted down her nose, raise her eyebrow ridges in a way that Susan was fairly sure meant surprise. The woman answered. Juliette answered again. And then, astonishingly, the Zetan soldier laughed, a short dry sound like a stick snapping, and touched her own chest, and sat.

"What did you say?" Susan murmured when Juliette sat down beside her.

"I said welcome to the house, the water here is clean and we will not ask you to pay for it tonight." Juliette unfolded her napkin one-handed. "It's a formal greeting. Very old. Nobody uses the second half anymore."

"And she laughed."

"She said my accent was terrible but my manners were better than any human she'd met." A faint, almost invisible smile. "And then she said her grandmother fought at the Ridge."

Across the table, Teyl was watching them both, its second eyelids sliding slowly across and back.

Dinner was long. Her father was charming, as he always was, and tired, as he always was now. Matteo arrived halfway through the second course, kissed his mother, bowed to the Zetans with exaggerated care, and spent the rest of the meal talking to Romeo about horses with the enthusiasm of a man who had found the only other person at the table worth talking to. Romeo was very good at it. He laughed at Matteo's jokes. He asked the right questions. And twice, when Matteo turned away to refill his glass, Susan saw Romeo's eyes go to her brother's hands, and stay there, and go cold.

Nonna Livia sat at the head of the table in green and said almost nothing at all.

Only at the very end, when the plates had been cleared and the Zetans had risen to go, did she reach out and catch Susan's wrist as she passed, and draw her down, and speak very softly into her ear.

"Be careful what you feel at my table tomorrow, child."

"Nonna?"

"The Zetans hear." The old woman's eyes were clear and sharp in their nest of wrinkles. "Not words. Hearts. When I was a girl there was a Zetan trader who used to come to this house, and she could stand at the end of the hall and tell you who in the room was lying, by the sound of their blood." She let go of Susan's wrist. "We have seven ways of touching the world, we humans. Air, water, earth, the green, the beasts, the pull of things, and fire. Seven, and we think we are so clever. The others have more. They always have. Remember that, when you are in a room with them, and you think you are hiding something."

Susan straightened slowly. Across the room, at the door, Observer Teyl had paused to let its soldiers go ahead, and was standing quite still, with its head very slightly tilted. As if listening.

She thought of the review room, the morning after Tannery Street. *Your heart is very loud, cadet.*

She made her own heart quiet. A de Ricci heart does not race at table.

But she had a terrible feeling it had never been quiet enough.

---

The stables were warm and smelled of hay and leather and the deep, sweet, dusty smell of horses. It was past midnight. Old Bastiano had gone to bed hours ago, muttering. The lamps were turned low.

Romeo stood in the middle of the aisle with his eyes closed and his hands held out at his sides, palms up, and forty horses stood in their stalls around him and did not make a sound.

Susan had never seen anything like it.

She had grown up with these horses. She had ridden them since she was three. And she had never once, in all those years, heard the stable this quiet. Every head was turned. Every ear was pricked forward. Forty pairs of dark, liquid eyes in the lamplight, all looking at one barracks boy with a crow asleep on a beam above him.

"They like you," she said quietly.

"Horses like everyone who listens." He didn't open his eyes. "Nobody listens to horses. They're very lonely, horses. Big and strong and everyone just wants to sit on them." His mouth twitched. "Which one's your father's?"

"Vesper. The grey. End stall."

He walked down the aisle. The mare was old, dappled silver going white at the muzzle, and she put her head over the stall door before he reached her and pushed her nose into his chest like a dog.

Romeo put his hand flat on her neck, and closed his eyes again, and was quiet for a long time.

Susan waited. She found that she was holding her breath, and made herself stop, and then found that she was doing it again.

"She's tired," Romeo said eventually. His voice had gone soft and far away. "Old. Her knees hurt in the cold. She loves him. Your father. He brings her apples with the skins peeled because her teeth are bad." A pause. "He's very gentle with her."

Susan said nothing. Her throat had gone tight.

"Night," Romeo murmured. "Lots of nights. Not every week. Every few weeks. He comes down himself, no groom, saddles her himself. They go out the back gate. Up." His brow creased. "Up through the vines. All the way up. Past the last terrace. It's steep. She doesn't like the stones at the top; they're loose."

"The old vineyard," Susan said. "At the top of the estate. Nobody's worked it in a hundred years. There's a ruin up there, an old Sown shrine. Nonna used to take me when I was little."

"There's someone waiting." Romeo's voice changed. "At the top. In the ruin. Not every time. Most times."

The mare shifted uneasily under his hand.

"She doesn't like him," Romeo said slowly. "She's afraid of him. Not like you're afraid of a stranger. Like you're afraid of a storm. He smells of..." His nose wrinkled, as if he were smelling it himself. "Smoke. Old smoke, and hot stone. And she's not afraid of fire. She's stood by bonfires at harvest all her life. But she's afraid of him."

The stable was very quiet.

"What does he look like?" Susan whispered.

"Horses don't see faces the way we do. A shape. Tall. Coat. He stands with your father, by the old wall, and they talk. Sometimes your father gives him something. Sometimes he gives your father something." Romeo was frowning now, deeply. "And once, a long time ago, years, the man put his hand on the wall. Flat. Like this." He spread his free hand against the stall door. "And when he took it away, the stone was black."

Romeo opened his eyes.

He took his hand off the mare's neck, slowly, and she blew softly through her nose and pushed her head against his shoulder, and he stroked her face, and looked at Susan in the lamplight.

Neither of them said anything for a long moment.

"I'm sorry," Romeo said.

And it was the way he said it, plainly, without a single joke anywhere near it, that undid her. She had held together through the ring, through the squad room, through Varga's office and the drive down and her mother's inventory and dinner with the Zetans and Matteo's laugh and her father's tired, charming smile. She had held together because she was a de Ricci and that was what de Riccis did.

She put her hand over her mouth, and turned away, and stood with her back to him in the warm dark stable among the horses who had known her all her life, and shook.

She didn't cry. She would not cry. But she shook.

She heard him move behind her. Then nothing. He didn't touch her. He didn't say anything. He just stood there, close enough that she knew he was there, and waited, the way he had waited for the peacock, and the horses, and the rats in the rubble.

After a while, she stopped shaking.

"He brings her peeled apples," she said, to the dark.

"He does."

"He used to do that for me. When I lost my milk teeth." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, though it was dry. "He'd sit on the edge of my bed and peel an apple in one long curl, and if the peel didn't break he said I could make a wish." Her voice cracked very slightly. "How can a man who does that be meeting the Ashen at midnight in a ruin?"

Romeo was quiet for a moment.

"I don't know," he said. "But I've never met anybody who was only one thing."

On the beam above them, Sergeant woke, and stretched, and looked down at the two of them with his black bright eyes, and for once did not make a sound.

---

Her father's study light was on when she came back up through the house.

She should have walked past. She knew she should. But the door was open a crack, and he looked up, and saw her, and smiled, and said, "Susan. Come in, cara. I can't sleep either."

So she went in.

The study smelled of paper and old leather and the lemon oil the maids used on the wood. Her father sat at his desk in his shirtsleeves, with his reading glasses pushed up into his grey hair and a stack of documents in front of him that he turned face down, gently, as she came in. Not hiding them. Just the habit of a man who had been turning things face down for a very long time.

She sat in the leather chair across from him, the one she'd curled up in to read when she was small.

"You looked well at dinner," he said. "Your friends are good people. The captain especially. Speaking Zetan to Ambassador Sath's guard captain." He shook his head, smiling. "Half the Assembly can't do that."

"She aced everything," Susan said. "At school they had to invent new grades."

He laughed. It was his real laugh, the one she remembered from being small. It hurt to hear it.

"Susan," he said. "Tomorrow. I want to ask you something." He hesitated. "Will you wear your ring?"

She went very still.

"Your ring," he said again. "The one we had made for you. It's been in that box for a year. You've never put it on." He spread his hands on the desk, and she looked at them. His hands. His bare hands. The pale band of skin on the fourth finger of the right hand, where something had sat for thirty years and wasn't sitting now. "The whole Assembly will be here. The Zetans. Everyone. I'd like them to see you as one of us. As a de Ricci. I'd like..." He stopped. "I'd like to see it."

"Where's yours?" Susan said.

She hadn't meant to. It just came out, in her ordinary voice, as if she were asking where he'd put the salt.

Her father looked down at his hand. Then up at her. His face was tired, and kind, and perfectly smooth.

"Lost it," he said, with a rueful little shrug. "Can you believe it? Thirty years. I was walking the upper terraces last week, checking the late harvest, and it must have come off. I've had Bastiano's boys combing the vines." He smiled. "Your mother is furious. She says it's an omen."

Susan looked at him.

She had known he would lie. She had known it since the squad room, since the lamplight on the gold and the little engraved letters inside the band. She had prepared for it the whole drive down. She had thought it would feel like a knife.

It didn't. It felt like nothing. Like a door closing very quietly in another part of the house.

*A de Ricci should always be able to do three things perfectly*, Nonna had said. *Dance, lie, and grow a lemon tree.*

Her father did all three beautifully. He always had.

"I'll wear it," she said.

His face lit. "Will you?"

"For you," said Susan, and smiled at him, the way she had been taught, and her heart did not move at all.

---

In her room, the lemon tree leaned towards her as she came in.

She took the small wooden box out of the drawer of her dresser. She opened it. The ring lay on its bed of green velvet exactly where it had lain for a year: gold, heavy, the vine and the key worked on its face. She turned it in her fingers and looked inside the band. *S. de R.*

She had never worn it because she had never wanted to be what it said.

She slid it onto the fourth finger of her right hand. It fitted perfectly. It had been made to.

Then she sat on the edge of her bed in the dark and looked at it for a long time, the gold catching faintly in the light from the window, and thought about the black stone on the old wall at the top of the vineyard, and a mare who was not afraid of fire, and a father who peeled apples in one long curl so that his daughter could make a wish.

She made one now. Silently. With her hand closed tight around the ring.

She did not tell anyone what it was.
