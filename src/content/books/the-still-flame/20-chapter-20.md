The old trial hall at headquarters had not been opened to the public in eighty years.

It was a round stone room, very high, with a domed glass roof and a gallery running all the way round under it, like the inside of a bell. Seven stations stood in a ring on the floor, each in its own circle of pale stone set into the dark. Juliette had sat her own trial here at thirteen. She remembered how cold the floor had been, and how small she had felt, and how the moving air at station one had come to her like a dog running to its name.

The floor was still cold. The gallery was full.

The Assembly's Committee on Western Affairs sat in the front row, in their red sashes. Behind them, rank on rank, the rest of the Assembly, who had come because the Committee had come. Generals. Journalists, with their cameras, more than Juliette had ever seen in one place. And on the eastern side of the gallery, in a block of sand-coloured robes, the Zetans, with Ambassador Sath in the middle and Observer Teyl at his shoulder.

Councillor Roe sat in the centre of the front row with his hands folded on his round stomach and a pleasant, interested smile on his face, like a man at the theatre.

Varga had not arrested him. She could not. Not yet. He had stood up in the Assembly first and made himself the man asking questions, and a man asking questions could not be locked up for asking them, not in Haven, not with every camera in the city pointed at him. So he sat in the front row and smiled, and Varga stood on the floor below him in plain grey by the door, perfectly still, and the two of them did not look at each other once.

Juliette stood just inside the ring of stations, three paces from the seventh.

She was the only person on the floor except for the trial officer, a nervous grey-haired major with a slate, and Dr Pell, who had insisted on being present and was perched on a stool by the wall with his enormous spectacles and an expression of acute scientific interest. Romeo and Susan were in the gallery, at the very front, by the rail. Juliette could feel them both. Romeo's heart, steady. Susan's, quick and hard.

She had asked Varga to let her stand on the floor. *If it goes wrong, ma'am. I'm the only one who's done it.*

Varga had looked at her for a long time. At her arm in its sling. At her face.

*Can you?* she'd said.

And Juliette had said *yes, ma'am*, because that was what you said.

She did not know if it was true.

---

They brought him in at the tenth bell.

He walked in through the north door in plain EDF grey, without an escort, the way cadets always walked into the trial hall: alone. He walked to the centre of the ring of stations and stopped. He did not look up at the gallery. He did not look at Roe. He looked, for one moment, at Juliette.

She nodded once. *I'm here.*

He nodded back.

Then the trial officer cleared his throat and began to read from his slate, in a voice that shook only slightly, the old formal words that had been read in this room for three hundred years. *Cadet Dimitri Gale. Load, registered at reassessment. Affinity, none recorded. You stand to be tried again before the stations, that what is in you may be known, and named, and trained to the service of Eta.*

It was meant to be a quiet ceremony. A handful of officers, a scribe. Juliette had never heard it read to a full gallery before. In the great stone bell of the room, with five hundred people holding their breath, the old words sounded different. They sounded like a sentence.

"Station one," said the officer. "Air."

Dimitri walked to the first circle and stood in it. A vent in the floor opened, and a current of air rose around him, steady, cool. Juliette felt it like a hand on her own face. It lifted his hair, stirred his sleeves.

Nothing else.

The major marked his slate. "No sympathy."

Station two. Water, in a shallow stone basin, perfectly flat. It did not tremble. Station three, a tray of dark soil. It did not stir. Station four, a young olive tree in a pot, its leaves silver-green. They did not turn. Station five, a small grey dove in a wicker box, blinking. It did not look at him.

Station six: a ring of brass weights hanging on fine chains from a frame. They did not swing.

Each time, the major said, "No sympathy," and marked his slate, and the gallery let out a little breath, and drew it in again.

And each time, Juliette watched Dimitri's hands.

They were at his sides. Open. Perfectly still. And she knew, because she had felt it in the Undercroft and the cellar and a dark ballroom, that every second of the walk round that ring he was holding something back that wanted to come out. Like a man walking through a crowd with a wolf on a leash, smiling.

"Station seven," said the major. His voice had gone very quiet. "Fire."

---

The seventh station was the oldest. It was a stone bowl on a stone pillar, waist high, with a small iron brazier inside, and in the brazier a single flame, burning on a bed of oil. It had been lit at dawn by the trial officer, as it was lit every morning, by hand, with a match.

It flickered. All fire flickered. It danced and leaned and shivered in its iron bowl, restless and hungry, the way fire on Psi had always danced.

Dimitri walked to it, and stood in the circle, and looked at it.

The flame leaned towards him.

The gallery made a sound. Not a gasp. Something lower. Five hundred people breathing out at once.

The major lifted his stylus.

And the flame stopped.

Not went out. *Stopped.* In one instant it went from a dancing, flickering, living thing to something perfectly, impossibly still. A gold teardrop in an iron bowl, as motionless as if someone had painted it on the air.

Juliette had seen it in the Undercroft, a little coin above his palm. She had never seen him do it to someone else's fire.

She looked at his face, and her stomach dropped, because he looked as shocked as she was.

He wasn't doing it.

"Dimitri," she said, low.

He didn't hear her. He was staring at the flame. And the flame, in its iron bowl, was beginning to grow. Not wild. Not reaching. Just rising, slowly, smoothly, still, like water filling a glass. A hand's height. Two. Three. A column of perfect gold, without a single flicker, rising out of the brazier towards the glass dome far above.

In the gallery, somebody screamed.

And then Juliette heard it.

She would never be able to explain it properly, afterwards. Not to the inquiry. Not to Romeo. Not even to Dimitri, who heard it differently. It was not a sound. Not exactly. It was the air. The air around the column of fire was moving in a way she had never felt air move, trembling, in very fine fast waves, the way the air trembles around a plucked string. And she was the air. She had always been the air. So the waves moved through her, too.

And they were words.

A voice. Low. Warm. Even. A voice that had never needed to be raised.

*There you are.*

---

Dimitri's lips moved. She could see it. He was answering. Silently. Not sending; the Choir would have caught that. Just speaking, inside himself, to the voice in the fire.

She couldn't hear what he said. But she could hear what came back. Through the trembling air, through her own skin.

*I'm not here, son. Don't look for me in the room. I'm a long way off. But fire talks to fire, if you know how. You'll learn that. You'll learn a great deal.*

The column rose. Four hands. Five. The heat came off it in a wall. The major had backed away against the far wall with his slate clutched to his chest. Dr Pell had risen from his stool and was standing very straight, his spectacles flashing gold.

*They put you in a ring and asked what you are. Let's tell them, shall we.*

"No," Juliette said. Out loud. "No. Dimitri. Don't let him..."

*I held your bell.* The voice in the air was quieter now. Almost gentle. *The night you were born. Not nine years later. That night. I came down out of the Reach and stood outside your mother's window in the rain, and when she was asleep I came in and put my hand on you. Just once. I had learned how, on the way north. Before I ever reached the snow.* A pause. *She never knew. Aurelio never knew. I made your fire still and I made it quiet, so nobody would come for you. Not the Assembly. Not the EDF. Not the ones in the mountains.*

The ones in the mountains.

Juliette's breath caught.

*But somebody already has, haven't they? A councillor with a smile. And soon, others. You can't sleep anymore, son. Not with the whole world looking at you.* The voice was very soft. *So I'm taking my hand away.*

"*No*," said Juliette.

*Come north,* said Idris Kane, *when you want to know the rest. I'll be where the snow starts.*

And the voice in the air was gone.

And Dimitri screamed.

---

It came out of him like a dam breaking.

Not from the brazier. From him. From his chest, his hands, his open mouth, a wall of gold fire that went up and out in a single silent rush, still, perfectly still, flawless as glass, and filled the whole centre of the trial hall from the floor to the dome.

Every station caught.

The olive tree. The wicker box; the dove burst out of it, untouched, and shot up into the light. The tray of soil, the hanging weights, the brass frame. The basin of water boiled dry in one breath. The stone circles in the floor glowed orange, then white. And in the gallery, five hundred people were on their feet, shouting, scrambling back from the rail, and the cameras were all still running, every one of them, pointed at the boy in the middle of a column of fire who was standing perfectly still with his arms out and his head back and his eyes open, burning gold.

Juliette was on her knees. She didn't remember falling. The heat was a wall, a fist. Her sling was smouldering. Her skin felt tight across her face.

*Fire needs air. I'm air.*

She pulled.

She reached out with everything she had, the way she had in the cellar, in the Undercroft, in a dark ballroom, and she took hold of the air around that column of fire, all of it, and she pulled it away. She dragged it back from the flames the way she had dragged it out of an earth-shaper's lungs in an alley in the rain. She had never pulled so hard. She felt something in her chest strain, like a rope about to snap. Her nose was bleeding; she could taste it.

The air came. She felt it come, a great howling rush of it, out of the centre of the hall, out of the fire, into her. She made a hole in the world where the air had been. A vacuum. Nothing for the fire to eat.

And the fire did not care.

It stood there in the airless dark at the heart of the hall, perfectly still, perfectly gold, burning on nothing. Not shrinking. Not even flickering. As if it had never needed air at all. As if air were a thing that only ordinary fire needed, and this was not ordinary fire.

For the first time in her life, Juliette Vasquez reached for the air and it did nothing.

She knelt on the burning stone with blood running down her lip and looked up at him, through the gold, and understood that she could not stop this.

Nobody could.

"Dimitri," she whispered.

---

She didn't know if he heard her. She would never know.

But the fire turned.

Not out. Not down. It turned, the whole vast still column of it, slowly, the way a head turns, the way four hundred and twelve candle flames had turned towards a hill. It turned towards her. Towards the small figure on her knees on the stone, with her burned sling and her bleeding nose and her hand stretched out.

And it stopped.

For one long, endless moment the fire hung there, filling the trial hall, gold and still and silent, bending towards her like a tree in a wind, and she knelt in front of it with nothing at all between them, and waited for it to take her.

It didn't.

It came down instead. Slowly. Smoothly. The way it had gone up. Folding back into itself like a great bird folding its wings, smaller, and smaller, down from the dome, down past the gallery rail, down into the ring of stations, down into the space around one young man standing in the middle of a circle of white-hot stone.

Down into him.

Into his chest. His hands. His open mouth.

And went out.

---

The silence afterwards was the loudest thing Juliette had ever heard.

She got up. She didn't know how. She went across the cooling stone to him on her knees and then on her feet, and he was standing there in the middle of the ring with his arms at his sides, and his uniform was ash, falling off him in flakes, and his skin underneath was perfect, and his eyes were brown again.

He looked at her.

"I didn't," he said. His voice was cracked and shaking. "Juliette. I didn't do that. He took his hand off. He just... took it off. And it all came. All of it. I couldn't..."

"I know."

"Did you stop it?" He looked at her bleeding face, her smoking sling, with something close to desperation. "Did you take the air? Is that what happened? Did you..."

She looked at him for a long moment.

She could say yes. It would be easy. He would believe her. Everyone would believe her. The girl with the wind, who had stood in front of the fire and taken its breath away. It was a good story. It was a story she could keep him safe with.

*There's a difference,* she had told him once, on a roof, a lifetime ago. *Between a lie and leaving something out. You'll learn it.*

"No," said Juliette. "I didn't stop it."

His face went still.

"I pulled every bit of air out of this room," she said, very quietly. "And it didn't care. It didn't need it." She held his eyes. "You stopped it, Dimitri. Not me. You turned it round. You looked at me, and you brought it back. You chose to."

He stared at her.

"I don't know if I can do it again," she said. "I don't know if I can ever do it again. I need you to know that. I'm not going to lie to you about it."

And he stood in the ash of his uniform in the middle of the ruined trial hall and looked at her, and something moved in his face that she did not have a word for.

Then, slowly, he reached out with his perfect, unburned hand, and wiped the blood off her lip with his thumb.

Neither of them said anything.

---

In the gallery, the cameras were still running.

Juliette turned, finally, and looked up.

Five hundred faces. White, every one of them. The Committee in their red sashes, pressed back against their seats. The generals. The journalists, still holding up their cameras with shaking hands. Romeo and Susan at the rail, gripping each other's arms. Dr Pell, by the wall, with his spectacles off and his mouth open.

And Councillor Bastien Roe, in the centre of the front row, on his feet now, his round face scarlet, his finger outstretched, pointing down at Dimitri. His mouth was open. He was shouting something. Juliette could not hear it. It didn't matter. She could see the shape of the word on his lips, and so could every camera in the room.

*Monster.*

On the eastern side of the gallery, the Zetans had risen too.

Every one of them. All twenty. Ambassador Sath in the middle, old and grey without his gold paint, his red eyes fixed on the floor of the trial hall. Observer Teyl at his shoulder.

And as Juliette watched, the old Ambassador put his hands open at his sides, and bowed from the waist.

And one by one, in a slow, silent wave along the eastern gallery, every Zetan in the room bowed with him.

On the floor by the north door, Commander Varga stood perfectly still, as she always did, and watched it all, and did not move. But Juliette, who was the wind, and who was listening for it, heard her breathe out. Once. A long, slow, shaking breath, like a woman who had been holding it for nineteen years.

---

That night, all across Haven, in every home and tavern and barracks and chapel, people would watch it on their screens. Again and again. The boy in the ring of stones. The fire that did not flicker, rising to the dome. The girl on her knees in front of it with her hand outstretched. The councillor, pointing. The Zetans, bowing.

And in the Fourth District, in a little chapel at the bottom of some stone steps, an old woman in a blue shawl would watch it too, with a cup of tea going cold in her hands and tears running down her face. And then she would get up, and light every candle in the chapel, all of them, and sit down on the second bench by the pillar, and sing.

And for one moment, all over the city, every flame in Haven would burn perfectly still.

And then, slowly, like breath let out, begin to dance again.
