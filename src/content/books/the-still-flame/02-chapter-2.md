The medic ran the scanner over his arm three times, and three times the screen came back the same colour: clean.

"No burns," she said. "No blistering. Not even redness." She looked at him over the top of the scanner the way people looked at a dog that had just said a word. "Your sleeve's gone, though."

"I noticed," Dimitri said.

She left him on the cot with a fresh shirt and a cup of something sweet and terrible. He didn't drink it. He sat in the white light of the medical bay and stared at his right forearm, at the fine hairs that should have burned away and hadn't, and waited to feel something other than sick.

The Choir hummed in the back of his skull. Normal. Steady. It had never once felt like company to him the way it seemed to for other people. It felt like being watched by someone polite.

A line of soft text slid into his awareness.

*Post-engagement review. Unmonitored zone. Assessment Wing, Room 4, 0800.*

Of course. Anyone who went dark got a review. It was routine. He told himself that twice.

---

He had been seeded at three days old, like everyone. He had been measured at three days old, like everyone. The number had been so small that the technician had written *trace* and then, his mother told him later, crossed it out and written *negligible* instead.

He'd grown up believing that. He'd liked believing it. Kids at school could make puddles shiver or pencils roll, and he could do nothing, and that had felt safe in a way he didn't have words for until he was nine and the apartment burned and his mother didn't come out.

After that, fire was just the thing that took her. It wasn't anything to do with him.

Then, at eighteen, the routine reassessment everyone sat on the way to adulthood. A bored technician. A machine that beeped wrong. A second machine, brought in from another room. A third. Then two officers in EDF grey who were very polite and very fast, and a form with his name already on it.

*Late bloomer*, they called it. It happened sometimes. Not like this, one of them had said, and then stopped talking as if he'd said too much.

That was eleven months ago. He still didn't know what he was.

---

Romeo came in at midnight with a crow on his shoulder.

It was a big, glossy, untidy bird with one white feather in its left wing, and it looked at Dimitri with the flat, unimpressed patience of a sergeant inspecting a recruit's boots. Which was, Romeo claimed, exactly why he'd named it Sergeant.

"Crows came over with the Migration," Romeo said, dropping into the chair beside the cot. "Earth bird. Proper old. Smarter than half the officers on this base, and better company than all of them." Sergeant made a dry clicking sound. "See? He agrees."

"You smuggled a bird into medical."

"I smuggle a bird into everywhere. Nobody stops a man with a crow. It's a confidence thing." Romeo leaned forward and looked at Dimitri's arm. He didn't say anything clever about it, which was how Dimitri knew he was worried. "How do you feel?"

"Like I'm going to throw up."

"Good. That's the correct response. If you'd said 'powerful' I'd have had Sergeant peck your eyes out."

They sat for a while. Outside the narrow window, the lights of the city ran all the way to the dark line of the hills, and beyond those, Dimitri knew, the land just kept going: forest, then coast, then places on the map coloured in faint grey and labelled *Nuite territory: no transit.*

"There was graffiti on the way back," Romeo said. "On the overpass by the dye works. Same thing they're painting everywhere this year." He drew it in the air with one finger. "*Psi is wide.*"

"Ashen?"

"Ashen. Their whole philosophy fits on a wall, which I respect. 'Our ancestors crossed the stars. Why are we rationing water on one continent?' You don't need the Choir if there's enough for everybody. Just go and take more."

"Take it from who?"

Romeo smiled without humour. "That's the part they don't paint."

The truce with the Zetans was fourteen months old. Dimitri had been in the EDF for eleven of them. He'd never fought in the war, and he'd grown up hearing about it the way you heard about weather somewhere else. Romeo and Juliette had grown up inside it.

"Is she all right?" Dimitri asked.

Romeo didn't need to ask who. He was quiet for a moment, scratching under Sergeant's beak.

"Julie is always all right," he said. "That's the thing about Julie." He stood, and the crow shifted its weight on his shoulder. "Get some sleep, firestarter. Your review's at eight. Mine's at nine. Hers was this afternoon. She's filed the squad report already, so just tell them what's in it."

He was at the door when Dimitri said, "What's in it?"

Romeo paused. "You'd have to ask her."

---

Assessment Room 4 had no windows and one long table, and there were two people sitting behind it.

The first was Commander Ilse Varga, whom Dimitri had seen exactly twice, both times from a distance, both times making someone else stand very straight. She was grey-haired and narrow and had the stillness of a person who had been in a great many rooms where things went badly and had decided long ago not to let any of it show.

The second was not human.

Dimitri had seen Zetans on the news, and once, briefly, at a trade market as a child. Never this close. The Zetan was the same height as a man, and built like one, but its skin was the deep warm colour of dune shadow, and a ridge of fine, hard plates ran along its jaw and up behind its ears, catching the light like sandstone. Its eyes had a second, clear lid that slid across them when it blinked, slow and deliberate, as if it were filing away everything it saw.

"Observer Teyl," Varga said, without looking at the Zetan. "Here under the terms of the truce. Exchange of omicron research. You may ignore it."

"Please do," the Zetan said pleasantly. Its voice was dry and very precise, each word set down like a stone on a path.

Dimitri sat. The Choir hummed. Somewhere, he knew, his heart rate was being drawn as a neat little line on a screen he couldn't see.

Varga asked her questions. When did they enter the zone. What did they find. How many hostiles. The chemical pistol. The water. Corran Vell's escape through the floor drain. The handprint.

"Captain Vasquez reports the mark was still warm," Varga said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"You confirmed that?"

"I held my hand near it. Yes."

Varga made a small note. "And during the engagement. Your own conduct."

Here it was.

"Captain Vasquez reports," Varga read, "that Cadet Gale held the stairwell and provided support. No omicron use by the cadet." She looked up. "Is that accurate?"

Dimitri felt the floor of the room tilt very slightly, the way the street had when the Choir went dark.

The Choir hadn't seen it. The Choir couldn't see anything in a dead zone. Which meant the only record of what had happened on that second floor was Juliette's report. And Juliette had written nothing at all.

He thought of the rope of fire coiling around his arm. Of Corran's face. *They told me there'd be one of you.*

If he told the truth now, the report was a lie, and the lie was hers. If he backed the report, the lie was his too.

"Yes, ma'am," he said. "That's accurate."

It came out steady. He was proud of that for exactly one second, and then he felt his heart going like a fist on a door and knew that somewhere a neat little line had just jumped.

Varga's eyes did not move from his face. She didn't glance at any screen. She didn't need to.

The Zetan tilted its head. The clear lid slid across its eyes and back.

"Your heart is very loud, cadet," Observer Teyl said mildly.

"He was in a fight eleven hours ago," Varga said, before Dimitri could answer. "Cadets' hearts are loud." She closed the file. "Review complete. Dismissed."

He stood. He saluted. He was nearly at the door when the Zetan spoke again, not to him, but not quite to Varga either.

"In my language," it said, "we have a word for what you call a late bloomer."

Dimitri stopped.

"It does not mean late," Teyl said. "It means *hidden.*"

"Dismissed, cadet," Varga said again, and this time it was not a suggestion.

---

He found her on the training roof, which was where she always was when she wasn't anywhere else.

Juliette was running drills alone in the cold morning light. Not with her omicron. Just footwork. Knife work. Over and over, the same six movements, each one exact. The wind moved around her in little eddies anyway, as if it couldn't help itself.

She didn't stop when he came up the stairs. She didn't look at him.

"You left it out," he said.

Step, turn, cut. "Left what out?"

"Juliette."

She stopped. She stood with her back to him, breathing hard, and then she sheathed the knife and turned around. Her face was perfectly composed. That was the frightening thing about her, he was starting to realise. It always was.

"If I'd written it down," she said, "Varga would have you off my squad by tonight. You'd be in a facility by the end of the week with wires in your head and a dozen researchers taking turns setting you on fire to see what happens. That's what they wanted to do with Susan. The only reason they didn't is that her family owns the southern basin." She held his eyes. "Your father doesn't own anything. Does he?"

He had nothing to say to that. He had never had anything to say about his father.

"So you lied."

"I left something out." Something crossed her face, too quick to read. "There's a difference. You'll learn it."

"There was a Zetan in my review."

That landed. He watched it land. "What did it say?"

"That my heart was loud." He hesitated. "And that late bloomer means *hidden* in Zetan."

Juliette was quiet for a long moment. The wind had gone still around her.

"Hidden by who?" she said.

"I don't know."

"Then that's the question." She picked up her jacket from the rail. On her way past him to the stairs she stopped, close enough that he could see the scar on her chin, a thin white line so fine it only showed in this kind of light. He had never asked about it. Whoever put it there had been fast, and very few people were faster than Juliette.

"You backed me in there," she said quietly. "I won't forget it."

Then she was gone, and he was alone on the roof with the city spread out below him, and somewhere far past the hills, a land that was wide.

He stood there a long time, composing a message in his head. *Why did you really do it?* He shaped the words carefully, every letter. He held them right at the edge of sending.

Then he let them go unsent, because everything sent was kept, and he was beginning to understand what that meant.
