Nobody slept in the de Ricci house that night.

Varga's soldiers brought them all down the hill in a long, silent line of lamps, through the vines, with Aurelio walking at the front beside the commander and neither of them saying a single word. At the bottom, in the kitchen garden, Varga had stopped and turned to him, and Susan, a few paces behind, had heard her say only two words, in a voice so low it was almost not a voice at all.

"Not here."

And her father had nodded, as if he had been waiting twenty years to hear exactly that.

Now it was nearly dawn. The ballroom was a wreck of overturned chairs and spilled wine and four hundred and twelve cold candles. The Assembly had gone, hustled away in a convoy of cars with their red sashes crumpled in their laps. The Zetans had withdrawn to the east wing, where their guards stood at every door with curved blades bare, and had sent a single message down to the house through Observer Teyl, written in formal Zetan on a folded card.

Juliette had read it aloud in the kitchen, at the long scrubbed table where the staff ate, with her bad arm in its sling and her face grey with exhaustion.

*The Ambassador of the Sere thanks the House of de Ricci for its hospitality. The Ambassador regrets that he cannot speak of water, or borders, or peace, with a people who allow knives in their halls. The Ambassador will sign nothing until he knows whose hand held this one. The Ambassador will wait three days.*

Three days.

The footman, the hollow with the crescent scar, was in the cellar with four of Varga's soldiers. He had not said a word. He had no Choir to read, no records, no history the network could find. He was nobody. A man who had burned himself out of the world. The Zetans could hear a lie in a man's blood. They could not hear a silence.

"He won't talk," Romeo had said, at the long table, rubbing his eyes. "Hollows never talk. That's the whole point of them."

Susan had sat very still, with her hands folded on the scrubbed wood, and the ring on her finger, and said nothing at all.

She already knew who to ask.

---

She found Matteo in the wine cellar.

Not the great vaulted cellar under the house where the reserve vintages slept in their racks, but the old one, down the stone stairs behind the press house, where nobody had stored anything for a hundred years. When they were children, he used to bring her down here to frighten her. He'd told her the de Ricci dead were buried in the walls, and that if you put your ear to the stone you could hear the vines growing through their bones.

She had believed him for years. He had been very good at telling stories.

He was sitting on an upturned crate with his back against the wall and a bottle between his feet, and when she came down the stairs with a lamp, he didn't look up.

"Little sister," he said. "Come to see the family vault?"

"Who hired the footman, Matteo?"

He laughed. It was a tired sound. "Straight to it. The army's ruined you. You used to be fun at parties."

"I was never fun at parties."

"No." He took a drink from the bottle. "You weren't, were you. You used to sit in the corner reading manuals. Nonna's favourite. The good one. The one with two gifts." He looked at her then, finally, and his eyes were red-rimmed and very clear. "Do you know what my gift is, Susan? Do you remember? Nobody does. I can make a grapevine grow an inch a day if I concentrate very hard. That's it. That's the whole of it. A trace and a half. They didn't even bother to send the polite men in grey for me. I'm twenty-six years old and the most interesting thing about me is my surname."

"Who hired the footman?"

"An agency in the Second District. Very respectable. They supply staff to half the chartered houses in Haven."

"And who told you to use it?"

Matteo looked at her for a long time.

"He told you," he said softly. "Didn't he. Up on the hill. Papa's monster." He laughed again, an ugly, broken sound. "Of course he did. He knows everything. He's known everything for years. He's been sitting in that ruin like a spider, holding Papa's leash, and Papa thinks it's the other way round."

"Matteo."

"Do you know what I found, when I took over the basin this spring?" He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, the bottle dangling. "The books. The real books, not the ones the Assembly sees. Twenty years of water going out through the back door. To the Fourth District. To the Wayfarers. To a deserter and his little army of hollows. Our water, Susan. Our grandmother's water, and her grandmother's. Bought and paid for with a de Ricci name." His voice shook. "And not once did Papa ask for anything back. Not once. Just peace. Just quiet. Just *please don't hurt anyone, please stay on your hill.*"

"So you..."

"So I asked what else the water could buy." He sat back against the wall. "Turns out, quite a lot. There are people in the Ashen who don't want peace. Who think your father's monster has gone soft. Who think the only way out of the cage is through the Zetans, and the sooner the better. And there are people in the Assembly who agree with them. Quietly. Over dinner." He took another drink. "One of them came to me in the spring. Very kind. Very understanding. He said I was wasted on the basin. He said a man like me could help make history."

"Who?" Susan said.

Her voice was perfectly calm. Her heart was not. She could feel it, high in her throat, hammering.

Matteo looked at her. For a moment he was nine years old again, leading her down these very stairs with a candle, whispering about the dead in the walls. Grinning. Waiting for her to scream.

"Councillor Roe," he said.

---

She had met Bastien Roe four times. At the Assembly's midwinter ball. At her cousin's wedding. At a reception for the Vintners' Guild, where he had complimented her mother's earrings and her father's wine. And last night, in the ballroom, an hour before the candles went out, where he had stood by the fireplace in his red sash with a glass of the house red in his hand and talked at length to the Speaker about the importance of strength in negotiations.

A small, round, smiling man. With beautiful manners. Who chaired the Assembly's Committee on Western Affairs.

"He said it would be a scare," Matteo said. He was not looking at her now. He was looking at the bottle. "Just a scare. Something to show the Zetans that the treaty wasn't safe. That humans weren't all as soft as the Speaker. Something to slow things down, so the Committee could argue against the pipeline, so we didn't sell our water west for nothing." His voice cracked. "He said nobody would be hurt."

"There was a knife, Matteo."

"I didn't know about the knife."

"There was a knife a foot from the Ambassador's boot. There were twenty Zetan blades drawn in our mother's ballroom. My captain was in the middle of them with one arm."

"I didn't *know*, Susan."

She looked at him. At her brother, on his crate, in the dark, with the bottle between his feet. She thought of every time he had laughed at her, and every time he had picked her up when she fell, and the time he'd taught her to ride Vesper when Vesper was young, running beside her down the long drive holding the bridle, shouting *you're doing it, you're doing it*.

She did not know if he was lying.

She realised, with a cold little shock, that she had no way to know. She had grown up in this house. She had grown up among people who could lie with their whole bodies, smiling, while their hearts stayed perfectly still. She had been raised to do it herself.

And she understood, very suddenly, exactly what she had to do.

"Get up," she said.

"What?"

"Get up, Matteo. You're coming with me."

---

The east wing was very quiet.

The Zetan guards at the door watched them come down the long corridor: Susan in front, still in her torn ballgown with a borrowed EDF jacket over it, her hair half down, the ring on her finger; Matteo behind her, unsteady, pale, his shirt stained with wine; and Romeo, a pace behind Matteo, with his hands in his pockets and Sergeant on his shoulder and an expression of perfect pleasant calm that Susan had learned meant he was ready to do something violent at very short notice.

She had not asked him to come. He had simply been at the top of the cellar stairs when she came up, leaning against the wall, as if he had been waiting there all along.

"Juliette's with the house doctor, getting her arm reset. She was with Varga till the commander walked off without a word," he'd said. "Dimitri's with Juliette. I'm with you." And then, looking at Matteo: "Whatever you're doing."

The guard captain with the white streak down her nose stepped forward to block the door. Susan stopped in front of her, and put her hand open against her chest, and bowed from the neck. *I show you I carry nothing.*

Then she said, in the halting Zetan that Juliette had drilled into her one evening in the training hall, three words. They were the only three she was confident of.

"Ambassador. Please. Truth."

The guard captain looked at her. Then at Matteo. Her clear second eyelids slid across her eyes, and back. She was listening. Susan could feel it, the way she'd felt it under her father's sleeve. A draught under a door.

The captain turned, and knocked once on the door behind her, and said something low in Zetan.

After a long moment, it opened.

---

Ambassador Sath was sitting by the window in a high-backed chair, in a plain grey robe, without his gold paint. In the early light he looked much older. Observer Teyl stood behind him. Two more guards by the wall.

And by the cold fireplace, in EDF grey, with her hands clasped behind her back, stood Commander Varga.

Susan's step faltered for an instant. Then she kept walking.

Varga's eyes moved over her. Over Matteo. Over Romeo. Her face showed nothing at all.

"Cadet de Ricci," she said.

"Commander." Susan stopped in the middle of the room, and turned to the old Zetan by the window, and bowed again. Then she spoke in the common tongue, slowly and clearly, the way you would speak in a court.

"Ambassador. My brother has something to say to you. He says it is the truth. I can't tell whether it is." She swallowed. "I was raised not to be able to tell. But I am told that you can."

The room was utterly silent.

Sath looked at her for a long time with his dark red eyes. Then he looked at Matteo, swaying slightly, white as paper, behind her.

"You would bring your own brother here," he said slowly. "To be listened to. By a Zetan."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Susan thought about it. She thought about her father on the hill, saying *I told myself it was a kind of peace.* About Nonna in the conservatory, saying *when the roots go bad, it never starts where you can see it.* About Juliette in the squad room, saying *I don't care whose name is on it*, and about her own neat signature at the bottom of a report that had gone into Varga's drawer and disappeared.

"Because three weeks ago I signed his name to a report," she said, "and it was buried, because the truth was inconvenient. And last night someone nearly died in my mother's house because of it." She held the Ambassador's eyes. "I'm a de Ricci. We're very good at making things disappear. I'd like to stop."

Sath was silent for a moment.

Then he lifted one long, thin hand, and beckoned Matteo forward.

"Come," he said. "Sit. Here, by me. And tell me about the knife."

---

Matteo told it.

All of it. The books. The water. The kind, round, smiling councillor who had come to him in the spring. The agency in the Second District. The footman, who had come with excellent references, and who Matteo had personally approved for the reception staff, a week ago, without asking a single question.

He told it badly, stumbling, starting sentences and abandoning them, his voice cracking. Twice he stopped and put his face in his hands. Once he laughed, horribly, at nothing.

And the whole time, Ambassador Sath sat beside him with his eyes closed. Both lids. Perfectly still. Listening to something none of the rest of them could hear.

When Matteo got to the part about the knife, *he said nobody would be hurt, I didn't know, I swear I didn't know about the knife*, the old Zetan's head tilted, very slightly. As if he had heard a wrong note in a piece of music.

Susan's heart stopped.

Then Sath straightened his head again, and said nothing, and Matteo went on.

When it was over, the room was silent for a long time.

Ambassador Sath opened his eyes.

"He believes," he said slowly, "that he did not know about the knife." A pause. "That is not quite the same as not knowing. There is a part of him that suspected. A part he did not wish to listen to. I heard it, very faintly, like a second heart under the first." The red eyes turned on Matteo, who flinched. "You did not ask, Matteo de Ricci, because you did not want to hear the answer. That is a very human crime. We have a word for it. It means *the one who looks at the sky so as not to see his feet.*"

Matteo put his face in his hands.

"But the name," said Sath. "Bastien Roe. That, he did not doubt. Not for one heartbeat." He turned his head towards the fireplace. "Commander Varga. I believe you will want to speak to the Committee on Western Affairs."

"I believe I will," said Varga.

She had not moved from the fireplace. But something in her face had changed. Something Susan had never seen there before. It took her a moment to place it.

It was the look of a heron, standing for hours in the shallows, that has finally seen the fish.

"Ambassador," said Varga. "Your three days."

"Stand," said Sath. "For now. Bring me the councillor. Bring me proof that your Assembly will cut out its own rot, and I will sit at the table again." He looked at Susan. "And I would like this one there, when I do. In her torn dress. With her ring."

Susan bowed. She did not trust her voice.

The Ambassador inclined his head. Then, as Varga's soldiers came in to take Matteo, gently, by the arms, he said something else. Not to Susan. Not to Varga. To Romeo, who had been standing silent by the door all this time with his crow on his shoulder.

"The fire-child," said Sath. "Your captain's friend. Where is he now?"

Romeo blinked. "With my sister, sir. Why?"

Sath did not answer immediately. He looked out of the window, towards the hill, towards the top of the vineyard where the ruin stood dark against the dawn.

"Because last night I bowed to him," he said, "and I would like him to understand why, before somebody else explains it to him wrongly." His eyes came back to the room. "Bring him to me tonight, beast-speaker. Alone. There is a story my people tell. He should hear it from one of us."

---

Matteo looked back once, at the door, between the soldiers.

He looked at Susan. His face was wet. He opened his mouth as if to say something, and then he didn't, and then he was gone, and the door closed behind him.

Susan stood very straight in the middle of the room in her torn ballgown and her borrowed jacket, with her ring on her finger, and did not move.

She felt someone step up beside her. Not touching. Just there. The way he had been in the stable, and on the dance floor, and at the top of the cellar stairs.

"That," said Romeo, very quietly, "was the bravest thing I have ever seen anyone do."

"He's my brother."

"I know."

"He taught me to ride."

"I know."

She stood there. Across the room, Ambassador Sath had closed his eyes again. He was listening. She knew exactly what he was hearing, and she did not care.

She let her heart race. All the way. As loud as it wanted. For the first time in her life, at a de Ricci table, in a de Ricci house, she let it.

And beside her, very gently, Romeo took her hand.
