Romeo had a theory that everyone was an animal.

Not in the insulting way. In the useful way. It had come to him slowly, over years of borrowing the heads of rats and pigeons and the occasional extremely offended cat, that the shape a creature's mind made was not so different from the shape a person's did. You just had to stop listening to what they said and start watching what they did when they were hungry, or frightened, or cornered.

Varga, for example, was a heron. Stood perfectly still in the shallows for hours, looking like furniture, and then struck so fast you didn't see it happen.

Observer Teyl was a tortoise. Old before its time. Slow on purpose. Carrying its whole house on its back and never once letting you see inside.

Susan was a cat. Obviously. Groomed within an inch of her life, sat very straight, pretended not to care whether you came or went, and would, if you were patient and did not make sudden movements, eventually decide that you were allowed to exist near her.

Dimitri was a large young dog. The kind with enormous paws he hadn't grown into yet, who didn't know his own size, who would follow you anywhere and knock things over with his tail and look heartbroken when you shouted. And who, one day, Romeo suspected, would turn out to be a wolf.

And Jules was a hawk. She had always been a hawk. Even when they were six years old in a tent at the edge of a war, stripping rifles blindfolded, she had sat on the edge of her cot with that hawk look on her face, watching everything, missing nothing, waiting for something to move.

Romeo himself, he had decided long ago, was a crow. Clever, loud, everybody's friend, nobody's pet. Good at finding shiny things and better at making people laugh before they noticed he'd taken something.

Sergeant disagreed. Sergeant thought Romeo was a pigeon.

They had agreed not to discuss it.

---

"Sugar," Romeo murmured. "Second tray. Behind the teapot. Don't be greedy."

It was breakfast in the mess hall, three days after the ring. Across the room, Sergeant Ordo, who ran the kitchens and had arms like hams and a face like a closed door, was supervising the tea urns with deep suspicion. Behind him, on a high shelf, sat the officers' sugar bowl, which no cadet in the history of the base had ever successfully raided.

On the rafter above the shelf, a black shape detached itself from the shadows, dropped without a sound, and was back up in the rafters before Ordo had finished turning around.

Romeo's crow landed on the table in front of him, set down a single perfect sugar cube by his mug, and looked at him with an expression of profound contempt.

"Thank you, Sergeant," Romeo said.

Across the hall, Sergeant Ordo turned, looked at the sugar bowl, looked at the rafters, and then looked across the whole length of the mess directly at Romeo, who raised his mug in salute.

"Vasquez," Ordo growled.

"Sergeant."

"Two crows in my kitchen. One with feathers. I'll have you both in a pie."

"You'd miss me."

Ordo's face did not change. But on his way back to the urns, he set a second sugar cube on the corner of Romeo's table, without looking, and walked on.

The first-years at the next table stared. One of them, a girl with a shaved head and a scar through one eyebrow, caught Romeo's eye and grinned. He winked at her. She went red and looked away, and her friends hit her.

It was, Romeo reflected, a very small skill. But a man used the skills he had.

He put both sugar cubes in his tea, which made it almost drinkable, and looked down the table at his squad, and stopped smiling.

They looked terrible.

Jules had her arm in the sling and dark circles under her eyes and was eating porridge one-handed as if it had personally insulted her. Dimitri was sitting next to her, not quite touching, and every time she reached for something he moved it an inch closer before she got there, and every time he did it she pretended not to notice, and every time she pretended not to notice he looked at her as if she had personally hung the moons.

*Subtle*, Romeo thought. *Very subtle, the pair of you. Nobody in this entire hall has any idea.*

And Susan was sitting at the end of the bench, very straight, with a cup of tea she wasn't drinking and a piece of toast she hadn't touched, looking at nothing.

She had looked like that for three days. Since the ring. Since *A. de R.*

Romeo looked at her for a long moment. Then he picked up the second sugar cube out of his saucer, fished it out of the tea, wiped it carefully on a napkin, and slid it down the table until it stopped beside her hand.

She looked at it. Then at him.

"It's been in your tea," she said.

"Only briefly. It's had a full and happy life. It wants to be with you now."

The corner of her mouth moved. It wasn't a smile. But it was the first time anything on her face had moved in three days, and Romeo decided it would do to be going on with.

---

The dancing lessons were Susan's idea, which was the only reason nobody refused.

"You can't walk into my mother's house in nine days looking like soldiers," she said. They were in the empty training hall after dark, with the mats rolled back and the doors locked. "You'll be spotted in a minute. Everyone at that reception will have been raised to it. They'll know how you hold a glass. How you stand. What you do with your hands." She looked at Dimitri. "What are you doing with your hands?"

Dimitri looked down at his hands, which were hanging at his sides, as if they had been caught doing something illegal.

"Nothing?"

"Exactly. Nobody at a de Ricci reception has done nothing with their hands since the Migration." Susan sighed. "All right. Glasses first. Then forks. Then the Zetan protocol. Then, if there's any time left before I lose the will to live, the dancing."

"I can do the Zetan protocol," Jules said from the bench by the wall, where she was sitting with her sling and an expression of deep, superior boredom.

"Of course you can," said Romeo. "She aced it," he told the others. "She aced everything. At school they used to have to make up new grades for her. Above top marks they had *Juliette*."

"Shut up, Romeo."

"I'm being supportive."

It took an hour to get them holding wine glasses by the stem. Dimitri kept gripping his like a weapon, and the third time Susan corrected him he got flustered and the glass got very slightly warm in his hand, and everyone pretended very hard not to notice that the water in it had started to steam.

It took another hour for the forks.

Then Jules taught them the Zetan bow. Slow, from the neck, eyes lowered, one hand open at the chest. *I show you I carry nothing.* She made them do it twenty times, correcting each one with a tap of her good hand on the backs of their heads, and she was a much harsher teacher than Susan. By the end of it even Romeo was doing it properly.

"Now," Susan said. "The dancing."

There was a silence.

"Jules can't," Romeo said quickly. "Broken arm. Tragic. So she'll need a partner for... observation." He looked meaningfully at Dimitri. "Somebody to sit with her. And observe."

Jules gave him a look that could have stripped paint.

Dimitri, rubbing the back of his neck and suddenly very interested in the floor, went and sat down on the bench beside her, at a very carefully calculated distance.

"Which leaves," said Romeo, turning to Susan with a deep, sweeping, entirely inappropriate bow, "you and me, Miss de Ricci."

Susan looked at his outstretched hand as if it were a dead rat Sergeant had brought her.

"Do you actually know how to dance?" she said.

"I know how to do everything badly with total confidence. It's the same thing at a party."

She sighed. And took his hand.

---

It was not the same thing.

It turned out that the de Ricci dances were old, and formal, and very precise, with a great deal of turning and stepping and changing hands, and that Romeo's total confidence carried him exactly as far as the first turn, where he went left and Susan went right and they very nearly took each other's feet off.

"Left," said Susan. "*Left.*"

"That's what I did."

"Your other left."

"I've only got the one."

On the bench, Jules made a small sound that, on anyone else, Romeo would have called a giggle. Dimitri was openly laughing, his head down, his shoulders shaking.

They started again. Susan counted under her breath. Romeo concentrated, which he did not enjoy, and looked at his feet, which was apparently wrong, and then at her face, which was apparently right.

She had a good face for looking at. He'd noticed that the first day. Everyone noticed it the first day; that was the point of a face like Susan's. But up close, in the bad light of the training hall, with her hair coming loose again and a small furious crease between her eyebrows as she counted, it was a different face. Less like a portrait. More like a person.

"And turn," Susan said. "And step. And hand."

He turned. He stepped. He took her hand.

She was a much better dancer than he had expected. Of course she was. She'd been dancing since she could walk. But she wasn't stiff about it, the way she was stiff about everything else. When she danced, the straightness went out of her shoulders and something easier came into them, something that moved like water, or like vines in wind.

"You're good at this," he said.

"I've been doing it since I was four." She turned under his arm. "My grandmother taught me. She said a de Ricci should always be able to do three things perfectly. Dance, lie, and grow a lemon tree."

"Which are you best at?"

She came back round to face him.

"I used to think lying," she said.

The music in her head must have stopped, because her feet stopped too. They stood there in the middle of the training hall, hand in hand, and she looked at him, and for a moment the cat was gone completely, and there was just a girl who had found out three days ago that her father was meeting the Ashen in a dye works at two in the morning.

"Why did you say it?" she said quietly.

"Say what?"

"In the squad room. *It's not Susan.* You didn't know that. You couldn't have."

Romeo opened his mouth to make a joke. He had one ready. He always had one ready. It was something about Sergeant having excellent taste in people and terrible taste in food.

He looked at her face and closed his mouth again.

"Because you signed it," he said. "Your brother's name. You sat there and signed it and you didn't look for a way out." He shrugged, and it came out less casual than he wanted. "I grew up on bases, Susan. I've seen a lot of people do brave things. Most of them were shouting. You did it in perfect handwriting."

She looked at him for a long time.

"You flirt with everyone," she said. "Do you know that? The cook. Those first-years at breakfast. The medic. Me." She tilted her head. "I've been watching you. I think you do it so nobody ever notices you never actually mean any of it."

It hit him somewhere under the ribs, quick and clean, the way Jules used to hit him when they sparred as children, before he'd learned to see it coming.

He should have laughed. He knew exactly how to laugh at a moment like this. He'd done it a thousand times.

"Sometimes," he heard himself say, "I mean it."

There was a pause.

On the bench, very loudly, Jules cleared her throat.

"Are we dancing," she said, "or are we having a moment? Because Dimitri and I are bored, and my arm hurts, and it's nearly midnight."

Susan dropped his hand as if it had burned her. Which, Romeo reflected, given present company, was a figure of speech that needed retiring.

"Dancing," Susan said briskly. Her face was back. The cat was back. Her shoulders were straight. "From the top. And Romeo, if you go right again, I'm going to lift you off the floor and leave you on the ceiling."

"You can do that?"

"I've wanted to since the day we met."

He went left. He went left all the way through, perfectly, three times.

He didn't look at his feet once.

---

Afterwards, the others went up to bed. Romeo said he'd lock the hall, and stayed, and sat on the bench where Jules and Dimitri had sat, in the dark, with Sergeant on his knee.

"Well," he said to the crow. "That was stupid."

Sergeant agreed.

"She's a de Ricci. She's got a grandmother who dresses entirely in green and a father who's doing whatever her father's doing. She's going to inherit half a continent's water. And I'm a barracks kid with seven years left and a pigeon."

Sergeant made a dry, insulted sound.

"Crow. Sorry. A crow." Romeo rubbed his face. "And there's Jules. And the big dog. And they're going to fall into each other any day now and pretend they haven't, and somebody's got to be the one standing behind them with a joke, because if everybody's falling into everybody, who's going to make the tea?"

He sat there for a while in the dark.

He thought about the hill-cat. Nine years old, in a gully at the edge of a war, with his hand on a dying animal's paw. How it had felt to be all of that, all at once. Hungry and furious and free. How much he had wanted to stay.

How he'd come back, and decided, very carefully, never to want anything that much again.

And he thought about Susan's hand in his, and her shoulders going loose like vines in wind, and her face when she said *you never actually mean any of it.*

*Sometimes I mean it,* he'd said.

He hadn't meant to say that.

He'd meant it.

"I've really got to stop doing that," Romeo said to the dark.

On his knee, Sergeant shifted, and settled, and tucked his head under his wing. Somewhere in the walls, a rat scratched. The training hall ticked and creaked as it cooled. Outside, beyond the base and the city and the hills, the land went on and on, wild and wide, full of things that had never been told what to do in their lives.

Romeo locked the hall and went to bed.

He did not sleep for a long time. But for once, he didn't mind.
