"Thirty days," Varga said.

It was the second night of Susan's three, and the window behind the commander was black, and the only light in the office came from the screen above the desk. On it, a map of the continent. Eta, green and grey, with the human cities clustered along the coasts like beads on a string. To the west, the long tan stretch of the Zetan deserts. To the north, the faint grey of land nobody human had ever properly mapped.

"In thirty days, the Assembly sits with the Zetan delegation to renew the truce," Varga went on. "Fourteen months of peace, and they want another five years of it. So do I. I have buried enough cadets at the Kesh Ridge."

Juliette kept her face still. Varga could not know whose mother was buried at the Kesh Ridge without looking it up. Varga had certainly looked it up.

"The Zetans want three things," Varga said. "Recognition of the border. Trade in omicron research, which is why Observer Teyl is enjoying our hospitality. And water. Their deserts are dying faster than ours. They'll pay almost anything for it." She touched the map, and a blue line lit up, running from the southern basin west towards the desert. "There's a pipeline proposal on the table. It runs through the southern basin."

Juliette did not need to ask whose basin.

"Meanwhile," Varga said, "there are people painting *Psi is wide* on every overpass in the city. The Zetans can read, captain. They know what it means. It means humans who think the answer to rationing is to take the Zetans' land." She let the map fade. "Every Ashen still, every pamphlet, every unregistered wielder who runs from my soldiers, is a story the Zetan hardliners take home with them. So the Assembly wants the Ashen quiet before the summit. Which means I want Corran Vell. Alive, talking, and in a cell within ten days."

"Ma'am."

"Go to bed, Vasquez. You look like your mother did before a push."

Juliette was at the door before she understood what Varga had said. She did not turn around. She did not let her heart move. But somewhere, she knew, a neat little line had just risen, very slightly, and Varga had wanted to see it.

---

Romeo found her in the mess hall the next morning, which was unusual, because he never woke before he was ordered to.

He sat down opposite her with two mugs of the base's terrible tea and slid one across. Sergeant was not on his shoulder for once. That meant he wanted to talk properly.

"We found the water," he said, and told her. The rats. The stone steps by the dead tram stop. The blue door and the ship and the old woman handing out cans. The pamphlet, which he set on the table between them, folded small.

Juliette read it twice. *The stars were only the first road.*

"So the Ashen have the Wayfarers," she said.

"The Ashen have *some* Wayfarers." Romeo turned his mug in his hands. "Some of the old ones. The ones who always believed the Voyage wasn't finished. You give a believer clean water and a reason, and a lot of other things start to make sense to them."

She looked at him. He looked very tired, and very serious, and neither of those was his normal face.

"You told Dimitri about the cat," she said.

It wasn't a question. He didn't treat it like one. "He told me about his mother. Seemed fair."

"You've never told anyone about the cat."

"I told you."

"I was there."

"Exactly. So you don't count." A ghost of his usual grin. "He's all right, Jules. He's soft, but it's not a stupid kind of soft. It's the kind that holds."

She didn't answer that. She drank her tea, which was as bad as ever, and looked out of the mess hall window at the parade ground where a squad of first-years were being shouted at in the cold.

"I'm not asking about anything," Romeo said quietly.

She put the mug down.

"I just want you to know that I'm not asking," he said. "That's all. There's nothing you need to tell me. There never will be. That's the whole message."

They had shared a womb, a barracks, a grief, and eighteen years of each other's bad moods. She knew every one of his faces. This was one she had seen only a handful of times, and never when he was lying.

She had never told him about Varra. She had told no one about Varra.

Somehow, he knew anyway. And he was telling her he would carry it without making her pick it up.

Juliette found she could not say anything at all. So she reached across the table and put her hand over his for a second, the way they used to when they were nine and their mother was at the front and the nights were very long. Then she took it back and finished her tea.

"Susan's back tonight," she said.

"I know." Romeo stood, and picked up the pamphlet, and tucked it inside his jacket. "Something tells me she's not bringing good news."

---

Susan came back at nine, in her grey, with her hair pinned exactly right and her face like a closed door.

They met in the squad room with the lock turned. Susan stood at the window with her back to them and told them in a level voice, as if she were reading an inventory. Her brother had run the southern basin since the spring. Her father had renewed the water license that same season, quietly, for twenty years, at the same moment he had let the EDF take her. Her grandmother had said: *he paid with you.*

Nobody spoke for a while after she finished.

"I'm sorry," Dimitri said eventually. He said it simply, the way you'd say it to someone at a funeral, and Susan's shoulders moved very slightly, as if something had touched them.

"Don't be." She turned around. "I've had three days to be sorry. I'm finished." She looked at Juliette. "You said three days and then you didn't care whose name was on it. Put his name in. Matteo de Ricci, director of southern basin operations. I'll sign it myself."

Juliette had known Susan for three weeks. In that time she had thought of her as many things. Polished. Fragile. The girl who had read all the manuals. Somebody whose family had bought her a soft place in a hard world.

She had not, until that moment, thought of her as brave.

"We don't have proof it's him," Juliette said. "Only that he's in charge."

"Then let Varga find the proof. That's her job." Susan's voice did not shake. "I'm not protecting him. If I protect him, I'm my father."

---

Varga read the report in silence.

All of it. The losses booked as evaporation. The stills. The chapel on the stone steps. The pamphlet. The name at the bottom of the page, and beneath it, two signatures: *J. Vasquez, Captain.* And under that, smaller and very neat, *S. de Ricci, Cadet.*

When she was done she sat back and looked at Juliette for a long time. Then she looked at Susan, who was standing at attention a pace behind, very straight, eyes fixed on the wall.

"No," Varga said.

Juliette felt it like cold water. "Ma'am?"

"No." The commander closed the file. "Not now. Matteo de Ricci is the operating director of the only water basin on this continent large enough to supply a Zetan pipeline. The de Ricci family is hosting the Assembly's reception for the Zetan delegation in twenty-eight days. If I arrest the heir to that house for supplying the Ashen, the pipeline is dead, the reception is a scandal, and the Zetan hardliners go home and tell their people that the humans are so rotten they can't keep their water clean. And the war comes back."

"So we let him do it," Susan said.

She hadn't been given permission to speak. Varga didn't seem to notice.

"So we wait," Varga said. "Twenty-eight days. You bring me Corran Vell. Through Vell, I get the people above him. And when the truce is signed, I will take your brother apart in front of the whole Assembly if that's what the evidence says. You have my word."

Susan said nothing. Her face did not move at all. Juliette, standing beside her, could feel the effort it was taking, like heat off a stove.

"This report stays in my drawer," Varga said. "Not in the log. Not in the record. Not in the Choir." She looked at Juliette, and something in her expression was almost apologetic, and that was the most frightening thing of all. "I'm sorry, captain. I know what I'm asking you to carry."

"Ma'am," Juliette said, because there was nothing else to say.

In the corridor outside, Susan walked three steps and then stopped and put her hand flat against the wall and stood there, breathing.

Juliette waited.

"I was going to be brave," Susan said to the wall. "I'd decided. I'd done it. And it doesn't count."

"It counts," Juliette said. "It counted to me."

Susan looked at her. Then she took her hand off the wall and straightened her jacket and walked on, and by the time they reached the stairs her face was a closed door again.

Juliette added it to the list. The fire. Varra. Varga's first instruction. Susan's three days. Now this: a report that existed, and didn't.

She was beginning to wonder how much of the EDF was made of things that weren't written down.

---

They set up on the chapel two nights later.

Romeo and Susan took the roof of the old tram shelter, at the bottom of the stone steps. Juliette and Dimitri took the laundry, the one Romeo had found, with its view straight down the alley to the blue door and the painted ship.

It was cold. Juliette lay flat on the roof with her chin on her folded arms, and Dimitri lay beside her, close enough for warmth and not one inch closer, and they watched the door.

For two hours, nothing happened. The line of people came, took their cans, took their pamphlets, and went. The old woman in the blue shawl handed them out one by one. In the back of the room, people sang.

Somewhere in the second hour Juliette realised Dimitri was humming.

Very quietly. Barely a breath. Five notes up, five notes down, in time with the voices from the chapel. She didn't think he knew he was doing it.

"What's that?" she said.

He stopped at once. "Sorry."

"I didn't say stop."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he told her. The kitchen. The bath. The edge of his bed when he couldn't sleep, and his mother's hand in his hair. He told it plainly, without any decoration, and when he got to the end he didn't say what had happened to her. He didn't need to. Juliette had read his file.

"I didn't know it was a hymn," he said. "I thought it was just hers."

Juliette watched the blue door. Down in the alley, a woman came out with a child on her hip and a can in her free hand, and the child was asleep with its mouth open against her shoulder.

"My mother didn't sing," she said.

She hadn't meant to say anything. It was just there.

"She taught us to strip a rifle when we were six. Blindfolded. We had races. Romeo always cheated and I always won anyway." She was surprised to find herself almost smiling. "That was her version of a lullaby. Clean the weapon, check the weapon, put the weapon by the bed. Then sleep."

"Did it work?"

"I still do it."

He turned his head on his arms and looked at her. She could feel it without looking back. She kept her eyes on the door. In the dark, the line of his shoulder was very close to hers, and she was aware of it in a way she was not prepared to examine on a stakeout, or possibly ever.

*He's soft,* Romeo had said. *But it's the kind that holds.*

"Juliette," Dimitri said.

And then he said it again, differently, because he was looking past her, down into the alley.

A figure was coming along the street from the far end. Young. Sleeves rolled up despite the cold. Two big water cans, one in each hand, carried easily, as if they weighed nothing. And around his wrists, glinting in the light from the chapel door, a faint shimmer, like heat haze. Like water held just above the skin.

Corran Vell.

Juliette's whole body went still and ready, the way it always did, the way it had in Varra. Her hand found her knife. In the back of her head the Choir hummed, and she sent one word down the squad channel, clean and silent: *Hold.*

On the tram shelter roof, she knew without seeing, Romeo and Susan had just gone flat.

Corran reached the bottom of the steps. The old woman in the blue shawl came out to meet him. They spoke briefly. She touched his face with one hand, the way a grandmother would. He set down the cans and the boy from the other night, the one who had carried two cans nearly as big as himself, ran up and threw his arms around Corran's waist.

Corran laughed and ruffled his hair.

Then, with the boy still clinging to him, he lifted his head and looked straight up the alley. Past the bakery. Past the dead streetlight. To the roof of the laundry, and the exact spot where Juliette and Dimitri lay in the dark.

He didn't look surprised. He didn't run.

He just looked at them for a long moment, and then he nodded once, politely, the way you'd greet a neighbour, and picked up his cans, and went inside.

Dimitri let out a breath beside her.

"He knew," he whispered. "He knew we were here."

Juliette didn't answer. She was thinking about Varga's office, and a stakeout that existed only in a drawer, and how many people could possibly have known which roof she would be lying on tonight.

Not many.

She could count them on one hand.
