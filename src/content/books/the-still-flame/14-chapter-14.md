There were four hundred and twelve candles in the ballroom.

Susan knew, because she had watched the staff light them, one by one, all afternoon. Tall white tapers in silver branches along every wall. Little floating lights in glass bowls on every table. Three great chandeliers, each one a frozen fountain of crystal, lowered on chains so the footmen could reach them with long brass lighters, and then raised again into the purple dark of the wisteria ceiling, blazing.

She had watched Dimitri watch them too.

He was standing now by the long windows that opened onto the terrace, in a dark suit of Matteo's that her mother had had taken in that morning. It fitted him well. He looked, Susan thought, like a young man from a good family who had wandered into the wrong party and was trying very hard not to knock anything over. That was the idea. That was what Romeo had called the big young dog in him. It was a good disguise because it was mostly true.

What no one else in the room could see was that he was holding his breath.

Not literally. He was breathing. Slowly. In, and out, the way Juliette had taught him. But every time he moved, Susan saw the flames on the nearest candles lean, very slightly, towards him, the way flowers lean towards a window. Every time he caught himself, and breathed, they straightened. Over and over. For an hour now. Since the first guests arrived.

She crossed the room to him with a glass of water in each hand, smiling at everyone she passed, and put one into his hand.

"Drink this."

"I'm fine."

"You're sweating through Matteo's collar and the candelabra by the piano has been bowing to you like a courtier. Drink it."

He drank it. His hand was hot. She could feel it through the glass.

"Four hundred and twelve," he said quietly. "I counted."

"So did I."

"They all want something." His eyes went round the room, flame to flame to flame. "It's like standing in a room full of dogs, all looking at you, all waiting to be fed. And if I feed one..."

"Then don't," said Susan. "Stand by the window. Look at the stars. Breathe. If any of them leans too far, I'll knock it over and blame the peacock."

That got something that was almost a smile.

"Thank you," he said.

She left him by the window and went back into the crowd, and felt the weight of the ring on her finger as she went, heavy and new.

---

Everyone was there.

The Assembly had come in its finest, a hundred men and women in silk and velvet and the deep red sashes of office. The Speaker herself, small and silver-haired and sharp as a needle, holding court by the fireplace. Generals in dress uniform, glittering with medals. And Commander Varga, in plain EDF grey without a single decoration, standing very still near the musicians, looking like furniture, missing nothing.

*A heron*, Romeo had said. Susan understood exactly what he meant.

And the Zetans.

There were twenty of them now. The advance party, and the delegation behind it, and at its head Ambassador Sath: tall for a Zetan, old, with the plates along his jaw gone pale with age and painted in fine lines of gold, and eyes so dark they looked almost black. He moved through the room with a slow, unhurried grace, bowing from the neck, his clear second eyelids sliding across and back, across and back. Wherever he went, conversations went quiet for a moment, and then started again, a little too loudly.

Susan watched the humans around him. She watched them laugh too brightly and stand too straight and hold their glasses too tight. And she thought of Nonna at the table last night. *The Zetans hear. Not words. Hearts.*

She wondered how this room sounded to him. Four hundred and twelve candles, and two hundred human hearts, all beating a little too fast.

She made her own heart quiet, and went to find her father.

---

Aurelio de Ricci was standing at the foot of the grand staircase with Ambassador Sath, under the green curtains of ivy, and when he saw her coming his whole face changed.

He looked at her hand. At the ring.

For one moment he didn't say anything at all. Then he held out his arm, and she took it, and he turned to the Ambassador with a pride in his voice that she had not heard since she was very small.

"Ambassador," he said. "My daughter, Susan."

The Zetan looked at her. Close up, his eyes were not black. They were a very deep red, like wine held up to a candle.

Susan put her free hand open against her chest and bowed from the neck. *I show you I carry nothing.*

"Miss de Ricci." Sath's voice was deep and soft, with a faint rasp in it, like wind over sand. "Your father tells me you serve in the EDF. Unusual, I think, for a daughter of a chartered house."

"Very unusual, Ambassador."

"And you wear your father's seal." The dark red eyes rested on her ring for a moment. "But not, I notice, your father." He turned his head slightly towards Aurelio. "You have lost yours, my friend."

"On the terraces," said her father easily. "Last week. My wife says it's an omen."

The second eyelids slid across Sath's eyes. Slid back.

Susan stood beside her father with her hand on his arm, and she felt it. Felt it the way you feel a draught under a door. The Ambassador was listening. Not to what her father had said. To what was underneath it. To the blood moving in his wrist, under her hand, under the fine wool of his sleeve.

And she felt her father's pulse, too, steady and calm, a de Ricci heart that had never once raced at table.

"Omens," said Ambassador Sath, "are only what we choose to notice." He smiled, a slow, polite Zetan smile that showed no teeth. "I am glad to meet you, Miss de Ricci. I hope you will be at the signing. It is good for the young to see the old make peace. You will be the ones to keep it."

He bowed, and moved on, and Susan let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

Her father put his hand over hers on his arm, and squeezed it.

"Thank you," he said softly. "For wearing it."

She looked up at him. He was smiling down at her, with his tired, kind face and his grey hair and his bare right hand, and in that moment she would have given almost anything in the world to be eight years old again, and to believe him.

"Of course, Papa," she said.

---

She found Juliette by the musicians, standing very straight in a dress that was not the borrowed grey one.

It was dark green silk, very simple, cut close, with the black sling against it like a stroke of ink, and it had very clearly come out of Celestine de Ricci's own wardrobe. Somebody had done Juliette's hair, too, swept up and back, so that the long line of her neck showed and the thin white scar on her chin caught the candlelight.

She looked, Susan thought, like a blade someone had wrapped in a ribbon. Which was probably, knowing her mother, exactly the effect Celestine had been going for.

"My mother," Susan said.

"Your mother," Juliette agreed grimly. "She came to my room with three dresses and a maid and wouldn't leave until I'd chosen one. She said, and I quote, *a woman who is going to stand guard in my ballroom should at least look like she's been invited.*"

"She's right."

"She's terrifying."

"She's both. That's how de Riccis work." Susan hesitated. "You look beautiful."

Juliette looked at her sideways, as if checking whether she was joking. Then, almost unwillingly: "So do you." Her eyes dropped to Susan's hand. "You're wearing it."

"He asked me to."

"How did he look?"

Susan thought about it.

"Happy," she said.

Juliette didn't say anything. But she shifted half a step closer, so that their shoulders were nearly touching, and stood there beside her watching the room. Susan had grown up among people who said a great deal and meant very little. She was beginning to learn the language of people who said almost nothing at all.

Across the ballroom, by the long windows, Dimitri turned his head and looked at Juliette.

It was only a moment. He was looking out at the terrace and then he turned and saw her, in the green dress, under the candles, and his face did something entirely unguarded, and every single candle on the windowsill beside him leaned towards him at once, like a row of people gasping.

Then he caught it, and breathed, and they straightened.

Juliette had gone very still.

"Did you see that?" Susan murmured.

"See what?" Juliette said. Her voice was perfectly level. But under the silk, very faintly, Susan could see her pulse beating in the side of her throat. "Go and dance with my brother, Susan. He's been looking at you for twenty minutes, and if he doesn't do something with himself soon he's going to start talking to the peacock again."

---

The music changed. The musicians struck up the old *Terrazza*, the slow formal turning dance her grandmother had taught her when she was four.

Romeo was at her elbow before the first bar had finished. He bowed, deeply, ridiculously, a little too low, so that two Assembly members nearby laughed.

"Miss de Ricci."

"Cadet Vasquez."

"I've been practising," he said. "Left. All week. Ask Sergeant. He's sick of it."

She took his hand.

They went out onto the floor among the silk and the sashes, and the candles burned, and the wisteria hung purple overhead, and Romeo went left. Every single time. He went left and turned and stepped and changed hands, and he didn't look at his feet once. He looked at her. And she found, somewhere in the second turn, that her shoulders had gone loose and easy, and that she was smiling, properly, at a party in her mother's ballroom for the first time in her life.

"You're good at this," she said.

"I had a very strict teacher. She threatened to leave me on the ceiling."

"Did it work?"

"I'm here, aren't I?" He turned her under his arm and caught her on the other side, and his voice dropped. "Your father's watching us."

"I know."

"He looks..." Romeo paused, choosing the word. "Proud."

"I know."

"Do you want me to stop making you laugh? So it's easier?"

She looked up at him. At his ridiculous grin and his clever, kind, careful eyes.

"No," said Susan. "Don't you dare."

On the edge of the floor, she glimpsed Juliette, still standing by the musicians in her green dress. And Dimitri was walking towards her. Slowly. Through the crowd. With his hands, for once, doing something: one of them held out, palm up, as he reached her.

Juliette looked at the hand. Then at the sling. Then at him.

He said something too quiet for Susan to hear.

And Juliette Vasquez, who had told Susan she could not dance with a broken arm, put her good hand in his and let him lead her out onto the floor.

They didn't do the *Terrazza*. They couldn't, not with the sling. They just stood at the edge of it, close, and turned slowly, barely moving, his hand light at her waist and hers on his shoulder, while the whole formal dance swept and wheeled around them like a river around a stone.

Romeo had seen it too. Susan felt him miss half a step.

"Well," he said, under his breath. "There it goes."

"Are you all right?"

"Me?" He turned her again, perfectly, going left. "I'm wonderful. I've been waiting weeks for that. I've got a bet with Sergeant." Then, more quietly, almost to himself: "She looks happy, doesn't she."

Susan looked. Juliette's face, turned up towards Dimitri's, in the candlelight. Not hawk. Not captain. Just a girl at a dance.

"Yes," she said. "She does."

---

It was Nonna who noticed first.

Susan saw it from across the room. Her grandmother had been sitting in her high-backed chair by the conservatory doors all evening, in green, receiving guests like a small old queen. Now, suddenly, she had turned her head, and was looking out through the glass. Out past the conservatory. Past the dark terraces of the vineyard climbing up into the night.

Up to the very top of the hill.

Susan followed her gaze.

There was a light up there. At the very top of the estate, where the last terrace gave way to scrub and loose stones and the old ruined shrine. A small, steady orange point, like a lamp in a window. Except there were no windows up there. There was nothing up there but a broken wall and a hundred years of silence.

She felt Romeo's hand tighten on hers.

"The old vineyard," he said.

"Yes."

She turned. Her father was standing at the foot of the staircase again, talking to the Speaker of the Assembly, smiling, a glass in his hand. As she watched, he glanced towards the conservatory windows, the way a man does when he is checking the weather.

And she saw him see it.

He did not change expression. Not for a second. He went on smiling at the Speaker. He finished his sentence. He laughed at something she said. He set his glass down on a passing footman's tray, and touched the Speaker's arm, and excused himself, with a little bow, and walked unhurriedly across the hall towards the corridor that led to the kitchens and the back of the house and the stables beyond.

His pulse, Susan thought. If anyone in this room could hear it, what would it sound like now?

She looked round for Ambassador Sath. He was by the fireplace with the Speaker's husband. He had not moved. But his head was turned, very slightly, towards the corridor where her father had gone. And his second eyelids were closed. Both of them. Clear, and still.

Listening.

"Susan," Romeo said quietly. "Look at the candles."

She looked.

All of them. All four hundred and twelve. Along the walls, on the tables, in the great crystal chandeliers overhead. Every single flame in the room had begun, very slowly, to lean.

Not towards Dimitri. Not towards anyone in the room.

They were leaning towards the windows. Towards the dark. Towards the hill, and the orange light at the top of it, like four hundred and twelve faces turning to look at something that had just walked into the room.

Across the ballroom, Dimitri had stopped dancing. He was standing perfectly still with Juliette's hand still in his, staring out of the windows, and his face had gone grey.

The music faltered. A woman near the fireplace laughed nervously and said, "Is there a draught?" Somebody else said, "How odd."

Nonna Livia rose from her chair.

She was very small and very old and she did not raise her voice. But the whole room seemed to hear her anyway.

"Close the doors," said Livia de Ricci.

And every candle in the ballroom went out at once.
