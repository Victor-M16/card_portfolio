Commander Varga's office had a window, which was how everyone on base measured rank. It looked out over the parade ground, and Varga stood at it with her back to the door for a full minute after Juliette came in, as if the parade ground might do something interesting.

"Sit, Captain Vasquez."

Juliette sat.

Varga turned around and touched the desk, and a screen woke in the air above it. Two lines, side by side. Heart rates. Juliette knew before she read the labels.

The left line was flat and calm and boring, with one small rise near the end. *Vasquez, J. Report filing, 1612.* The right line was flat and calm and then, at one precise moment, climbed like a startled bird. *Gale, D. Review, 0814.*

"Your rise is very small," Varga said. "His is not. You should teach him how you do that."

Juliette said nothing. She had learned a long time ago that silence was the only answer nobody could use against you.

"I'm not going to ask you what happened on that second floor," Varga went on. "I don't need the truth from you, captain. I only need you to understand that I know the shape of it." She let the screen fade. "Corran Vell told your cadet that the Ashen expected someone like him with the soldiers. Someone like what, I wonder? And how would the Ashen know who was on a squad that was assigned that same morning?"

The cold went down Juliette's spine slowly, the way water finds a crack.

"A leak," she said.

"A leak. Somewhere between my desk and that street." Varga sat for the first time. "So. Your squad takes the stills. Find where Vell gets his water, find who he sells it to, find who he answers to. You report to me. Not to the duty log. Not to your division. Me."

"Ma'am."

"And captain." Varga's voice didn't change at all, which was what made it land. "Whatever your cadet is, keep it off my Choir until I tell you otherwise."

Juliette stood and saluted and walked out, and she was halfway down the stairs before she understood what had frightened her. Varga hadn't told her to stop lying. Varga had told her to keep doing it.

---

"So we're spies now," Romeo said.

"We're investigators."

"Spies with better posture."

They were in the squad room with the door locked: the four of them and Sergeant, who was perched on the back of a chair, eating something that had once been a biscuit. Dimitri sat with his hands clasped between his knees. He'd been doing that since the review, holding his own hands as if they might go somewhere without him.

"There's a second problem," Juliette said. "Dimitri can't control it. Next time it might not go out on its own. He needs training."

"Then train him," Susan said.

"They know he's strong. They don't know what he is, and we keep it that way. A human with fire like that? There's exactly one other name on file, and it's on every wanted board on Eta. Every time he uses it, the Choir logs a signature. One fire signature from this base and the lie in my report is a lie in the record, and then he's in a lab and I'm in a cell." She looked at Dimitri. "Sorry."

"No," he said quietly. "It's true."

"So we need a dead zone," Romeo said. "A quiet one. Somewhere nobody's jamming, so nobody's watching the jammer." He rubbed Sergeant's head with one knuckle and his eyes went distant the way they did when he was listening to something the rest of them couldn't hear. "The Undercroft."

Susan frowned. "The old drains under Fourth District? Those are sealed."

"Sealed to people. Not to crows." Romeo shrugged. "Sergeant goes down there sometimes. Says it's quiet. Not quiet like no noise. Quiet like the Choir. Nobody knows why. The stones down there are older than the city."

"How much older?"

"He's a crow, Susan. He doesn't do dates."

---

Susan asked to stay behind when the others left.

She waited until the door had closed and then she set a small silver slate on the table, the kind that only came out of de Ricci offices, with the family's vine-and-key seal worked into the casing. Her hands were steady. Her face was not.

"I've been looking for where Vell gets his water," she said. "He can draw some from the air, but not hundreds of litres. Not every night. It has to come from a main line."

"And the main lines in the southern basin are yours."

"My family's." She corrected it the way you'd brush a hair off your sleeve. "I still have access to the distribution data. My father never thought to take it away." She touched the slate and a map of the southern districts bloomed above it, laced with thin blue lines. Scattered along them were small amber marks. "Every pipe loses some water. Leaks, theft, evaporation. These are the losses the company records as evaporation."

Juliette looked. The amber marks weren't scattered at all. They sat in clusters, and the clusters sat in the poorest districts, and one of them sat directly on top of the dye works on Tannery Street.

"That's not theft," Juliette said slowly. "Thieves don't get their losses written up as weather."

"No." Susan swallowed. "Somebody inside the company is selling water to the Ashen. Or giving it to them. And booking it so nobody looks."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Juliette thought about the river turning in the air over Corran's head, forty families' worth of clean water. About taps that ran four hours a day. About the de Riccis, who owned the basin, and whose daughter had been pulled into the EDF over her family's objections and their very old charter.

"I have to report this," she said.

"I know." Susan's voice was very even. "Give me three days."

"Susan."

"Three days. If my father is part of this, I want to know before Varga does. If he isn't, I want to know who is before they have time to make it look like he was." She held Juliette's eyes, and for the first time Juliette saw past the posture and the manuals and the polish, to the thing underneath, which was a girl who was terrified and was not going to let anyone see it. "You'd do it for Romeo."

Juliette would. She would do far worse for Romeo.

"Three days," she said. "And then I don't care whose name is on it."

Susan nodded once, picked up the slate, and left. Juliette sat alone in the squad room and added it to the list in her head. The fire on Dimitri's arm. Varra. Varga's instruction. Now this.

She was a captain with very few things she was willing to write down.

---

The Undercroft smelled of wet stone and something older, something dry and mineral underneath, like the inside of a mountain.

They went down through a maintenance hatch Romeo had found by following Sergeant, and then down a long sloped tunnel with the city's pipes sweating overhead, and then, with no warning at all, the Choir went silent.

Juliette was ready for it this time. It still felt like stepping off a ledge.

Behind her, Dimitri let out a long breath. When she glanced back, his face had changed. It took her a moment to place it. He looked relieved.

"You like it," she said.

"I don't know." He looked around at the dark. "It's the first time nobody's watching."

Romeo and Sergeant stayed at the tunnel mouth to keep watch. Susan had gone home to her family's house for the night, to start pulling on threads she wouldn't name. So it was just the two of them who went on into the chamber at the bottom, a round, high space with a floor worn smooth by centuries of water that no longer ran.

Juliette set her lamp on a ledge and turned to him.

"Breathe," she said.

"That's it? That's the training?"

"That's where all of it starts. Every wielder, every element. You breathe, and you find it, and you ask." She drew a slow breath and let the air in the chamber gather around her, a small cool turning at her fingertips. It came easily. It always had. "Air wants to move. I just give it somewhere to go."

Dimitri closed his eyes. She watched him try. He had a habit of frowning when he concentrated, a small crease between his eyebrows, as if every problem were a math test he hadn't studied for. She found she didn't mind watching it.

Nothing happened. Then something did.

The lamp flame on the ledge stretched. Not far. Just a finger's length, a thin bright tongue leaning towards him the way it had on Tannery Street. Dimitri's eyes opened. The flame jumped from the lamp to the air between them and hung there, the size of a fist, alive and turning.

"Good," Juliette said softly. "Now hold it."

"I am."

"You're not. It's holding you."

She saw it before he did: the fire breathing in, swelling, the light going from gold to white at its heart. It was reaching. Not for him. For everything. For the air in the room, for her, for anything that would burn.

"Juliette..."

She pulled.

Not at the fire. At the air around it. She drew it away the way she had drawn it away from Corran's wave, and the flame was suddenly sitting in nothing, in a pocket of empty space with nothing to eat. It shrank. It guttered. It went out with a sound like a sigh.

They stood in the half-dark with the lamp barely glowing between them.

"You can do that," Dimitri said. He sounded almost frightened. "You can just... take it away."

"Fire needs air." She hadn't known it would work until it did. Her heart was going harder than it had in Varga's office. "I'm air."

He looked at her for a long moment, in a way she wasn't ready to name, and she looked back for a moment longer than a captain should have.

"Again," she said. "Smaller."

They went again. And again. By the tenth time he could hold a flame the size of a coin for the length of three breaths before it started to reach, and she could feel the exact instant it began to want more, a hunger like a pulled thread. By the twentieth time he was soaked in sweat and grinning like a child, and so, she realised, was she.

On the twenty-first, he lost it.

It went up, not out. A column of fire rose to the roof of the chamber, and for one blinding second the whole room was lit gold. Juliette pulled hard and the column collapsed into sparks and smoke and was gone.

But in that second, she had seen the walls.

"Dimitri," she said. "Bring the lamp."

He brought it, his hands shaking. She held it up to the stone.

The walls were carved. All of them, all the way round, under a skin of dust and mineral crust. The carvings were worn almost smooth, but in the low, slanting light they rose out of the stone like faces out of water.

Ships. Long, strange ships coming down out of a sky full of stars. People spilling out of them, small, human, carrying bundles and children.

And waiting for them on the ground, the carving made clear, were others. Figures far taller than the humans. Many times taller. Standing in a long line with their hands raised, palms out.

In every one of those raised hands, someone had carved a flame.

"Eta was empty," Dimitri said slowly. "That's what they teach us. The Migration found an empty land."

Juliette looked at the tall figures, at the humans kneeling before them in the dust of a million years.

"Then somebody," she said, "has been lying for a very long time."
