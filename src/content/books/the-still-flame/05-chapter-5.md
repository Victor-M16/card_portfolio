For two days after the Undercroft, Dimitri practised fire without fire.

It was the only kind of practice the Choir couldn't see. He lay on his bunk at night with his eyes closed and breathed the way Juliette had taught him, and found the warm place under his ribs where the fire lived, and did not ask it for anything. He just sat with it. Learned its edges. Felt it turn over in its sleep like something that had been dreaming for a very long time and was starting, slowly, to wake.

Sometimes, when he did this, he thought about the walls. The ships. The tall figures with their hands raised.

More often, he thought about the moment the fire had swelled white in the dark and Juliette had simply taken the air away, and it had died in front of him like a candle pinched out. *Fire needs air. I'm air.* He had replayed it so many times that the memory had started to wear smooth, like the carvings, and he was no longer certain which parts he'd seen and which parts he'd added.

He did not think about why he kept replaying it. That seemed safest.

---

They had told Romeo about the carvings, of course. Romeo had listened, and fed Sergeant a strip of dried meat, and said, "People draw giants everywhere. My mother had a tattoo of a giant. Doesn't mean she met one."

"Your mother had a tattoo of a giant?"

"It was a very bad tattoo. It might have been a tree."

Juliette had decided they would say nothing until they understood what they'd seen. Nobody would believe three cadets who'd been somewhere they weren't allowed, describing pictures nobody else had seen, in a place the Choir couldn't verify. And if anyone did believe them, that would probably be worse.

So the carvings sat. Somewhere under Fourth District, in the dark, the tall figures kept their hands raised over the kneeling humans, and above them the city went on teaching its children about an empty shore.

---

On the second night of Susan's three days, Juliette was called to Varga's office, and Romeo appeared at Dimitri's door in a black jacket with Sergeant on his shoulder.

"Get your coat, firestarter. We're going to talk to some rats."

They went out through the motor pool and over the back wall, which Romeo assured him everyone did and nobody logged, because the Choir knew exactly where they were and simply didn't care until someone told it to. The city was cold and clear. In the Fourth District the streetlights ran on rationed power and half of them were dark, so the stars showed through between the rooftops, more of them than Dimitri was used to.

Romeo took him up a fire escape onto the flat roof of a laundry and crouched at the edge, looking down into an alley that smelled of soap and rot.

"Susan's map says there's a cluster of water losses round here," he said. "Which means a still. Which means people carrying water away from it at night, in cans and buckets and whatever else. Which means wet footprints, and spills, and puddles where there shouldn't be puddles." He whistled very softly through his teeth. "And rats can smell clean water from three streets away. It's the most interesting thing that ever happens to them."

They came. First one, then four, then a dozen, slipping out of drains and gaps in the brickwork, grey shapes in the dark that sat up on their hind legs and looked at Romeo with their small bright eyes. Dimitri had seen him do it on Tannery Street, but that had been one rat in a hurry. This was different. This was Romeo kneeling on a rooftop with his hand held out and a dozen animals going still around him, like an audience before a play.

He touched one on the nose. Then the next. Then the next. His eyes half-closed.

"Go on," he murmured. "Find it for me, sweethearts. Find the clean water."

The rats scattered.

Dimitri sat beside him on the cold roof and waited. Sergeant hopped from Romeo's shoulder to the parapet and walked up and down it with the air of a supervisor.

"What's it like?" Dimitri asked. "When you do that."

Romeo was quiet for a moment. It was the first time Dimitri had ever heard him not have an answer ready.

"Like being very slightly drunk," he said eventually, "in a language you don't speak. With rats, it's small. They want food. They're scared of cats. They're thinking about the drain they were born in. You borrow a bit of that, and you lend them a bit of you, and for a while you're both a little bit something else." He shrugged. "With a crow, it's bigger. Crows have opinions."

On the parapet, Sergeant clicked.

"See?"

---

The rats came back in ones and twos over the next hour, and each one Romeo touched, and each one made the picture in his head a little clearer. He narrated it under his breath. Left at the bakery. Down the stairs by the old tram stop. A door with a blue sign. Lots of feet. Lots of water. Singing.

"Singing?"

"That's what the rat says. I'm just the translator."

They went down off the roof and followed it through the dark streets, keeping to the shadows. Left at a shuttered bakery. Down a flight of stone steps beside a tram stop that had not seen a tram in thirty years. And at the bottom, set into the wall of an old warehouse, a narrow door with a hand-painted blue sign above it: a simple picture of a ship rising, and beneath it, in careful letters, *THE LONG VOYAGE.*

A Wayfarer chapel.

The door was propped open. Warm yellow light spilled out onto the wet stones. Inside, Dimitri could see a long low room with benches pushed against the walls, and in the middle of it, rows and rows of water cans. Clean, sealed, glinting. A line of people shuffled past them: old men, women with children on their hips, a boy no older than ten carrying two cans nearly as big as he was. At the end of the line, an old woman in a faded blue shawl handed out the cans one by one, and with each can she pressed a folded piece of paper into the person's hand and said something too quiet to hear.

Nobody paid. Nobody was asked to.

And in the back of the room, a handful of people were singing.

Dimitri stopped walking.

It was a simple tune. Five notes going up, five notes coming down, the shape of something lifting off and returning. He had not heard it in nine years, but he knew it the way he knew his own hands. His mother had sung it in the kitchen while she cooked. In the bath when he was small. Once, sitting on the edge of his bed when he couldn't sleep, her hand on his hair.

*We came from the stars and the stars are still waiting,* *The road is behind us, the road is ahead.*

He had not known it was a hymn. He had thought it was just a song she knew.

"Dimitri." Romeo's hand was on his arm. "You all right?"

"My mother sang that," he said. His voice sounded strange to him.

Romeo looked at him, and then at the chapel, and then back at him. He didn't say anything clever. He just kept his hand where it was.

They stood in the shadow of the tram stop and watched the line move. The old woman. The cans. The folded papers.

"That's Ashen water," Romeo said quietly. "From the de Ricci mains, through Corran's stills, and out through the Wayfarers to anyone who needs it. Free."

"So we report it."

"Do we?" Romeo nodded at the boy with the two cans, staggering up the steps past them into the dark. "We report it, Varga sends a squad, they shut down the chapel and arrest the old lady. And tomorrow that kid's family drinks four hours of tap water and whatever falls out of the sky." He let go of Dimitri's arm. "I'm not saying don't. I'm saying when we do, we should at least have the decency to know what we're doing."

Dimitri had never heard him say anything like that. He wasn't sure Romeo had ever said anything like that in his life.

---

They went back up onto the laundry roof afterwards. Neither of them suggested it. They just went, and sat with their backs against the cold brick of the chimney, and looked at the stars over the Fourth District while Sergeant dozed with his head tucked under his wing.

"Can I ask you something?" Dimitri said.

"You can ask."

"Why rats? You could have had anything. You said it yourself on Tannery Street. You could've had a hill-cat."

Romeo laughed, but there wasn't much in it.

"I had one," he said. "Once."

Dimitri waited.

"We grew up on bases," Romeo said. "Me and Julie. Our mother was a field officer. Wherever she went, we went. When we were nine she got posted to the front, out past the Kesh Ridge, where the war was. Barracks kids aren't supposed to go near the line, but nobody tells barracks kids anything, so we did." He picked at a thread on his sleeve. "There was a hill-cat in the scrub behind the camp. Native. Big as a man. Grey and gold, with eyes like lamps. It had been hit by something, shrapnel maybe, and it was lying in a gully dying, and I went down to it."

"You were nine."

"I was nine and stupid. I thought I could help." He was quiet for a moment. "I touched it. Just its paw. And it let me in."

Sergeant shifted in his sleep. Somewhere far off, a tram bell rang on a line that still worked.

"It wasn't like rats," Romeo said. "It wasn't like anything. It was huge. It was hungry and furious and completely, completely free. It had never been told what to do in its life. It didn't know what a wall was. It was dying, and it wasn't afraid of dying. It was just angry it couldn't hunt anymore." He tipped his head back against the brick. "And for about ten seconds, I wasn't a nine-year-old barracks kid. I was that. I was all of that. And I liked it so much I didn't want to come back."

"What happened?"

"Julie pulled me out of the gully by my collar. Punched me in the face, actually. I was growling at her. On all fours." A faint smile. "Cat died that night. Our mother died three weeks later, at the Ridge. And I decided I'd stick to things that wanted to be fed."

Dimitri didn't know what to say. So he said nothing, which seemed to be the right thing, because after a while Romeo went on.

"Julie became a soldier the day Mom died. Like a switch. Nine years old and she started making her bed with corners. Somebody had to make her laugh, so." He spread his hands. "Here I am. Hilarious."

"You're not just that."

"No." Romeo looked at him sideways. "But it's the bit people like."

The wind moved across the roof. Dimitri thought about the hill-cat. About wanting something so much you didn't want to come back.

"How long do you have left?" he asked. "Your service."

"Seven years." Romeo said it lightly, but his jaw tightened. "Fauna strong enough to clear threshold, they want a full term. Then I get my license, and some company pays me a fortune to keep pests out of their grain stores, and I spend the rest of my life talking to rats in a warehouse." He laughed. "Or I go out to the Reach, and find out what I am. One or the other."

"Which one?"

"Ask me in seven years."

He was quiet for a while after that. Then, without looking at Dimitri, he said:

"I'm not going to tell you to stay away from my sister."

Dimitri felt his face go hot in the dark. "I wasn't..."

"I've got a crow, Dimitri. I know what people's faces do." Romeo still didn't look at him. "I'm not telling you to stay away. She can make her own choices; she's better at it than I am. I'm just telling you she's had enough people leave. That's all."

There was something underneath it. Dimitri could hear it, the way you could hear a second voice in a room next door. Something Romeo knew and wasn't saying.

He didn't ask. It seemed like the kind of thing you had to be told.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay," Romeo said, and that was the end of it.

---

Sergeant woke a little before dawn, stretched his wings, and dropped off the edge of the roof without a sound. He came back a few minutes later and landed on Romeo's knee with something white in his beak.

One of the folded papers from the chapel.

Romeo opened it and held it up to the grey light so they could both read it. It was printed cheaply on thin paper, and at the top was the same picture as the sign over the door: a ship, rising.

*The stars were only the first road,* it said. *Our ancestors crossed the dark to find a new world. They did not stop at the shore. Neither should we.*

And underneath, in smaller letters, like a signature:

*Psi is wide.*

They looked at it together for a long time. Then Romeo folded it carefully and put it in his jacket, and they climbed down off the roof and walked back through the waking city towards the base, not talking.

Somewhere along the way, Dimitri realised he was humming. Five notes up, five notes down. He stopped. Then, after a few streets, without quite meaning to, he started again.
