Her arm was screaming.

Juliette registered it the way she registered everything: as information, filed and set aside. Forearm, cracked. Wrist, swollen. A long climb at a run, up a hill of loose stones in a silk dress, holding the bad arm against her body with the good one. Pain, considerable. Useful, none. Later.

She had already counted the ruin. Romeo, whole. Susan, whole, her dress torn to the knee and her face like chalk. Aurelio de Ricci, standing by the cold ring of stones where a fire had been, with his hand over his mouth. A grey mare, tied to the old wall, shivering. A handprint burned into the stone beside the archway, still faintly orange at the edges, the smell of hot rock coming off it like breath.

And Dimitri, in the archway, with his hands in his pockets, looking at a man who had just told him he had his mother's eyes.

Nobody had moved for what felt like a very long time. The wind came up over the top of the hill, cold, and moved the loose ends of everyone's hair and clothes, and Juliette let it. She didn't touch it. She didn't need to. She wanted to hear what it carried.

It carried four hearts, beating hard. And a fifth, Aurelio's, beating harder than all of them.

"You knew my mother," Dimitri said.

It was not a question. His voice was very quiet and very steady. She had heard him use that voice once before, in a cellar under the dye works, telling her that she was not empty.

Aurelio lowered his hand.

"Yes," he said.

"How?"

The old man looked round the ruin. At his daughter. At Romeo. At Juliette, last, as if checking what kind of soldier she was. Then back at Dimitri. And something in him seemed to give way, the way a dam gives way: not all at once, but with a long slow groan, and then a crack.

"She worked here," he said. "In the kitchens. Nineteen years ago. She was seventeen." His voice was rough. "Mira Gale. From the Fourth District. My mother hired her because she sang while she worked, and my mother said a kitchen that sings makes better bread."

Susan made a small sound.

"Nonna," she said. "Nonna hired her."

"Your grandmother hired her. And I..." Aurelio stopped. Started again. "I was twenty-eight. Married, with a little boy at home. Running the basin for my father. And there was an EDF officer assigned to the estate that year. The Assembly had been worried about water riots in the south, and they sent a garrison. A captain. Young. Brilliant. The strongest fire wielder anyone had seen in a century." He looked at the handprint on the wall. "We became friends. He and I. I'd never had a friend who wasn't born into a chartered house. He used to come up here at night to practise, where nobody would see. I used to come up with a bottle and watch."

The wind moved. The handprint on the wall ticked as it cooled.

"And he met your mother," Aurelio said to Dimitri. "Here. On this hill. She used to come up here too, after the kitchens closed, to sing to the old shrine. She was Wayfarer, but she said the Sown shrine had the best echo on the estate." A faint, broken smile. "He heard her singing one night, and came up the hill to find out who it was. And that was that."

Dimitri had not moved at all. Juliette, standing a pace behind him and to the side, could see his hands in his pockets. They had stopped shaking. That frightened her more than the shaking had.

"What happened?" he said.

"He changed." Aurelio's voice dropped. "Not all at once. Over that year. He started asking questions the EDF didn't like. About the Assembly. About rationing. About why the maps had blank corners. He started talking about the Spine. About the people up there, and what they could do, and what they knew that we didn't. He said the Choir was a cage we'd built around ourselves because we were afraid of a wide world." He shook his head slowly. "I thought it was talk. Young men talk. Then one night he burned his seed out. Here. On this hill. With his own fire. I found him the next morning lying by that wall with the skin behind his ear still smoking, laughing like a madman. He said it was the first time in his life he'd heard himself think."

"A hollow," Romeo said quietly.

"The first I ever met. Not the last." Aurelio looked at the ground. "He deserted that week. Walked out of the garrison and into the Reach with nothing but the clothes on his back. Heading west. North. I never knew where. And Mira was..."

He stopped.

Juliette saw Dimitri's throat move as he swallowed.

"She was carrying me," Dimitri said.

"Yes."

---

The silence after that went on so long that the mare by the wall shifted her feet and blew through her nose, and the sound made everyone flinch.

Juliette made herself breathe. She kept her eyes on Aurelio, because watching Dimitri's face was too much like watching someone stand in a burning building, and she was a soldier, and her job right now was to watch the man who was talking, and be ready.

"She was so frightened," Aurelio said. "Not of being alone. Of him. Of what he might come back and want. She'd seen what his fire could do. She'd seen how much it had changed him. She didn't want her child anywhere near it." He spread his bare hands. "I had money. I had a city full of rooms nobody asked about. I sent her to Haven. To the Fourth District, near her people, near the old Wayfarer chapel. I paid for a room above the laundry and I paid the laundry to hire her, and I paid Sister Orla to keep an eye on her and say nothing, and I told my wife it was a charity for the Wayfarers." A hollow laugh. "Celestine has never once believed me. She's never once asked."

"And him?" Juliette said.

It was the first thing she'd said. Aurelio's eyes came to her.

"He came back," he said. "Nine years later. Out of nowhere. One night I came up this hill to be alone, and he was sitting by that wall, waiting for me. Older. Harder. His hands..." He glanced at the handprint. "He'd been up in the Spine. He wouldn't say much about it. He said they'd taught him things. That he could do things with fire that no human had ever done." His voice shook. "And he'd heard. About Mira. About the fire in the Fourth District. Three weeks before."

Dimitri's breath went out of him in one long, ragged rush.

Juliette didn't think. She stepped forward, beside him, close, so her good shoulder was against his arm. He didn't look at her. But she felt his weight shift, just slightly, towards her. Like someone leaning on a wall.

"Did he do it?" Dimitri said. His voice cracked in half. "The fire. Did he..."

"No." Aurelio said it instantly, fiercely. "No. I asked him. Here. That night. I put the question to him exactly as you just did, and he put his hand flat on that wall," he pointed, not at the fresh print by the archway, but at another place, further along, where Juliette now saw a second mark, old and black and weathered almost smooth, "and he swore on the shrine and on her name that he had not set it, and had not known, and had been five hundred miles away in the snow when it happened." He was quiet for a moment. "The stone went black under his hand. I have never seen a man's grief burn a wall before."

Nobody spoke.

"He wanted you," Aurelio said to Dimitri, very gently. "That night. He wanted to go down to Haven and find you and take you with him. And I begged him not to. I said: she died to keep him away from this. Let him have the life she wanted for him. A quiet one. In a city. With a Choir, and a school, and no fire." He swallowed. "And he listened. I don't know why. Perhaps because I was his friend once. Perhaps because he was tired. We made a bargain, here, on this hill. He would never come near you. Never. And in return I would tell him how you were, every season. Whether you were well. Whether you were happy."

"And the water," Susan said.

Her voice was very cold and very clear. Aurelio turned to look at her.

"The water," he said. "Yes. In time. He asked for it, for his people. For the Fourth District, and the others like it. And I gave it. Because the taps ran four hours a day in places where I had sent a frightened girl to live. And because every can that went out through Orla's door was one more family that didn't need to burn something down to be heard." He looked at his daughter, and his face was naked. "I told myself it was a kind of peace. That I was buying peace, the only way a de Ricci knows how."

"And tonight?" Susan said. "Was tonight peace, Papa?"

Aurelio didn't answer.

---

Juliette was the one who heard it first.

Not with her ears. With the wind. It was coming up the hill from the house, and it was carrying things. Boots on stone. Many of them. Metal, clinking. Lamps swinging. And, very faint, under all of it, the hum of something she had felt every day of her life and had not felt on this hilltop all night.

The Choir.

Not in her head. On the slope below. Moving. A lot of seeded people climbing fast, and the network moving with them, a bright net drawn up the hill.

"Soldiers," she said. "EDF. Twenty, maybe more. Coming up the bridle path." She looked at Aurelio. "You have about three minutes, sir."

"To do what?"

"Decide what you're going to tell them."

Aurelio looked at her for a long moment. Then, unbelievably, he smiled. A tired, rueful, wrecked smile.

"You sound exactly like her," he said. "Your commander. She used to say things like that."

Juliette went still. "You know Commander Varga?"

"Everyone who was here that year knows Ilse Varga." Aurelio looked past her, down the dark slope, at the lamps coming up through the vines. "She was his lieutenant."

The ground seemed to tilt under Juliette's feet.

She thought of Varga in her office, looking out over the parade ground. *I don't need the truth from you, captain. I only need you to understand that I know the shape of it.* She thought of Varga in the review room, looking at Dimitri a moment longer than she needed to. *Whatever your cadet is, keep it off my Choir until I tell you otherwise.*

*Keep it off my Choir.*

She had thought it was ambition. Or caution. Or politics.

She had never once thought it might be grief.

"Dimitri," she said, low. "Hands. Pockets. Whatever happens."

He nodded. He hadn't taken them out all night.

---

The lamps came over the top of the last terrace and into the ruin.

Twenty soldiers. EDF grey. Rifles slung, sidearms holstered, every one of them seeded and humming, the Choir pouring back over the hilltop like light into a dark room. Juliette felt it hit her: time, vitals, map, the soft green certainty of who she was. She felt it hit the others too. Romeo blinked. Susan's shoulders went back. Dimitri flinched, very slightly, like a man stepping out of shadow into sun.

And behind the soldiers, walking up the last of the slope alone, without a lamp, came Commander Ilse Varga.

She did not look at Juliette. She did not look at any of them. She did not look at Aurelio de Ricci, or at the grey mare, or at the empty ring of cold stones where a fire had been.

She walked straight across the ruin to the archway, and stopped in front of the wall, and looked at the handprint.

The new one. The one still ticking as it cooled, faintly orange at the edges, the smell of hot stone rising off it into the cold night.

She stood there a long time. Her soldiers waited, not understanding. The lamps hissed. The wind came up over the hill.

Then Varga raised her hand. Slowly. Her own hand, narrow and pale and lined, and laid it flat against the stone, inside the burned print. It was smaller. It fitted inside the black outline with room to spare, like a child's hand pressed inside a grown man's.

Juliette was close enough to see her face. It was the face she always wore. Still. Closed. A heron in the shallows.

Except for her mouth.

Varga's lips moved. Very slightly. No sound came out. She was not sending it; the Choir would have caught that. She was simply saying it, alone, to a warm stone wall, in the dark, where she thought no one could hear.

But the wind could hear. And Juliette was the wind.

She caught it. One word. A name.

*Idris.*
