The Choir went silent at the corner of Ninth and Tannery, exactly where it had the first time.

Dimitri had been ready for it. He'd been ready for it all day, the way you're ready for a cold wave at the beach. It didn't help. The quiet came down over him like a hand over his mouth, and for a second he was nine years old again, standing in a smoke-filled hallway, listening for a voice that didn't answer.

Then it passed, and he was eighteen, in the dark, with a knife on his hip and Juliette a step ahead of him, and that was better. Not much. But better.

They had spent the whole of the day before planning. Without the Choir there would be no sent messages, no shared positions, no little green markers. So they had gone back to the oldest ways. Romeo and Susan would take the alley behind the dye works and the canal side, where the drains came out. Juliette and Dimitri would go in through the roof. If anything went wrong, one long whistle meant *out*, and two short ones meant *help*, and three meant *run and don't come back for us*.

"Nobody's using three," Romeo had said.

"Everybody uses three eventually," Juliette had said, and he hadn't answered.

Now Romeo and Susan peeled off into the dark without a word, Sergeant a black shape on Romeo's shoulder, and Juliette touched Dimitri's arm once and pointed up.

---

The roof of the dye works was slate, slick with frost. They went across it on their bellies, slowly, to the broken skylight Romeo's rats had found, and looked down.

The second floor was dark now. The barrels were gone. The copper pipes along the walls were cold. Only the smell remained, steam and metal and old dye, and a single lamp burning on a crate in the middle of the room.

There were four people around it.

Corran, sleeves rolled up, standing with his back against a pillar and his arms folded. Two men Dimitri didn't recognise, rough-looking, the kind you'd see unloading boats at the canal. And a fourth figure, sitting on an upturned barrel with his back to the skylight, in a long dark coat of very good cloth.

He was speaking quietly. Dimitri couldn't make out the words. But when the man raised one hand to make a point, the lamplight caught something on his finger: a heavy signet ring, and on its face, worked in gold, a vine twisting around a key.

Beside Dimitri, Juliette went completely still.

He knew that seal. He had seen it on the silver slate Susan had set on the table in the squad room. On the gates of a house he had never been inside. On every bottle of the most expensive wine on Eta.

The man in the coat finished speaking. One of the dockworkers handed him something, a thick envelope, and he tucked it inside his coat and stood.

Then Corran looked up.

Not at the skylight. At the ceiling beside it, a little to the left, as if listening. His face changed. He said one word, sharp, and both dockworkers moved at once, and one of them pulled something from his belt that was not a knife.

It was a squat black canister with a fuse.

"Down," Juliette breathed, and grabbed Dimitri's collar, and they were dropping through the skylight before he'd understood what he was seeing.

---

It happened very fast after that, and he would never afterwards be sure of the order.

He remembered landing hard on the boards. He remembered the man in the good coat already gone, a door banging somewhere below. He remembered Juliette moving through the room like a blade through water, the air going with her, slamming one dockworker off his feet and into the wall.

He remembered Corran, not fighting. Standing by the pillar with his hands raised, water starting to gather around his wrists, looking not at Juliette but at the other dockworker. The one with the canister.

"Don't," Corran said. "*Don't.* Not in here, you fool, the air's too thick..."

The man lit the fuse.

Powder didn't burn right on Psi. Everybody knew that. It burned fast or slow or sideways, depending on the omicron count in the air. And the air in that room was full of it: an air wielder, a water wielder, and Dimitri, with fire waking up under his ribs like an animal hearing its name.

The fuse didn't burn down. It flashed. One instant it was a sputtering thread; the next, it was simply gone, and the canister was a bright hard white in the man's hand.

Dimitri felt the fire in it before it went. Felt it the way he'd felt the lamp in the Undercroft, reaching for him. And without thinking, without asking, he reached back.

He could not stop it. It was too fast, too big. But for one fraction of a second, he *bent* it.

The blast went down instead of out.

The floor disappeared.

---

He woke in the dark, on his back, on something wet and cold, with his ears ringing like a bell that had been struck and wouldn't stop.

For a moment he didn't know where he was. Then he breathed in and tasted stone dust and old dye and smoke, and it all came back.

"Juliette," he said. It came out as a croak. "*Juliette.*"

"Here." Her voice was close, and hoarse, and very calm. "Don't move yet. Check yourself first. Hands. Feet. Can you feel everything?"

He could. He hurt everywhere, but he could feel everywhere.

"Yes."

"Good. So can I." A pause. "Mostly."

"What does mostly mean?"

"It means my left arm is telling me things I don't want to hear." Another pause. "It's not broken. I don't think. Make a light, Dimitri. Small. Very small. The way we practised."

He lay there in the black and found the warm place under his ribs. It was awake now, wide awake, pacing like something in a cage. He didn't fight it. He sat with it, the way he had on his bunk every night for a week. And then he opened his hand and asked for very little.

A flame bloomed above his palm. The size of a coin. Gold, and steady.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

They were in a cellar. Low, stone-walled, the old dye vats lined up along one side like great dead wells. Above them, where the floor of the room should have been, there was a tangled mass of broken beams and slate and rubble, wedged tight across the whole opening. No light came through it. No sound.

Juliette was sitting against a vat a few feet away, cradling her left arm, her face streaked with grey dust. She was looking at the little flame in his hand.

"That's good," she said softly. "That's very good. Keep it exactly like that."

"The others..."

"I don't know." She said it flatly. "Corran went towards the stairs. The dockworkers I didn't see. Romeo and Susan will have heard the blast. They'll be coming." She looked up at the rubble. "It'll take time."

Dimitri looked up too. Then he looked at the flame in his hand, and then at the low ceiling, and then at the sealed space around them, and he understood.

"The air," he said.

"Yes."

"There's not much."

"There's enough, if we're careful." She closed her eyes for a moment. "I can feel it. I can keep it moving. Pull a little in through the gaps in the rubble, push the bad air out. It's slow, but I can do it all night if I have to." She opened her eyes and looked at the flame again. "But fire eats air. That flame is breathing the same air we are."

He started to close his hand.

"No," she said. "Don't. I'd rather have the light." A faint, tired twist of her mouth. "I'll feed it. You keep it small. We'll share."

So they did.

He sat beside her against the cold curve of the dye vat with the little flame floating above his open palm, and he felt her working the air around them, a slow, constant turning, like the breath of something very large and patient. Every now and then the flame would lean, just slightly, and he would feel the fire in him reach for more, and he would hold it, and she would send a little thread of fresh air towards it, and it would settle.

It was the strangest thing he had ever done. It felt like holding hands without touching.

---

They had been there a long time when she said, "Ask me."

He didn't pretend not to understand.

"I don't have to," he said.

"Corran told you. Didn't he. At the chapel." She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the flame. "Your heart went off the top of the display, Dimitri. I watched it. And then you came back and told me you went to hear the singing."

"I did go to hear the singing."

"I know. You didn't lie. You left something out." She almost smiled. "I taught you that. I'm not angry. I'm just tired of being the only one at this table who knows the rules."

The flame trembled. He steadied it.

"He said I should ask you about Varra," Dimitri said.

She was quiet for so long that he thought she wouldn't answer. The air turned slowly around them. Somewhere above, very far away, he thought he heard a scraping sound, like stone being moved. Then nothing.

"It was raining," Juliette said.

And she told him.

She told it plainly, without decoration, the way he had told her about his mother on the laundry roof. The dead zone at the docks. The earth wielder who had brought a warehouse down on her training squad. The friend who hadn't lived. The alley with no way out, and the ground shivering under her feet, and how afraid she had been. How she had taken the air out of his lungs, and how long it had taken, and how all that time he had been looking at her face, and she at his.

And then she told him the rest. The part she had never told anyone.

"I waited to feel bad," she said. "Afterwards. I stood there in the rain and waited for it. For guilt, or horror, or something. Anything. I'd killed someone. I was sixteen. I was supposed to feel *something*." Her voice didn't shake. That was the worst of it; it didn't shake at all. "And there was nothing there. Just quiet. Like a clean room. I thought: he deserved it. He killed Petra, he'd have killed me, he deserved it. And that was all I thought. And then I went back and lied to the inquiry, and I didn't feel bad about that either."

The little flame hung between them, gold and steady.

"That's what scares me," she said. "Not that I did it. That I could do it again. That if someone hurt Romeo, or you, or Susan, I'd do it again, in a dead zone, where nobody could see. And I'd sleep fine." She finally turned her head and looked at him. "So now you know what your captain is."

Dimitri looked back at her. Her face was grey with dust and there was a cut above her eyebrow and the scar on her chin showed white in the firelight. He thought about all the things he could say.

"You were scared," he said.

"What?"

"In the alley. You said you were scared. You said it twice." He held the flame very still. "Somebody who felt nothing wouldn't remember being scared. They wouldn't remember the rain. They wouldn't have spent two years trying not to think about it." He swallowed. "I don't think there's nothing in there, Juliette. I think there's so much that you had to close the door on it to keep standing up. And you've been leaning on that door ever since."

She stared at him.

"And you didn't lie to me," he said. "Tonight. You could have. Down here, nobody would ever know. You told me anyway."

For a moment her face did something he had never seen it do. It came apart, just slightly, at the edges, like a wall with the first crack in it. Her eyes were very bright in the firelight.

Then she looked away, quickly, and put the back of her good hand against her mouth, and they sat like that for a while in silence while the air turned slowly around them.

"My turn," Dimitri said eventually.

And he told her everything. Sister Orla, and the bench by the pillar. His mother, Mira, who couldn't bear candles and sat through the singing anyway. The man with burns on his hands that never scarred. Corran, hollow, with the little crescent behind his ear. The orders that came from someone nobody had seen, with a handprint burned into the paper. *Don't hurt him.*

When he finished, Juliette was looking at him with an expression he couldn't name.

"A handprint about your size," she said quietly.

"That's what he said."

She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to. They both sat with it, in the dark, under the rubble, and the little flame burned between them, and neither of them said the word *father*.

After a while, without either of them deciding it, her head came to rest on his shoulder. He didn't move. He hardly breathed. Her hair smelled of stone dust and smoke.

"Keep the light going," she murmured.

"I will."

"Wake me if the air goes wrong."

"I will."

She didn't sleep. He could feel the air still turning, steady and patient, under everything. But she stayed where she was, and he held the flame, and for a long time that was the whole world.

---

The rats came first.

He heard them before he saw them: a scrabbling high up in the rubble, then a thin grey shape squeezing through a gap he hadn't known was there, dropping onto a beam, sitting up on its hind legs to look at them with bright black eyes. Then another. Then a dozen.

Then, distant and muffled through the stone, two short whistles.

*Help.* Not asking for it. Offering it.

Juliette lifted her head off his shoulder. Something passed over her face that he would think about for a long time afterwards: a kind of startled tenderness, quickly folded away.

"Romeo," she said. Then she put two fingers to her lips, and whistled back, two short, clear notes that bounced off the stone.

It took them another hour to dig through, and when the first grey light of morning broke through the gap, it fell on Romeo's face, filthy and furious and grinning, with Sergeant on his shoulder and a crowd of rats around his knees like a very small army.

"You," he said to his sister, "are a terrible captain. You were supposed to use *one* whistle. One. Long. Out."

"I was busy," said Juliette.

Behind him, Susan was kneeling in the rubble, her hands pressed flat against a beam, and the beam was groaning upward, inch by inch, held by nothing but her will. Her face was white with effort. When she saw them, she let out a breath that was almost a sob, and the beam settled, and stayed.

---

It was only later, as they were climbing out, that Dimitri saw it.

It was lying in the dust at the edge of the hole, where the stairs had been. Small and gold and heavy. It must have come off in the blast, or in the scramble to get out.

He picked it up.

A signet ring. On its face, a vine twisting around a key.

He looked up. Susan was standing at the top of the rubble, a few feet away, with her back to him, helping Juliette up the last of the slope with her good arm. In the grey dawn light her hair was coming loose from its pins.

Dimitri closed his hand around the ring.

Then he stepped out into the street, and the Choir came rushing back into his head like light into a dark room, and with it a message, already waiting, already marked urgent:

*Squad Vasquez. Report to Commander Varga immediately.*

In his fist, the ring was still warm from the fire.
