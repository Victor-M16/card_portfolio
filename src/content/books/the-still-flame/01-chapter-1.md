The Choir went silent at the corner of Ninth and Tannery, and Juliette felt it like a missed step on a staircase.

One moment it was there, the low hum behind her thoughts that every human on Eta had carried since the day they were seeded: the time, her squad's heart rates, the patrol map, the soft green certainty that she was who she said she was. The next moment, nothing. Just her own breathing, which sounded far too loud without company.

"Dead zone," Romeo said, tapping the back of his skull like a man shaking a broken watch. "Somebody paid a lot of money to make this street disappear."

"Or somebody's very good with a jammer."

Behind them, Susan de Ricci had stopped walking. Both her hands were pressed to her temples, and under the streetlamps her face had gone the colour of milk. Three weeks in the EDF and this was her first time without the Choir. Most people went their whole lives without that.

"It comes back when we leave," Juliette told her.

"I know that." Susan lowered her hands. "I've read the manuals."

Of course she had. Susan had read every manual, and she held her shoulders like someone who had been taught posture by a professional. Juliette had been taught posture by a drill sergeant who threw boots.

Dimitri said nothing. He was standing by the last working streetlamp, an old oil one, staring at the flame inside the glass. It was leaning towards him. Only a little. Juliette decided she hadn't seen it.

She drew her sidearm out of habit. The grip sat cold and dead in her palm. Where the authorisation light should have glowed, a thin red line of text scrolled across the barrel: **NO CHOIR. NO FIRE.**

Four years of drills, she thought, and the first thing they hand you is the first thing that stops working.

She holstered it and drew the knife from her hip instead. Plain steel. No serial number, no chip, no ledger. The network couldn't see it, which was exactly why the EDF made every captain carry one, and exactly why the courts got nervous whenever one came up in an inquiry.

"Blades," she said. "Everyone."

Romeo already had his out. Dimitri fumbled his, nearly dropped it, caught it. Susan unclipped hers like she was opening a letter she didn't want to read.

"Brief me again," Juliette said, because hearing it out loud helped.

Romeo recited it in the bored voice he used for things that mattered. "Corran Vell. Nineteen. Hydrokinetic, crossed the registration threshold eight months ago and never reported for service. Suspected of working with the Ashen. Bring him in breathing if possible."

*If possible.* The EDF loved those two words.

---

The warehouse at the end of Tannery had been a dye works once. Now its windows were painted black and someone had run new pipe up the outside walls, good pipe, the kind you didn't find in a street like this.

Romeo crouched at the gutter and whistled low. Something answered. Then something else. A grey rat came out of the drain, sat up on its hind legs, and stared at him with an intelligence that rats were not supposed to have.

"City rats," he muttered. "Fat, stupid, and loyal to whoever has bread. Out in the Reach I could've had a hill-cat. Here I get this." He touched the rat's nose with one finger and closed his eyes. "Go on, sweetheart. Count heads."

The rat vanished under the door. Romeo stayed crouched with his eyes shut, his lips moving a little, like a man listening to a radio in another room.

"Second floor," he said after a while. "Six of them. Maybe seven. Rats can't count past three, so call it lots. And there's water. A lot of water. It's... warm in there. Like a bathhouse."

"He's running a still," Susan said quietly.

They all looked at her.

"The southern basin had its water rights sold two years ago. The taps in these districts run four hours a day. People pay for clean water wherever they can get it." She paused. "A licensed hydro charges more than this street earns in a month."

Juliette knew who held the southern basin's water rights. So did Susan. Neither of them said it.

---

They went in fast and low, the way the manuals said and the way Juliette's body did without asking.

The second floor was all steam and copper. Barrels lined the walls, and above them, hanging in the air like a held breath, was a slow river of water moving from tank to tank. Clean water. Hundreds of litres of it, turning gently in the lamplight.

In the middle of it stood a boy with his sleeves rolled up and his hands raised. He looked younger than nineteen. He looked tired.

"EDF," Juliette said. "Corran Vell, you're under arrest for unregistered use. Put your hands down slowly."

Corran looked at her. Then at the river over his head. Then back at her.

"If I put my hands down," he said, "this all hits the floor. Forty families don't drink tomorrow."

"Then put it in the barrels first."

"And then you take me in, and they put a collar on my license and send me to the Rim for ten years. And then nobody drinks."

A man by the barrels moved. Juliette caught it in the corner of her eye, the old shape of a chemical pistol coming up, black-market, the kind no Choir could lock because it had no chip to lock.

"Don't!" she shouted, and she wasn't sure who she was shouting at.

He pulled the trigger.

Powder didn't burn right on Eta. Everybody knew that. It burned fast or slow or sideways depending on the omicron count in the air, and a room full of wielders was thick with it. The chamber coughed, flared green, and came apart in the man's hand. He screamed. Metal sang off the ceiling.

And the river fell.

Corran couldn't hold it and run, so he stopped holding it. Hundreds of litres came down at once, and he went with it, riding the wave of it across the floor towards the stairs, towards Juliette, a wall of water with a boy inside.

She breathed in and pulled.

Air moved for her the way it always had, eagerly, like a dog that had been waiting all day. She tore the air out of the space in front of the wave and the water collapsed into the vacuum, hissing, scattering. Then she pushed, and she was across the room before her feet had properly decided to move.

Behind her she heard crates scraping against the floor. Susan, her face still white, had lifted three of them off the ground with nothing but a look and slammed them into the doorway the other men were running for. They stuck there, trembling, held up by a girl who had been doing this for real for three weeks.

Corran raised a hand and a whip of water came off the floor at Juliette's face. She ducked it. The second one caught her ribs and threw her into a barrel. Her knife skidded away into the steam.

Then he was standing over her, water coiling around his wrist, and she understood with perfect clarity that the Choir was dark. No record. No vitals. No one watching.

She had been here before. Varra, two years ago. A different dead zone, a different boy with a different gift. She had told the inquiry she lost sight of him in the smoke.

She had not lost sight of him.

Corran's face changed. He wasn't looking at her any more. He was looking past her.

The lamp on the wall had burst. Not broken. *Burst*, the oil inside lifting out of the glass in a long bright rope of fire that had wrapped itself, somehow, around Dimitri's right forearm. He was staring at it as if it belonged to somebody else. The fire was not still. It pulled against him like something alive, leaning towards the water, towards the steam, towards everything.

"Dimitri," Juliette said carefully. "Put it out."

"I don't know how." His voice cracked on the last word.

Corran took one step back. Then another. The water around his wrist dropped and splashed at his feet.

"They told me there was one of you," he whispered. Not to Juliette. To Dimitri. "They told me there'd be one of you with the soldiers."

Then he was gone, down through the floor drain in a rush of water that should not have fit through a hole that size.

---

The fire on Dimitri's arm went out on its own, eventually. Not because he did anything. It just seemed to lose interest.

His sleeve was ash. The skin underneath was perfect.

Nobody spoke. Romeo was wrapping the hand of the man with the burst pistol. Susan had finally lowered the crates and was sitting on one of them, very straight, shaking slightly.

Juliette found her knife in the steam and then found something else.

On the back wall, behind the tallest barrel, there was a handprint burned into the steel. Clean, deep, black at the edges. A grown man's hand, fingers spread.

The mark of the Ashen. She'd seen it in briefing files a dozen times. She had never seen one this fresh.

Dimitri came and stood beside her. He looked at the print for a long time. Then he lifted his own hand, slowly, and held it a finger's width from the steel without touching.

"It's still warm," he said.

Fire omicrons were one in a million. Everybody knew that too.

Two of them had been in this room tonight.

---

The Choir came back the moment they stepped onto Tannery Street, rushing into Juliette's head like light into a dark room. Time. Vitals. Map. And a report form, already open and waiting, because the network knew precisely how long she'd been gone.

*Target status.* She thought: *escaped.*

*Injuries to squad.* *Minor.*

*Use of lethal force in unmonitored zone (Y/N).*

She stood on the wet street with her squad behind her and looked at that question for a long time, the way she had looked at it in Varra.

*N*, she thought, and this time it was true.
