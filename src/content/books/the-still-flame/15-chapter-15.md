He felt them go.

That was the thing nobody else in the ballroom would ever understand. To them, the candles simply went out, all at once, as if a great breath had blown across the room. To Dimitri it was like four hundred and twelve threads, every one of them tied somewhere under his ribs, all yanked at the same instant by a hand much stronger than his. Not snuffed. *Taken.* Pulled out of their wicks and away, through the glass of the windows, out into the night, up the long dark slope of the vineyard to the orange light at the top of the hill.

And then the orange light at the top of the hill flared, bright as a struck match, and went out too.

The ballroom was black.

Somebody screamed. Then a lot of people were shouting at once, and glass was breaking, and chairs were going over, and somewhere near the musicians a man was calling for lamps, *lamps, for pity's sake, lamps*, and nobody was bringing any.

Dimitri stood in the dark with Juliette's hand still in his and could not breathe.

It was still out there. Whatever had done it. He could feel it, the way you can feel someone standing behind you in an empty room. Up on the hill. Huge, and hot, and very, very calm. And it was *looking* at him. Not at the house. At him. As if it had reached into the ballroom and pulled out every flame just to see which way he would turn.

The fire under his ribs had woken up and was throwing itself against him like a dog against a gate.

*Go*, it said. *Go up. He's there. Go.*

"Dimitri."

Juliette's voice, close, low, hard. Her hand had tightened on his until it hurt.

"Breathe. With me. Now."

He breathed. In. Out. He couldn't see her. He could only feel her: her hand, her shoulder against his arm, and around them both, the air moving, turning slowly, the way it had in the cellar. She was doing it on purpose. Wrapping it round him like a coat.

"That's it," she murmured. "Stay here. Stay with me."

And then she went rigid.

"Somebody's moving," she said.

---

He would think about it afterwards, again and again: how she knew.

It was pitch dark. Two hundred people were milling and shouting and stumbling into furniture. The air in the ballroom was churning like a river in flood. And somehow, in the middle of all of it, Juliette had felt one thread of it move differently. Fast. Low. Straight. Cutting through the crowd like a fish against the current.

Towards the fireplace.

Towards the Zetans.

She let go of his hand.

He felt her go, a rush of air past his face, and then nothing, and then, from the far side of the room, a sound he had heard once before, in a dye works on Tannery Street: a body hitting a wall, hard, and all the breath going out of it.

Something metal skittered across the marble floor.

And then a Zetan voice, deep and sharp, barking a word he didn't know, and the hiss of something long being drawn from a scabbard, and another, and another.

Dimitri didn't decide. There wasn't time to decide. There was only the dark, and Juliette somewhere in it with one good arm, and Zetan blades coming out, and nobody able to see who was who.

He held out his hand, palm up, and opened it.

The flame came up out of his palm the way it had in the cellar. Small. Gold. Steady. The size of a coin.

It was the only light in the room.

---

In the little circle of gold it made, he saw everything at once.

He saw Juliette, on one knee by the great fireplace, her good arm locked across the throat of a man in de Ricci livery, the deep green coat of the house staff, pinning him flat against the marble. The man's face was turned sideways, pressed into the floor. His eyes were open and furious.

He saw the knife. Long, thin, plain grey steel. Lying on the floor a foot from the toe of Ambassador Sath's boot.

He saw the Zetan guards, five of them, in a ring around their Ambassador, curved blades drawn, held low and level, the white paint on their jaws catching the light. The guard captain with the white streak down her nose had her blade a hand's breadth from Juliette's neck. She was not moving. Neither was Juliette.

And he saw Ambassador Sath.

The old Zetan was standing perfectly still in the middle of his guards with his hands folded in front of him, exactly as he had stood all evening. He had not looked at the knife. He had not looked at the man on the floor, or at Juliette, or at his own guards.

He was looking at Dimitri.

At Dimitri's open hand, and the little flame floating above it.

His dark red eyes were wide open. His second eyelids were drawn all the way back. And in his face, for one moment, there was something Dimitri had not expected to see on the face of an ambassador who had just nearly been murdered.

Not fear. Not anger.

Recognition.

"Captain," said Juliette, very calmly, in Zetan, without turning her head. Dimitri didn't understand the words, but he understood the tone. *I am on your side. Look at what I am holding down.*

The guard captain looked. At Juliette. At the man in the green coat. At the knife on the floor.

Then, slowly, she lowered her blade.

She said something short and sharp to the others, and one by one the curved blades came down. Not away. Down.

Juliette let out a breath.

Dimitri held the flame very, very still.

Across the room, beyond the edge of the little circle of light, he could feel the whole ballroom turning towards it. Two hundred people in the dark, who had been shouting and stumbling, going quiet one by one, as they saw that there was one light in the room, and that it was not a candle, and not a lamp. That it was floating above a young man's bare hand.

Somewhere in the dark, Commander Varga was watching. He couldn't see her. He didn't need to. He could feel her, the way you feel a heron in the reeds.

And somewhere in the dark, he knew without looking, Observer Teyl's head was tilted, listening to his heart. Which was going like a drum.

---

"Lamps," said a voice from the doorway. "Now. Quickly."

It was Celestine de Ricci. She had a candle of her own, an ordinary one, lit with an ordinary match from a box she must have been carrying in the pocket of her pale gold dress. Behind her came the footmen, the real ones, with lanterns, a dozen of them, then twenty, flooding the ballroom with ordinary yellow light.

Dimitri closed his hand.

The flame went out. In the new lamplight, nobody could have said for certain where it had been. A trick of the eye. A reflection on a glass. A young man standing near the fireplace who had simply been holding up a lighter.

Anybody could have said that. Except the people who had been close enough to see.

Juliette was hauling the man in green livery to his feet, one-armed, with the Zetan guard captain on his other side holding his wrist twisted up between his shoulder blades. Varga was already crossing the room towards them, her face like a closed door. Generals were shouting. The Speaker of the Assembly was being hurried out of a side door by four men in red sashes. Celestine was everywhere, a candle in her hand, saying calm things to people in a calm voice.

And Ambassador Sath was still looking at Dimitri.

He had not moved. He stood among his guards in the lamplight with his hands folded, and he looked across the ballroom at Dimitri with those deep red eyes, and then, very slowly, he did something Dimitri had never seen a Zetan do on any news broadcast or at any trade market or in any lesson.

He bowed from the waist.

Not from the neck. Not the polite bow, the *I show you I carry nothing*. A deep, slow bow, from the waist, with both hands open at his sides. The way you might bow, Dimitri thought, to something very old. Or very dangerous. Or both.

Then he straightened, and turned, and let his guards lead him away.

---

Juliette found him by the windows.

She had blood on the sleeve of the green dress, not hers. Her hair was coming down. The sling had twisted and she was holding her bad arm against her body with her good one, and her face was white, and she was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen in his life.

"The man," he said. "Who was he?"

"Footman. Hired three weeks ago, Celestine says. Through an agency." She lowered her voice. "When I had him on the floor, his hair moved. Behind his left ear." She touched her own head, just there. "A little crescent. Burn scar."

Dimitri felt cold all the way through.

"A hollow," he said.

"A hollow. With cold steel. In a ballroom full of the Assembly, during the one hour it was completely dark." Her eyes went to the windows, to the black slope of the vineyard. "Somebody turned off the lights for him."

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. She had felt it too. Not the way he had, from the inside, every thread yanked out under his ribs. But she had felt the air in the room move when the candles went, all four hundred and twelve flames pulled in the same direction at the same moment. She knew where they had gone.

"He was looking at me," Dimitri said. His voice came out strange. "Up there. Whoever it was. He took all the light out of this room, and then he just... waited. To see what I'd do." He swallowed. "I wanted to go. Juliette, I wanted to go up there so badly. It was like being called. Like someone calling my name from the next room."

"But you didn't."

"You were holding my hand."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"They saw," she said quietly. "Sath. The guards. Teyl. Probably Varga."

"I know."

"You could have stayed in the dark. Nobody would have known."

"There were blades out," Dimitri said. "And you were in the middle of them. With one arm." He shrugged, helplessly. "I couldn't see you."

Something happened to her face then. The same thing that had happened in the cellar, when he'd told her that she wasn't empty. A crack in a wall. She looked down, quickly, at the floor.

Then her good hand came up, and found his, the hand that had held the flame, and closed round it. It was still warm. She held it anyway.

They stood like that by the window, in the lamplight, with the ballroom in chaos around them.

And then Juliette's grip tightened suddenly, and her head came up, and her eyes went round the room in one quick hawk's sweep, and he saw something new come into her face. Something that, on anyone else, he would have called fear.

"Where's Romeo?" she said.

Dimitri looked round. The musicians, huddled by their instruments. The Assembly members, being herded towards the doors. The generals. The footmen. Celestine. Nonna Livia, back in her high chair by the conservatory, very small and very still, with her hands folded on her cane.

No Romeo.

No Susan.

And at the foot of the grand staircase, where he had stood all evening under the green curtains of the ivy, no Aurelio de Ricci.

"They went after her father," Dimitri said slowly. "When he left. Before the lights went. I saw Romeo looking at the corridor." His stomach dropped. "They went up the hill."

Juliette was already moving towards the conservatory doors. He caught her arm, her good one.

"You can't. Your arm. And Varga will..."

"Varga can court-martial me in the morning." She pulled free. "That's my brother."

Then she stopped. Turned. Looked at him. At his hand, the one that had held the flame.

"Whatever's up there," she said, "it wanted you to come. It took the whole room's light to ask."

"I know."

"So if you come with me, you're walking straight up to it."

"I know."

She held his eyes for one more second.

"Then stay behind me," said Juliette Vasquez, "and keep your hands in your pockets until I say."

They went out through the conservatory, past Nonna Livia's chair. The old woman did not try to stop them. She only lifted her head as they passed, and looked at Dimitri, very hard, with her clear sharp eyes.

"He has been coming to this hill for twenty years," she said softly. "Tonight is the first time he has ever turned out my lights."

Then they were through the glass doors, and out into the cold, and the dark slope of the vineyard rose ahead of them, row after row after row, all the way up to the top of the hill, where something was waiting that had known his name before he did.
