The vines on the gate knew her before the guard did.

She felt them wake as the car slowed, a long green stirring up the iron bars, leaves turning towards her like faces in a crowd. Old vines. Her great-great-grandmother had planted them, and every de Ricci since had kept them, and they did not grow for anyone outside the family. On the other side of the gate, the guard's eyes went flat for a moment as the Choir confirmed her, and then he smiled and bowed his head.

"Welcome home, Miss de Ricci."

She hadn't been called that in three weeks. Cadet, mostly. De Ricci, by Juliette, in a tone that made it sound like a rank she hadn't earned. Rich girl, by Romeo, which she had decided to find funny.

The car went up the long drive between the terraces. In the evening light the hills of the estate were striped gold and green, row after row of vines heavy with the late harvest, and below them, the orchards, and below those, the glass roofs of the conservatories shining like water. The air through the open window smelled of crushed leaves and wet earth and money.

It was the most beautiful place on Eta, and she had grown up believing that was simply what places looked like.

---

Her mother met her in the hall with both hands held out and her eyes doing a quick inventory: hair, skin, posture, the EDF grey.

"You're thinner."

"I'm fine, Mother."

"You're thinner and you're fine. Both can be true." Celestine de Ricci kissed her once on each cheek and stepped back. "Change before dinner. Your grandmother's come down, and Matteo's home, and your father has had a day."

"What kind of day?"

"The kind where he says he hasn't had a day."

Her room was exactly as she had left it. That was the strange part. The bed made, the books in their rows, the little lemon tree in its pot by the window, which she'd grown from a seed at seven and which leaned towards her now, eagerly, as if it had been waiting. She touched one of its leaves and felt the small, happy hum of something alive and well cared for.

Then, without quite deciding to, she looked at the hairbrush on the dresser and lifted it.

It rose an inch off the wood and hung there. Easy. Easier than plants, if she was honest, which she was not supposed to be. Telekinesis was a common gift. Half the porters in the city had a trace of it. Her mother had made her promise, the year it appeared, never to use it at table.

She set the hairbrush down. She changed into a dress she hadn't worn in a month, and looked in the mirror at a girl who seemed to belong in this house, and went down to dinner to find out whether anyone in her family was helping the Ashen.

---

The dining room had a living ceiling. Wisteria, trained across a lattice of fine wire, its long purple clusters hanging low enough that a tall guest had to mind his head. It flowered all year, because a de Ricci had told it to two hundred years ago and no de Ricci since had told it to stop.

Her father was already seated. Aurelio de Ricci rose when she came in, which he did for very few people, and held her by the shoulders and looked at her for a long moment. He looked tired. He looked, she thought, older than three weeks should have made anyone look.

"My soldier," he said.

"Cadet."

"My cadet, then." He smiled, and it almost reached his eyes. "Sit. Eat. Tell us everything you're allowed to tell us, and nothing you aren't."

That was the de Ricci way. Speak freely; reveal nothing.

Matteo was across from her, lounging, already on his second glass. Her brother was twenty-six and handsome in the careless way of people who had never had to be anything else. He raised the glass to her.

"The prodigal daughter," he said. "How's the army? Have they made you salute anyone yet?"

"Constantly."

"Barbaric."

At the head of the table, in the chair nobody else ever sat in, was Nonna Livia.

Susan's grandmother was ninety-one years old and very small and dressed, as always, entirely in green. She did not rise. She held out one hand, palm up, and Susan went to her and put her own hand in it. The old woman's skin was dry and warm and thin as a leaf, and through it Susan felt something she had felt all her life without ever learning to describe: a deep, slow, rooted strength, like standing on top of a very old tree and feeling it go down and down into the dark.

"Sit by me," Nonna Livia said. "Your mother will want to look at you, and I want to listen."

---

They ate. Lamb from the lower pastures. Bread. Greens from the conservatories, which tasted the way only de Ricci greens tasted, sharp and alive. And the wine, the house red, older than Susan, from the terraces she'd seen on the drive up.

The talk went the way talk went in this house: lightly, around things, never into them.

Matteo told a story about a buyer from the coast who had tried to bargain on the reserve vintage and had left with half the case and twice the bill. Her mother asked about Susan's bunk, her food, whether the other cadets were the right sort of people. Susan described Romeo and Sergeant, and her mother looked faintly ill, and Matteo laughed so hard he had to put his glass down.

And underneath it, Susan listened.

That was something the EDF had taught her in three weeks that the house had never taught her in eighteen years. Not to listen to what people said. To listen to the shape of what they didn't.

When she mentioned the southern districts, very casually, her father's fork stopped for half a breath and then went on.

When she mentioned the water ration, her mother changed the subject to the autumn gala.

When she mentioned the Ashen, nobody said anything at all for a moment, and then Matteo said, "Ah, yes. The romantic outlaws," and reached for the bottle.

"You've seen the graffiti?" Susan asked. "*Psi is wide?*"

"Everyone's seen it." He poured. "Can't say they're wrong, can you? Look at a map. We're sitting on one continent rationing water like a besieged town, and the rest of the world is just lying there."

"Matteo," said her father.

"I'm only saying what half the city says."

"The rest of the world," her mother said, "is full of Nuites, who would eat you, and Zetans, who would sue you."

"The Zetans would do worse than sue," Matteo said. "Have you heard what they're teaching now? That Eta was theirs. That their ancestors lived on our coasts before the Migration and we drove them into the desert." He shook his head. "A million years later, and suddenly they remember."

"They have always said it," Nonna Livia said.

The table went quiet. Her grandmother did not speak often at dinner, and when she did, people stopped.

"When I was a girl," the old woman went on, "the Zetan traders who came to this house said it too. Politely. As a matter of course. As you might mention the weather." She took a small sip of wine. "We were always taught it was a story they told themselves, to make the desert feel like exile instead of home."

"It is a story," Matteo said. "The schools are very clear. The Empty Shore. The ships came down and the land was waiting and nobody was here. It's the first thing they teach you, before letters."

Susan remembered it. She'd been five. A teacher with a soft voice and a picture on the wall: long silver ships settling on a green coast under a pale sky. *And the land was empty, and the land was waiting, and the land was ours.*

"The schools teach what the Assembly agrees to teach," her father said quietly. "That's not the same thing as knowing."

He said it the way he said everything, mildly, like a man commenting on the soup. But Matteo's mouth tightened.

"And what do the Sown say, Nonna?" Matteo asked, with the particular sweetness he used when he was being cruel. "Tell Susan. She's been away. She might have forgotten."

Nonna Livia looked at her grandson for a long moment. Then she turned to Susan, and her old eyes were very clear.

"The Sown believe we did not come here by accident," she said. "That we were planted. That someone, or something, prepared this ground before the ships ever landed, as a gardener prepares a bed before the seed. That is why the omicrons woke in us here, and nowhere else. The soil was made ready for us."

"Which means," Matteo said, "the land was empty because somebody emptied it for us."

"Which means," Nonna Livia said, without looking at him, "that a gift was given. And gifts are owed something in return."

"There it is." Matteo leaned back. "The family religion. We're the gardeners' gardeners. Our vines grow, so we must be blessed."

"Your vines grow," said Nonna Livia, "because I have kept them growing for seventy years, and your father has kept them growing for thirty, and you have kept them growing for none."

Matteo laughed, but he didn't answer.

Susan looked down at her plate. She had grown up inside these arguments. The Sown in the family chapel, with its living altar of olive and fig. The Wayfarers on the street corners in the city, insisting that Earth had been paradise and that humans were exiles meant to return to the stars someday. The Assembly's schools, with their silver ships and their empty shore. And most people, the people on Tannery Street, who didn't care where humanity had come from because they were busy trying to get through the week on four hours of tap water a day.

She had never asked herself which of them was true. It had never seemed like the kind of question that mattered.

---

After dinner her father kissed her forehead and said he had work, and went up to his study, and she heard the door close. Her mother went to see about the gala lists. Matteo went out, to somewhere he didn't name, in a car she didn't hear leave.

Susan found her grandmother in the conservatory, sitting in the green dark among the orchids, as she did every night.

"Sit," Nonna Livia said. "You've been wanting to ask me something since the soup."

Susan sat on the low bench. Around them, the plants breathed. She could feel all of them, the orchids, the ferns, the old fig against the far glass, each one a small warm presence like a candle in a dark room.

"Who runs the southern basin now?" she asked.

Her grandmother was quiet for a while.

"Your father did, for many years," she said at last. "Since the spring, your brother has."

Susan's hands were folded in her lap. She did not let them move. *A de Ricci heart does not race at table.* Her mother had taught her that when she was four, before she knew what the Choir was or who might be watching.

"Why?"

"Because Matteo is twenty-six and the heir, and your father thought responsibility might make a man of him." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "Your father has been thinking that for a long time."

"Does Father still look at the basin accounts?"

"Your father," said Nonna Livia, "has been looking at very little this spring except the EDF."

Susan turned to her. "What does that mean?"

The old woman reached out and laid one hand on the fig tree's trunk. Susan felt it answer her, a slow deep warmth rising through the wood.

"The basin license comes up for renewal every twenty years," her grandmother said. "It came up this year. The Assembly has wanted it back for a long time. Too much water in too few hands, they say. And they are not wrong." She paused. "This spring the license was renewed. Quietly. For another twenty years. And this spring, the EDF came asking for a girl with two gifts, and a family with a very old charter that should have kept her at home said yes."

The conservatory was very still.

"He didn't lose you to them, child," Nonna Livia said gently. "He paid with you."

Susan sat with that. She waited to feel something. Anger, or grief, or the urge to go up the stairs and open her father's study door and make him say it to her face. What she felt instead was a strange cold clarity, like stepping out of a warm room into winter.

The EDF had wanted to study her. Her father had wanted the water. And somewhere in between, somebody in her family had started giving that water away to people who painted *Psi is wide* on overpasses.

"Does Father know?" she asked. "About the basin. About where the water's going?"

"I don't know what your father knows." Her grandmother's voice was very tired now. "I am ninety-one years old, Susan. I know vines. I know this family. And I know that when the roots go bad, it never starts where you can see it."

She took her hand off the fig.

"Go to bed. You have two more days, I think. You have that look your grandfather used to get. Like someone counting."

Susan stood. At the door of the conservatory she stopped, and looked back at the small green figure among the orchids.

"Nonna. Do you believe it? That we were planted here?"

Her grandmother didn't answer for so long that Susan thought she hadn't heard.

"I believe," Nonna Livia said finally, "that whatever was here before us, it is still owed something. And I believe this family has been very careful never to ask what."

---

Upstairs, the lemon tree leaned towards her as she came in.

She sat on the edge of her childhood bed in the dark and listened to the house. Her mother's footsteps in the hall. A door. The wisteria shifting on its wires two floors down, the whole old estate breathing around her like a sleeping animal.

In the corner of her awareness, the Choir hummed, steady and patient. Somewhere a neat line was being drawn of her heart, and it was calm, because she had been raised to make it calm.

She thought about Juliette in the squad room, saying *three days, and then I don't care whose name is on it.* She had believed her. She still did.

She thought about Matteo, raising his glass. *Can't say they're wrong, can you?*

Then she lay back, fully dressed, and looked up at the ceiling, and did the thing she had been trained her whole life never to do in this house.

She let herself be afraid.
