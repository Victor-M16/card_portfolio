The medic said the arm wasn't broken. Cracked, possibly, a hairline in the forearm, and the wrist was sprained badly enough that it had swollen to twice its size and gone the colour of a storm cloud. She strapped it, and put it in a sling, and told Juliette to keep it still for three weeks.

"Three weeks," Juliette said.

"Or you can do what you like and have it set wrong and ache every winter for the rest of your life." The medic snapped her bag shut. "Your choice, captain. I've stopped arguing with your squad. Your brother brought a crow in here last week."

The Choir hummed. In its quiet green certainty, the message was still sitting there, marked urgent, waiting: *Squad Vasquez. Report to Commander Varga immediately.* It had been waiting for forty minutes. Varga would know exactly how long it had been waiting. Varga would know that Juliette knew.

She stood up.

Out in the corridor the others were waiting. Romeo, filthy, with Sergeant asleep on his shoulder. Susan, grey with dust, her hair half down, sitting very upright on a bench with her hands in her lap as if she were at a recital. And Dimitri, leaning against the wall, with a long scrape down one cheek and his eyes on her the moment she came out.

She had told him about Varra.

It hit her again, the way it had been hitting her every few minutes since the rats came down through the rubble. She had sat in the dark and said all of it out loud, every word, to another person. She had put her head on his shoulder. She had nearly cried. She, Juliette Vasquez, who had not cried at her own mother's funeral, had sat in a cellar and nearly cried in front of a cadet.

She waited to feel exposed. Ashamed, maybe. Like someone who had taken off her armour in the middle of a battlefield.

What she felt instead was lighter. As if she had been carrying something heavy for two years and had set it down for a moment, and found, to her great surprise, that somebody else had picked up one end.

She didn't know what to do with that. So she did what she always did when she didn't know what to do. She became a captain.

"Varga," she said. "Now. Let me do the talking."

They walked. On the stairs up to the command wing, Dimitri fell into step beside her, on her left, by the sling. His hand brushed hers. Something small and cold and heavy pressed into her palm, and his fingers closed hers over it.

"Found it at the bottom of the stairs," he said, very low. "In the rubble. After."

She didn't need to look. She could feel the shape of it. A ring. Heavy gold. A raised seal.

She slid it into the pocket of her jacket without breaking stride.

"Thank you," she said, just as low.

He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. Two days ago he would have kept it. They both knew that.

---

Varga did not tell them to sit.

She stood behind her desk with her hands resting flat on it, as still as a carving. Observer Teyl was not in its chair by the window. The screen above the desk was dark. There was nothing in the office at all but the commander, and the morning sun, and the four of them in a line on the carpet like children called before a headmistress.

"At second bell after midnight," Varga said, "my entire squad dropped off the Choir, in the same dead zone where one of them was nearly killed two weeks ago. For six hours. Nobody had logged an operation. Nobody had told me." Her eyes went to Juliette. "At twenty past the second bell, the city's ground sensors registered a detonation at the old dye works on Tannery Street. Chemical. Large. At dawn, my squad reappeared on the Choir, one by one, filthy, and one of them with an injury." A pause. "And now they are standing in my office, forty-one minutes after I asked them to come."

"Ma'am," Juliette said. "The delay was mine. I was with the medic."

"I know where you were, captain."

"We had information about an Ashen meeting inside the dead zone. We went in. Vell was there, and three others. One of them used a chemical charge in a room thick with omicrons and brought the floor down. Cadet Gale and I were trapped below until Cadets Vasquez and de Ricci dug us out." She kept her voice perfectly even. "Vell escaped. So did the others."

"And you did not tell me you were going."

"No, ma'am."

"Why not?"

Juliette looked straight ahead, at a point on the wall just above Varga's shoulder.

"Because the last time we set up on a target," she said, "six people knew where we'd be. And the target looked straight at us. This time, I made it four."

The office went very quiet.

Beside her, she felt Susan stop breathing for a second. She felt Romeo shift his weight. She did not look at either of them. She looked at the wall.

Varga did not move for a long moment. Then she straightened, and walked slowly around the desk, and stopped in front of Juliette, close enough that Juliette could see the fine lines around her eyes.

"Six," Varga said softly. "You counted the Zetan."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you counted me."

"Yes, ma'am."

Varga looked at her for a long time. Juliette could not read her face at all. She thought she saw something in it that might have been anger, and something else underneath that might, absurdly, have been approval.

"Good," the commander said quietly.

Then she turned away, and went back behind her desk, and when she spoke again it was in her ordinary voice, dry and flat, as if the last minute had not happened.

"The detonation on Tannery Street was a gas main," she said. "Old pipes. A tragedy that no one was hurt. The city's maintenance office will issue a statement this afternoon." She touched the desk, and the screen woke. "Vell is no longer your priority."

Juliette blinked. "Ma'am?"

"The Zetan delegation has asked to arrive early. Ten days from now, not eighteen. The Assembly has agreed. The reception at the de Ricci estate has been moved accordingly." A map came up: the estate, the long drive between the terraces, the house with its glass conservatories, the gates with their old vines. "The Assembly guard will handle the outer perimeter. The Zetans will bring their own. But the Assembly wants EDF wielders inside the house. Discreet. Young enough to pass as staff or guests. People the family will trust."

She looked at Susan.

"Cadet de Ricci," she said. "Your mother has specifically requested that you attend. As a daughter of the house."

Susan said nothing. Her face was perfectly smooth.

"Your squad will go with you," Varga said to Juliette. "As her friends from the EDF. As security. As whatever the evening requires." Her eyes moved along the line: Romeo, Susan, Juliette. They came to rest on Dimitri, and stayed there a moment longer than they needed to. "And you will keep your eyes open, captain. For anything. Anyone. In that house."

"Ma'am."

"Dismissed." And then, as they turned: "Vasquez. The arm. How long?"

"Three weeks, ma'am."

"You have ten days," said Varga. "Heal faster."

---

Romeo cleaned the cut over her eyebrow in her quarters afterwards, with water from the basin and a cloth he'd stolen from the medic, the way he'd done since they were nine. He was not gentle. He had never been gentle. He was very careful, which was different.

"Two whistles," he said, dabbing.

"Ow."

"Two short whistles. *Help.* You never ask for help, Jules. In eighteen years. I heard two whistles come up out of that rubble and I thought I was going to be sick."

"I was answering yours."

"I know you were. Hold still." He dabbed again. Sergeant, on the back of the chair, watched the whole operation with deep professional interest. "You were down there a long time."

"Six hours."

"With Dimitri."

"With Dimitri."

He wrung out the cloth. He didn't say anything else for a while. Then, very casually, not looking at her:

"Did you tell him?"

She went still under his hands.

He had never once asked. Not in two years. Not when she woke up shouting in the barracks. Not when she flinched in the rain. Not in the mess hall, when he had told her he was not asking, and would never ask, and had meant it.

He wasn't really asking now. She could hear that. He was just making a space, the size of one word, in case she wanted to put something in it.

"Yes," she said.

Romeo nodded slowly. He folded the cloth into a square, and then into a smaller square, and set it on the edge of the basin.

"Good," he said. And then, in a different voice, rougher: "Sergeant saw. That night. In Varra. He was on the roof." He still wasn't looking at her. "I'm sorry. I should have told you I knew. I just thought, if I didn't say anything, you'd never have to live it twice."

Juliette looked at her twin brother. At his filthy face and his steady hands and the crow behind him, the black-eyed witness who had carried her secret for two years and never told a soul but one.

"You're an idiot," she said.

"I know."

She put her good arm around his neck and pulled him in and held on, hard, with her face against his shoulder. He smelled of stone dust and rats. After a second his arms came round her too, careful of the sling.

On the chair, Sergeant made a small, disgusted sound and turned his back on them both.

---

They met in the squad room that evening. All four, and the crow, with the door locked.

Juliette waited until everyone was sitting. Then she took the ring out of her pocket, and set it on the table, in the middle, where the lamplight fell on it.

Nobody said anything.

Susan looked at it for a long moment. Her face did not change. Her hands, folded on the table, did not move. *A de Ricci heart does not race at table.* Juliette had heard her say it once, half joking. She did not look like she was joking now.

"Dimitri found it in the rubble," Juliette said quietly. "At the dye works. The man in the coat was wearing it. We didn't see his face."

"Matteo," Romeo said. Not a question.

Susan reached out and picked up the ring.

She turned it over slowly in her fingers, the way you would handle something that might break. She held it up to the lamp and looked at the face of it, the vine and the key, worked in gold. Then she turned it, and tilted it, and looked inside the band.

Juliette saw the moment it happened. It was very small. Just the faintest tightening at the corners of Susan's mouth, and a stillness that went all the way down, like a pond freezing.

"Every de Ricci has their own," Susan said. Her voice was perfectly level. "They're made when you come of age. The seal on the front is the same. But inside, each one is engraved. Your initials. The year." She set the ring back down on the table, very precisely, in exactly the spot it had been. "Matteo's says *M. de R.* Mine is in a box in my room at home. I've never worn it."

She looked up. Her eyes went round the table, to each of them in turn, and came to rest on Juliette.

"This one says *A. de R.*," she said. "It's my father's. He hasn't taken it off since before I was born."

The lamp hissed softly. Out on the parade ground, somebody was shouting orders at a squad of first-years in the dark.

Nobody said anything at all.
