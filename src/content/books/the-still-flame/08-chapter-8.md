At sixteen minutes past four in the afternoon, Dimitri Gale's heart had nearly come out of his chest.

Juliette sat on the edge of her bunk with the lights off and looked at it. A captain could see her squad any time she chose. Most captains never bothered beyond positions. Juliette had always bothered. She had learned in Varra what happened when you didn't know where your people were, and what state they were in, and she had promised herself she would never not know again.

So she had watched him all afternoon. A little green marker crossing the canal bridge. Turning into the Fourth District. Stopping at the bottom of the stone steps. The Fourth District was poor, but it wasn't dark; the Choir reached every inch of it, the chapel included. And then, for an hour and a half, sitting still in a chapel, while the thin line of his heart did things she had never seen it do. Not even in the review Varga had shown her, when he lied for her. The only time she could imagine it climbing like that was Tannery Street, with fire wrapped around his arm, and there had been no Choir there to draw it.

At 1612 the line had climbed so high it had clipped the top of the display. It had stayed there for nearly four minutes. Then, slowly, deliberately, it had come back down. She knew what that looked like. It looked like someone breathing the way she'd taught them to breathe.

*I went to hear the singing. That's all.*

She had stood at the bottom of the stairs and looked at him and known that it was not all. She had known it as surely as she knew the weight of her own knife.

And she had let it go.

She was still not entirely sure why. Partly because his face, when he said his mother's name, had been too raw to be a lie. Partly because she thought of Romeo across the mess table, saying *I'm not asking*, and of how it had felt to be handed that. Like someone setting down a heavy pack for you, just for a while, so you could rest your shoulders.

And partly, if she was honest, because she had stood there and thought: *He has a secret now.* And something in her had been almost glad. Because it meant they were even. It meant she wasn't the only one.

She turned the display off. In the dark, she reached under the bunk and took out her sidearm, and the strip of black cloth she kept with it, and tied the cloth over her eyes.

Slide. Spring. Barrel. Cell. Her fingers knew it the way they knew her own face. Clean the weapon, check the weapon, put the weapon by the bed. Her mother's voice, flat and patient, in a tent at the edge of a war.

She put it back together in the dark and set it on the shelf by her pillow. She lay down.

She did not think about Varra. She had a very great deal of practice at not thinking about Varra.

She thought about it anyway.

---

It had been raining. That was the first thing she always remembered. The kind of thin cold rain that got under your collar and stayed there. A dead zone on the edge of the Varra docks, an Ashen jammer the size of a cupboard humming in a basement somewhere, and a boy, a wielder, an earth-shaper who had brought half a warehouse down on two members of her training squad.

One of them had lived.

She had chased him through the rain into an alley with no way out. He had turned to face her with his hands up, and the ground under her feet had shivered, and she had known he could bring the alley down on both of them if he chose. She had been sixteen. She had been very, very afraid.

She had pulled the air out of his lungs.

Not all at once. She hadn't known how to do it all at once, then. It had taken a long time. Much longer than she'd thought. And all the time she had been watching his face, and he had been watching hers.

And when it was over, she had stood in the rain and waited to feel what she was supposed to feel.

She was still waiting.

That was the thing she would never be able to explain to the inquiry, or to Romeo, or to anyone. Not that she had done it. She could explain that. He'd killed her friend. He'd have killed her. Anyone would understand that. What she could not explain was the silence afterwards, inside her, where the guilt was supposed to be. Just a clean, cold space, like a room someone had swept.

It scared her more than the boy had. More than the alley. More than the ground shivering under her feet.

Because if she could do that, and feel nothing, then what else could she do?

She lay in the dark with her hand resting on the shelf by her pillow, near the cold metal of the sidearm, and eventually, because she was a soldier and soldiers slept when they could, she slept.

---

"Four days," Varga said in the morning.

The office was bright with winter sun. Juliette stood at attention on the carpet. Observer Teyl sat in a chair by the window with a slate on its knee, reading, its clear second eyelids sliding slowly across and back. It did not look up when Juliette came in. It never seemed to look up, she was beginning to notice, and it never seemed to miss anything either.

"Four days left on your ten," Varga said. "And you have Corran Vell walking into a chapel in front of you, waving, and walking out again."

"He knew we were there, ma'am."

"So you said." Varga did not ask how. She did not ask who could have told him. She simply looked at Juliette with that grey stillness, and Juliette understood that the question was being left on the table deliberately, for Juliette to pick up or not. "What do you propose?"

"No more roofs," Juliette said. "The chapel is a node. Water goes out, orders come in. Corran is one wielder; the chapel is where the orders reach him. If we raid it, we get an old woman and some cans. If we watch it with animals instead of soldiers, we see who carries the orders, and we follow them up."

"Animals."

"Cadet Vasquez's crow, ma'am. And whatever else in the Fourth District will talk to him."

On the windowsill, Observer Teyl turned a page on its slate. Its head tilted, just slightly.

Varga considered her for a long moment. "Do it," she said. "Report to me only."

On her way out, Juliette passed close to the window, and saw what was on Teyl's slate. The screen was in Zetan script, curling and dense. Most of the base couldn't have read a word of it. Juliette had taken the advanced course at fifteen, because her mother had told her the first rule of war was to read the enemy's letters, and she had never let it slip. It was a list of reports, in the formal register Zetans used for official correspondence. The one at the top was headed \*Human internal matters: the network of the burned hand.\* Beside it sat a symbol she had seen on every briefing she had been given for the last nine days.

The EDF's own case marker. The one for the Ashen file.

She kept walking. She did not let her heart move.

Outside, in the corridor, she stood for a moment by the window and counted again.

Varga. The four of them. And a Zetan observer who sat in the commander's office every morning, reading, under the terms of a truce that both sides wanted very badly not to break.

Six.

---

She found Teyl that evening at the edge of the parade ground, where the base wall looked out over the city and, past it, to the dark line of the western hills. The Zetan stood very still with its hands clasped behind its back, looking west. In the low gold light, the plates along its jaw glowed like sandstone at sunset.

"Captain Vasquez," it said, without turning. "You have been wanting to speak to me since this morning."

"Is it that obvious?"

"Your heart is very quiet," Teyl said. "In my experience, the quietest hearts are holding the most."

Juliette came and stood beside it at the wall. For a while neither of them spoke.

"My mother died at the Kesh Ridge," she said finally.

"I know," said Teyl. "I was there."

She turned her head sharply.

"Not as a soldier," the Zetan said. "As a medic. On our side of the ridge. I was very young. I carried water to the wounded for eleven days." Its voice did not change; each word set down like a stone on a path. "We lost four thousand at the Ridge. You lost three. Nobody on either side remembers why the ridge mattered. I think it was something to do with a well."

The sun was going down behind the western hills. Somewhere out there, past them, was the desert.

"Why are you here?" Juliette asked. "Really."

"Omicron research," said Teyl. "The exchange." Then, after a moment: "And because I have buried enough people, captain. I would like to stop. My people are divided. Many of them believe humans will never be satisfied. That you will always want more land, more water, more sky. When they see *Psi is wide* painted on a wall in your city, they believe they are seeing your true face."

"It isn't."

"It is one of your faces." Teyl's second eyelids slid across, and back. "You have many. So do we."

Juliette thought about the carvings. She thought about the ships, and the tall figures waiting with fire in their hands. She had told no one outside the squad. She was not going to tell a Zetan with the EDF's case marker on its slate.

But she could ask.

"Your people say Eta was yours," she said. "Before the Migration."

"We say we lived on its coasts. Fished its bays. Buried our dead under its cliffs." Teyl was quiet for a moment. "Your schools teach your children that the ships came down and the land was empty. Do you know what our schools teach?"

"No."

"They teach that the land was not empty, and that it was not taken." The Zetan's voice was very soft now. "They teach that it was *given*. That when your ships came down out of the dark, there were others waiting for them on the shore. Older than us. Older than anyone. The ones in the mountains, who were already old when my people were young. And they met your ancestors on the shore, and took them in, and gave them the coast. Our coast. And sent us into the sand."

Juliette's hands were very still on the cold stone of the wall.

"The ones in the mountains," she said carefully. "You mean the Muites."

"That is your word for them. We have older ones." Teyl turned its head, finally, and looked at her. "We are not angry with humans because you took our land, captain. We are angry because you were *given* it, and you do not even remember. You built schools that say the shore was empty. You forgot that someone made you welcome."

"Why would they do that? The Muites. Why give us anything?"

"That," said Teyl, "is the question my people have been asking for a very long time. The Muites do not answer. They no longer speak to us either. Not for a thousand years." It turned back to the hills. "There are old stories. About what they wanted in return. About what they could do with fire. One day, when we trust each other more, perhaps I will tell you some of them."

The sun went down. The plates on Teyl's jaw dimmed from gold to grey.

"Your cadet," the Zetan said quietly, "the late bloomer. Watch him, captain. Not for us. For him."

Then it bowed its head slightly, the way she'd seen Zetans do on the news, and walked away across the darkening parade ground, and left Juliette alone at the wall with the cold coming up out of the stone.

She stood there a long time. She thought about Teyl in the review room, telling Dimitri that late bloomer meant *hidden*. She thought about the EDF's case marker on its slate. She thought about a medic, very young, carrying water to the wounded for eleven days on the other side of a ridge where her mother had died.

She could not tell if she had just found the leak, or a friend.

She was beginning to suspect it might be possible to be both.

---

Romeo burst into her quarters a little after midnight with Sergeant on his fist and his face lit up like a flare.

"He's been watching all day," Romeo said, before she was even fully awake. "Sergeant. And three pigeons, and a very stupid cat. Nobody looks at pigeons, Jules. Nobody."

She sat up. "And?"

"And a runner came to the chapel tonight. Kid. Maybe twelve. Went in the back, came out without the bag he went in with. Sergeant followed him." Romeo crouched beside the bunk. His voice dropped. "He went back towards Tannery. Into the quiet block. Into the dead zone. Sergeant lost him at the edge; the Choir dropping out spooks him. But the kid dropped something on the way in."

He held it out. A scrap of thick paper, folded small, damp from the crow's beak.

She unfolded it under the lamp.

There was very little on it. A time, written in a neat, upright hand: *Second bell after midnight, Restday.* A place: *The dye works.* And in the lower corner, so small she almost missed it, a mark burned into the paper itself. Not printed. Burned. Five fingers, spread.

A handprint.

"Restday's the day after tomorrow," Romeo said quietly. "A meeting. Inside the quiet block. Where nobody can watch, and nobody can record, and the Choir's as blind as Sergeant on a moonless night." He looked at her. "Varga wants Corran in four days. That's three."

Juliette looked at the handprint for a long time.

She thought about the dye works, and the river of water hanging in the steam, and Corran's face as he lifted his hand. She thought about Dimitri with fire coiling around his arm.

And she thought about a dead zone at night, with no line to watch and no record to keep and nobody, anywhere, to see what she did.

She had been in one of those before.

"We go in," she said.

Romeo nodded. He stood, and went to the door, and then paused with his hand on the frame.

"Jules," he said. "Whatever happens in there."

"I know."

"I'm still not asking."

Then he was gone, and she was alone again, holding a piece of paper with a burned handprint on it, and her heart, for the first time in a very long time, was not quiet at all.
