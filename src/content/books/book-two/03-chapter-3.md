*Plants burn, de Ricci. Find out what else you can throw.*

Susan had been finding out for three weeks.

She was in the Kiln at the fifth bell, an hour before anyone else, alone, with a canvas bag at her feet. In the bag: river stones, smooth and flat, the size of coins. A box of iron tacks. A coil of steel wire. Twenty glass marbles she had bought from a toy stall on the canal and been mildly embarrassed about ever since.

The trouble with telekinesis, she had discovered, was not lifting things. Lifting things was easy. Half the porters in Haven could lift a crate. The trouble was *precision*. Holding one thing, exactly, in exactly one place, and putting it exactly where you wanted. That was what made her seed-throwing work: not the throw, but the placing. Forty seeds, forty spots, all at once.

It turned out that forty seeds weighed almost nothing. Forty iron tacks did not.

She threw them at the old scorched post at the far end of the yard. Forty tacks, in a fan. She held them all in the air for one heartbeat, the way she held seeds, and felt the weight of each one dragging at her like forty tiny hands on her sleeve, and then pushed.

Thirty-one hit the post. Nine went wide. Two went backwards, for reasons she did not understand, and one of them stuck in the gate.

She stood there in the cold breathing hard, with a thin trickle of something warm running from her left nostril. She wiped it on the back of her hand without looking. *Draw and burn*, Dr Pell called it. Use too much, too fast, and the body pays.

Thirty-one out of forty. Yesterday it had been twenty-six.

She picked up the bag and walked down the yard to collect them.

"You're bleeding," said a voice from the gallery.

She looked up. Observer Teyl was standing behind the thick glass, alone, with its hands clasped behind its back. She had not heard it come in. She never did.

"A little," she said.

"Your heart has been going very fast for some time." The Zetan's voice came faintly through the brass speaking tube. "You practise alone. In the dark. Before the others come." A pause. "Why?"

Susan pulled a tack out of the post. She thought about not answering. Then she thought about Ambassador Sath's red eyes, closed, listening to her brother lie.

"Because I'm the weakest one on the squad," she said. "And I don't want anyone to see me work at it."

Teyl was quiet for a long moment.

"In my language," it said finally, "there is no word for *weakest*. There is a word for *the one who has not finished yet.*" Its second eyelids slid across, and back. "You are bleeding on the inside of your hand too, Cadet de Ricci. From the wire. You did not notice."

She looked down. She hadn't.

"Thank you," she said.

"I observe," said Teyl. "It is what I am for."

---

The letter came at the eighth bell, by hand, on thick cream paper with a vine and a key in the corner.

It was from her grandmother. It was four lines long.

*The Assembly sits this afternoon on the matter of the boy. Your father has resigned his seat. Your brother is in no condition to take it. I am taking it back. You will sit beside me. Wear the ring.*

Susan read it twice. Then she went to find Juliette, and showed it to her, and Juliette read it once and handed it back and said, "Go."

"I'm a cadet. I can't just..."

"Varga will say yes." Juliette's eyes were very sharp. "She'll want somebody in that room who isn't on anybody's side. And she'll want to know what they say about him before they say it to his face." She paused. "Susan. Whatever they decide in there, it's about Dimitri. You understand that? Not the truce. Not the water. Him."

"I know."

"Then go and sit next to your grandmother," said Juliette, "and listen to them."

---

The Assembly hall was a great round chamber of pale stone under a green copper dome, with tiered benches rising on every side like the inside of a shell. Susan had been in the gallery once, as a child, on a school visit. She remembered being bored. She remembered the red sashes and the long speeches and a man who had fallen asleep in the third row.

She had never been on the floor.

The chartered houses sat together, on the lowest tier, on the eastern side. Twenty-two seats. Twenty-two old names, each with a little brass plate set into the wood. The de Ricci seat was third from the end. It had been her father's for twenty years. Before that, her grandfather's. Before that, her grandmother's, for nineteen years, until her grandfather had come of age and she had stepped aside.

Nonna Livia was already sitting in it when Susan arrived.

She was dressed in green, of course. Deep forest green, high-necked, plain. Her white hair was pinned up very simply. She looked tiny in the big carved chair, and very old, and perfectly comfortable, like a small bird that has landed on a branch it has known for a hundred years.

She did not look round as Susan sat down beside her, on the low stool reserved for the heir.

"You're wearing it," she said.

"Yes, Nonna."

"Good. Don't fiddle with it. They'll see you fiddle and think you're nervous."

"I am nervous."

"Of course you are. Don't fiddle." Her grandmother's dry old hand found Susan's on the arm of the chair, and squeezed it once, and let go. "Now. Listen. And watch their hands. Never their faces. Their faces are trained. Their hands never are."

---

The Speaker of the Assembly opened the session at the second bell after noon.

Annelise Halloran was small and silver-haired and sharp as a needle, and she had been Speaker for eleven years, and she had been at the de Ricci reception on the night the candles went out. Susan watched her hands, as she'd been told. They were folded on the lectern, very still. Too still. Like a person holding something heavy very carefully.

"The Assembly sits," said the Speaker, "on the matter of Cadet Dimitri Gale, of the Etan Defence Force. And on the question of what, if anything, the Assembly should do about him."

The chamber was full. Every bench. The gallery above was packed shoulder to shoulder, and Susan could see the little winking lights of recorders all along the rail. Somewhere up there, she knew, there would be a dozen people with gold flames pinned to their collars, and a dozen more with silver ships.

The first to speak was General Bram Hask, of the Assembly's Defence Committee, who was red-faced and enormous and very sure of himself. He spoke for twenty minutes, and the shape of what he said was simple. The boy was a weapon. The most powerful weapon Eta had ever seen. Weapons should be held by the state. Not by a single commander, not by a single squad, and certainly not by a single frightened cadet. He proposed that Cadet Gale be registered under a new designation, *Strategic Wielder, Class One*, removed from ordinary EDF command, and placed under the direct authority of the Assembly's Defence Committee, for the security of the continent.

Susan watched his hands. They were big and red and they kept opening and closing on the edge of the lectern, as if grasping for something just out of reach.

"He wants him," murmured Nonna Livia, without moving her lips. "Not for the continent. For himself. Look at his fingers."

The second to speak was Councillor Ysolde Marr.

Susan had heard her name. Everyone had, in the last three weeks. She sat for the Second District, and she was young for a councillor, perhaps thirty-five, with dark hair cut short and a plain grey coat and a voice that carried to the very back of the gallery without ever seeming to rise. She wore no badge. But when she stood up, a ripple went through the gallery, and Susan saw a dozen silver ships catch the light along the rail.

"Fellow members," said Councillor Marr. "General Hask calls the boy a weapon. I agree. But a weapon is a thing you point at an enemy. And I would ask this Assembly: whose enemy?"

She let that sit.

"We have all seen the recordings. We have all watched a hall full of Zetans bow to him. We have all watched the Fourth District kneel to him in the street." Her voice was calm, reasonable. "We have heard the Zetan ambassador call him the fulfilment of a desert legend. We know who his father is. The most wanted man on Eta. A deserter. A traitor. A hollow, who leads hollows. Who ran north into the arms of the mountain people and came back with fire in his hands." She looked round the chamber. "The boy did not choose his father. I do not blame him for his blood. But I ask you: when the time comes, when the Zetans whisper their legends in his ear, and his father calls him from the snow, and half the city is on its knees in front of him, *whose side will that fire be on?*"

The gallery roared. Half of it in agreement. Half not. The Speaker rapped her gavel three times before it quieted.

Susan looked at Councillor Marr's hands.

They were perfectly relaxed. Open, palms down, resting lightly on the lectern. Not grasping. Not holding. The hands of a woman who already had exactly what she wanted, and was simply waiting for everyone else to notice.

"She's the dangerous one," Nonna Livia breathed.

"What does she want?"

"She wants him afraid of us," said her grandmother, "so that we will be afraid of him."

Councillor Marr's proposal was simple. Not registration. Not a new designation. Restraint. Cadet Gale should be confined to headquarters under permanent guard, his Choir monitored at all times by the Assembly, his training suspended, until such time as the Assembly was satisfied that he posed no threat to the security of the human coast.

"Until such time," Nonna murmured, "means forever."

---

They heard from Commander Varga next.

She stood at the witness rail in plain grey and answered every question in the same flat, dry voice, and Susan, watching her hands, saw that they were clasped behind her back. Hidden. Of course they were. A heron does not show you its feet.

She said that Cadet Gale was under her command, and was training under strict conditions, and had not once used fire against a person. She said that he had saved the life of an elderly woman in the Fourth District three days ago, at considerable risk to himself, and that the people who had set that fire carried silver ship badges and were now in her cells. When she said this she did not look at Councillor Marr. She did not need to. Susan saw Marr's hands, on the bench in front of her, go very slightly still.

She said that the EDF did not want a weapon. It wanted a cadet. She asked the Assembly to let it keep one.

And then, last, the Speaker called the Zetan observer.

The whole chamber went quiet as Teyl walked down the aisle. It stood at the witness rail with its hands clasped behind its back, exactly as it did in the gallery of the Kiln, and its second eyelids slid across its eyes, and back, and it looked round the great stone shell of the hall at five hundred human faces.

"The Sere," it said, in its dry precise voice, "has asked me to say one thing to this Assembly. Only one. I will say it, and sit down."

Nobody breathed.

"If the human coast locks the still flame in a cell," said Observer Teyl, "the Sere will understand that the human coast is afraid of what it was given. And the Sere will remember that the last time the shore was afraid of what it was given, the desert was green, and then it was not." A pause. "That is all."

It sat down.

The silence afterwards went on for a very long time.

---

The vote came at dusk.

Three proposals. General Hask's: registration, removal from EDF command. Councillor Marr's: confinement. And a third, offered by the Speaker herself, almost apologetically, at the very end, in a tired voice: that Cadet Gale remain under EDF command and continue his training, but that he be presented to the full Assembly in thirty days, in this chamber, for formal assessment and assignment, and that the Assembly decide then.

"A delay," Nonna Livia murmured. "She's buying time. She doesn't know what to do with him either."

They voted by standing. Each member, in turn, around the great shell of the hall, rising and speaking a single word into the silence.

Hask's proposal failed early. It was too naked; even his own committee could see the hunger in it. That left two. Marr's, and the Speaker's.

And as the count went round, tier by tier, bench by bench, Susan watched the numbers, and felt her heart start to climb into her throat.

It was close.

It was very close.

The Shorekeepers' friends voted for confinement. So did a great many members who were not Shorekeepers at all, but who had watched a column of fire fill a stone hall to the dome and been frightened in a way they could not put into words. The Wayfarer-sympathising benches voted for the Speaker. So did most of the generals, who did not want the Assembly telling them how to run their soldiers. So did a handful of members who had been at the reception, in the dark, when a boy opened his hand and made the only light in the room.

The chartered houses voted last. By tradition. The oldest names, the final word.

One by one, down the lowest tier on the eastern side, the twenty-two old families stood, and spoke. Susan counted. She didn't mean to. She couldn't stop.

When it came to the third seat from the end, the count stood level.

The whole chamber turned to look.

Nonna Livia did not stand. Not at first. She sat in her big carved chair in her green dress, very small, very still, with her old hands folded in her lap, and she looked out at the Assembly. At the tiers of red sashes. At the gallery, with its winking recorders and its gold flames and its silver ships. At General Hask, red-faced. At Councillor Marr, perfectly relaxed. At the Speaker, tired, at her lectern.

Then she put her hand on Susan's shoulder, and used it to push herself up out of the chair.

She stood at the de Ricci seat with her granddaughter's shoulder under her hand, and when she spoke, her voice was very quiet, and it filled the whole hall.

"I am ninety-one years old," said Livia de Ricci. "I have sat in this chair before. I sat in it when most of you were in your cradles. I remember the last war, and the one before it." She was silent for a moment. "My family has a faith. Some of you share it. Most of you think it's an old woman's superstition, and you're welcome to. We believe that we were planted here. That this ground was made ready for us, by somebody, before we came. And that a gift is owed something in return."

She looked across the chamber, directly at Councillor Marr.

"Councillor Marr asks whose side the boy's fire will be on," she said. "It's a fair question. Here is mine. When a gift comes to your door, and you lock it in a cellar because you are afraid of it, whose side do you think it will be on, when it gets out?"

The chamber was utterly silent.

"The house of de Ricci," said Nonna Livia, "votes for the Speaker's motion. Thirty days. Let the boy train. Let him be what he is. And let us find out, in that time, whether we are a people worth his fire."

She sat down.

The Speaker's gavel came down once.

The motion carried by one vote.

---

In the car afterwards, on the way back to the estate, her grandmother fell asleep against the window almost at once, the way very old people do, suddenly and completely. Susan sat beside her and looked at her small face in the passing lamplight, and at her green dress, and at the ring on her own finger.

She thought about General Hask's grasping hands. About Councillor Marr's quiet, open, patient ones.

She thought about thirty days.

At the gates of the estate, the old vines stirred awake on the iron bars and turned their leaves towards the car, as they always did. Nonna woke. She blinked at the dark, and then turned and looked at Susan with her clear sharp eyes, as if she had not been asleep at all.

"You watched their hands," she said.

"Yes, Nonna."

"And?"

Susan thought about it.

"Marr's were too calm," she said. "Like she already knew how the vote would go. Like losing by one was fine." She frowned. "Like it was part of it."

Her grandmother looked at her for a long moment. Then she smiled. A small, sharp, delighted smile that Susan had seen only a handful of times in her life.

"There," said Livia de Ricci softly. "Now you are a de Ricci."

---

She got back to base after midnight.

She went straight to the squad room, because she didn't know where else to go, and found that the light was on, and that all three of them were there. Juliette, at the head of the table, with her braced arm resting on it. Romeo, in the window, with Sergeant asleep on his shoulder. And Dimitri, sitting at the table with his hands flat on either side of a small wooden box with a scorched, blistered lid.

They all looked up when she came in.

"Thirty days," Susan said. "By one vote. Nonna."

Romeo let out a long breath. Juliette closed her eyes for a moment. Dimitri didn't move. He was looking at her, and then at the box under his hands, and then at her again.

"I waited," he said quietly. "I didn't want to open it until everybody was here."

Susan looked at the box. At the burned corners. At the little brass catch, blackened by the fire Sister Orla had walked back into.

She pulled out a chair and sat down.

"Then open it," she said.
