The catch was stiff with soot. He had to work at it with his thumbnail.

Nobody helped him. Nobody spoke. Juliette sat at the head of the table with her left arm resting on the wood in front of her, bare for the first time in six weeks; the medic had cut the brace off that morning, and the skin underneath was pale and thin and faintly yellow with old bruising, and every few minutes she flexed the fingers of that hand, slowly, as if checking they were still hers. Romeo leaned in the window with Sergeant on his shoulder. Susan sat very straight in the chair she'd pulled out, her ring on her finger, her hands folded on the table, not fiddling.

The catch gave.

Dimitri lifted the lid.

---

It was not a large box. It had been made to hold a book, perhaps, or a set of papers, and it was lined with faded blue cloth, the same blue as the door of the Long Voyage before the fire. Wayfarer blue. Inside, there were three things.

A folded sheet of paper, thick, yellowed, sealed with a blob of plain candle wax.

A second, larger sheet, folded into quarters, with charcoal smudged at its edges where it had been handled.

And lying on the cloth between them, small enough to cover with his thumbnail, something hard and dark and misshapen. Like a seed. Or a tooth. Or a little lump of metal that had been very hot, once, and melted, and set again.

He didn't touch that. Not yet.

He picked up the sealed letter. The wax was cracked with age. On the outside, in a careful, rounded hand, a hand that had clearly been taught its letters late and was proud of every one of them, someone had written a single word.

*Baby.*

His throat closed.

She hadn't known his name yet. She had written this before he was born. Before he was anybody. She had sat somewhere, in some room above a laundry, with a candle she was afraid of, and written a letter to a person who didn't exist yet.

He broke the seal.

---

*To my baby, whoever you turn out to be,*

*If you are reading this then you came back to the chapel, and you asked Sister Orla about your father, and she has given you my box, because she promised. Orla always keeps her promises. I hope you have been good to her. She is the kindest person I know.*

*I am writing this in my room over the laundry. It is late and I cannot sleep. You are kicking. You kick a lot. Orla says it means you will be strong. I hope you will be strong. I hope you will not have to be.*

*I want to tell you about your father, because I think if you are asking, then somebody has already told you something, and I want you to hear it from me first.*

Dimitri stopped. He could not see the words properly. He blinked, hard, and they came back.

*His name is Idris Kane. When I met him he was a captain in the EDF, the youngest captain in the south, and everybody on the de Ricci estate was afraid of him except me. I don't know why I wasn't. I think it was because the first time I ever saw him he was standing in the old shrine at the top of the hill, at night, all by himself, practising, and he was making a little fire in his hands and then letting it go out and then making it again, over and over, the way you'd practise a scale. And he looked so lonely. He looked like the loneliest person I had ever seen.*

*I sang to him. Not on purpose. I used to go up there after the kitchens closed and sing to the shrine because it has the best echo on the hill, and that night I didn't know he was there, and I sang, and he heard me, and he came and found me. He said I had a voice like a bell. After that he called me his bell. His little bell. It was a silly name. I liked it very much.*

Beside him, very quietly, he heard Susan draw a breath.

*He was kind, baby. I want you to know that. Whatever they tell you. He was the kindest, cleverest, most frightening man I ever met, and he was kind to me, and he made me laugh, and when he held my hand his hand was always warm, even in winter.*

*But he changed. Not all at once. Over that year. He started going down into the old drains under Haven, on his leave days, the very old ones, the ones nobody goes into. He said there were pictures down there on the walls. Old ones. Older than anything. He took me once. I didn't like it. It was too quiet. The Choir didn't work down there and I hated how quiet it was in my head. But I saw the pictures. I drew them for him afterwards, because he asked me to, because he said I had a good eye. I've put my drawing in the box.*

Dimitri's hand had gone very still on the paper.

Across the table, Juliette had stopped flexing her fingers.

*After that he couldn't stop asking questions. Who were the tall ones in the pictures? Why did they have fire in their hands? Why do the schools say the shore was empty, if it wasn't? Why are the maps blank at the edges? Why does the Choir stop working in the oldest places? He said the Choir was a cage, and that we had built it ourselves because we were frightened of how big the world was. He said that somewhere north, in the mountains, there were people who had the answers, and he was going to go and ask them.*

*He burned his seed out. On the hill. I wasn't there. Aurelio found him. Afterwards he came to me with the skin behind his ear all raw and pink and he was laughing, and he said, "Bell, I can hear myself think." And he gave me what was left of the seed. He'd dug it out of the ash. He said: "Keep this. It's the last thing they'll ever know about me." I don't know what he meant. I've put it in the box too.*

*He asked me to come north with him.*

*I said no.*

*I said no because of you. Because I knew you were coming and I didn't know what he would want you to be. He kept saying you would be special. That you would be strong. That a child of his would have fire like nobody had seen. He said it the way some men talk about a sword they're going to buy. And I was so frightened, baby. I am still so frightened. I don't want you to be a sword. I want you to be a boy who sings in the bath.*

*So I let him go, and I came here, to Haven, where Mr de Ricci has been kind, and Orla has been kind, and nobody knows who I am, and I am going to raise you in a little room above a laundry and I am never, ever going to light a candle.*

*I don't know if he will come back. I think he might. He said he would come back for both of us when the world was ready. I don't know what that means either. If he comes, he will speak very gently. He always speaks very gently. That is the most dangerous thing about him.*

*Baby, I want to ask you something, and then I will stop, because the candle is nearly out and I am not going to light another one.*

*Don't let anyone make you into something. Not him. Not the army. Not the Wayfarers, either; I love them, but they are always looking for a miracle, and they will make you into one if you let them. Not anyone. If you have his fire, then you have it. But it's yours. Not his. Not theirs. You get to choose what you do with it. That is the only thing I want for you. That you get to choose.*

*I love you. I haven't met you and I love you so much I can hardly breathe.*

*Sing sometimes. Even if you have a terrible voice. Especially if you do.*

*Your mother,* *Mira*

---

He didn't know how long he sat there.

At some point he realised that he was crying, and that he had been crying for a while, and that he didn't care. The letter was in both his hands. He had folded it once, carefully, along its old crease, and he was holding it the way he had once held a coin of fire in a cellar: very still, so it wouldn't go out.

At some point, he realised that Juliette had moved. She was sitting in the chair beside him now, not at the head of the table. She hadn't said anything. She hadn't touched him. She was just there, close enough that he could feel the air round her moving, slowly, steadily, the way it had in the cellar. Like breath.

At some point, he heard Romeo say, very softly, from the window, "*Little bell*," and then nothing else.

And at some point Susan reached across the table and put her hand flat on the scorched wood of the box, beside his, not touching him, and said in a low, clear, steady voice, "Dimitri. The drawing."

He looked up at her. Her eyes were wet. Her face was not.

"I know," she said. "I know. But she wanted you to see it. That's why she put it in."

He set the letter down. Very carefully. On the table, beside the box, smoothed flat.

Then he picked up the folded sheet with the charcoal-smudged edges and opened it.

---

It was the Undercroft.

He knew it at once. Not the place itself; she hadn't drawn the chamber, or the tunnel, or the worn smooth floor. She'd drawn the walls. The carvings. Exactly as he and Juliette had seen them, half-lit, in the second the fire went up and the whole room flashed gold.

The long strange ships, coming down out of a sky of stars. The small figures spilling out of them. Humans, carrying bundles, carrying children. And waiting for them on the shore, in a long line, the tall ones. Many times taller. Hands raised, palms out. In every hand, a flame.

She had drawn it beautifully. Better than beautifully. Carefully, patiently, every line exact, every worn curve of stone traced out in soft charcoal by a kitchen girl who had been taught her letters late and who, it turned out, had a better eye than any painter he'd ever seen. You could see the cracks in the stone. You could see the places where time had worn the faces smooth.

And she had drawn things they hadn't seen.

He and Juliette had seen one wall, in a flash, for one second. Mira had stood down there with a lamp and Idris beside her and taken her time.

She had drawn the other walls too.

On the second wall, the humans were no longer kneeling. They were building. Houses. Walls. A city on the shore. And the tall ones were walking away from them, up into a line of jagged peaks at the edge of the stone. Leaving.

On the third wall, there was a war. She had drawn it small and crowded, in quick dark strokes, as if she hadn't wanted to look at it for long. Humans, and figures with plated jaws, and between them the ground split open and burning.

And on the fourth wall, at the very end, there was only one figure.

He was human-sized. He was standing alone on a high ridge, between two armies, with both his hands raised. And from his hands came not a flame, the way the tall ones held theirs, but a great still column of fire, rising up and up, past the edge of the carving, off the top of the wall, into the dark.

Underneath it, in different handwriting, someone had written in pencil. Not Mira's round careful letters. A man's hand. Quick, sloping, impatient.

*They were here first. They gave it to us. They sent one back once to stop the burning. Why? What do they want? I'm going to find out. I.*

---

Nobody said anything for a very long time.

Romeo had come away from the window. He was standing at Dimitri's shoulder, looking down at the drawing. Susan had gone pale. And Juliette, beside him, was leaning in so close that her hair brushed his arm, staring at the fourth wall, at the figure on the ridge with the column of fire.

"Sath's story," she said quietly. "The Long Burning. The man who came down from the mountains with a fire that didn't flicker, and stood between the armies." Her voice was very level. "It's on the wall. Under Haven. Under our own city. It's been there the whole time."

"And my father saw it," said Dimitri.

"Twenty years ago." Juliette looked at the pencilled words. "And it's why he went."

"*What do they want,*" Susan read aloud, softly. She looked up. "Nonna said that. In the conservatory. She said: *whatever was here before us, it is still owed something. And this family has been very careful never to ask what.*"

The lamp on the table hissed.

Dimitri looked at the drawing for a long time. At the tall figures, leaving. At the war. At the one small human on the ridge, burning, alone.

*They sent one back once.*

*When the shore forgets what it was given, we will send another.*

He thought about a voice in a fire, warm and gentle and even. *I made your fire still and I made it quiet, so nobody would come for you. Not the ones in the mountains.*

*He said it the way some men talk about a sword they're going to buy.*

He put the drawing down.

---

It was Romeo who picked up the seed.

He did it carefully, between finger and thumb, the way you'd pick up a beetle. He held it up to the lamp.

It was black and blistered and twisted, a little lump of something that had been very hot. But in the lamplight, you could see what it had been. A tiny, flat, oval capsule, smaller than a fingernail, with a thread of something silvery coiled round inside it, like the filament of a lamp. Most of the silver was melted into the casing. Some of it, at one end, wasn't.

"That's a seed," said Susan. "A Choir seed. I've seen diagrams."

"It's his," said Dimitri. "*The last thing they'll ever know about me.*"

"What does that mean?" Romeo turned it slowly in the light. "It's dead. It's been dead for twenty years. He burned it out of his own head."

"Seeds store things," said Juliette slowly.

They looked at her.

"They have to." She was frowning at the little blackened lump. "The Choir doesn't keep everything in one place. Every seed keeps a little of its own record, locally, in case the network drops. A few days. A week. Positions. Vitals. Sent messages." She held out her bare left hand. Romeo put the seed in her palm. She held it up close to her eyes. "If the casing kept enough of the filament intact..."

"Hang on," said Romeo. "If they keep their own records, why didn't mine remember Tannery Street? Or the dye works? Or the Undercroft? We'd all be in cells."

"Because a dead zone isn't a dropout." Juliette didn't look up from the seed. "A dropout is a storm, or a broken relay, or a valley too deep for the signal. The seed keeps writing; it just can't send, so it holds on until it can. A dead zone is different. A jammer doesn't only cut the line. It floods the seed with noise until it goes to sleep. The natural ones do the same thing, and nobody knows how." Her mouth tightened. "In a dead zone, the seed stops writing. Not a heartbeat, not a step. That's the only reason the lie in my report has held."

Romeo looked at the little blackened lump. "And he burned his out on the hill. Not in a dead zone."

"On the hill," said Juliette. "Awake. Writing. Right up until the moment it melted."

"Then the last few days of his life in the EDF are in there," said Susan.

"Where he went. What he sent. Who he sent it to." Juliette's voice had gone very quiet. "The week before he deserted."

"Can anyone read it?" said Romeo.

Juliette closed her hand round the seed.

"Choir engineers," she said. "At the Assembly's network office. They're the only ones on Eta with the equipment." She looked at Dimitri. "Which means if we want to know what's on this, we have to hand Idris Kane's seed to the Assembly. The same Assembly that's going to decide in twenty-nine days whether to lock you in a cell."

Nobody said anything.

And in the silence, Dimitri felt it.

Very faint. So faint he almost didn't notice. A warmth, in Juliette's closed hand. Not hers. Not his. Something else. Something small and old and nearly gone, like the last ember in a grate in the morning.

He reached out without thinking, and put his hand over hers, round the seed.

And for one heartbeat, under his palm, through her fingers, something flickered.

Not a flame. A pulse. One tiny gold pulse of warmth from inside the blackened casing, like a heart beating once in its sleep. And then nothing.

Juliette's eyes went wide. She had felt it too.

"What was that?" she whispered.

Dimitri took his hand away slowly. He looked at the seed, closed in her fist. Then at his own palm.

"I don't know," he said. "But I think it knew me."

---

The others left after midnight. Romeo, with a hand on his shoulder on the way past. Susan, who stopped at the door and looked back at him for a long moment, and then said, very quietly, "Your mother had a beautiful eye," and went.

Juliette stayed.

She didn't say anything. She sat in the chair beside him with the seed wrapped in a handkerchief in her pocket and her bare left arm resting on the table, and she looked at the drawing, and at the letter, and at him.

After a long time he said: "She didn't want me to be a sword."

"No."

"Everybody wants me to be a sword. Hask. Marr. Probably my father. The Zetans want me to be their story. The Fourth District wants me to be a miracle." He looked down at his hands. "She's the only person who ever just wanted me to sing in the bath."

"I don't want you to be a sword," said Juliette.

He looked at her.

"I don't know what I want you to be," she said. "I'm not good at knowing what I want. I'm good at knowing what needs doing." She was quiet. "But not that. Not a sword. I know what it's like to be one. I don't want it for you."

He didn't say anything. He couldn't.

She reached out with her left hand, her bare left hand, pale and thin and only just healed, and put it over his on the table. Her fingers were cool. His were warm. They both felt it.

"Do you have a terrible voice?" she said.

He laughed. He didn't mean to. It came out wet and cracked and helpless.

"Awful," he said.

"Good," said Juliette Vasquez. "Then you'll have to sing sometimes. She said. Especially if you do."

And after a while, very quietly, so that nobody outside the squad room could possibly have heard, with her hand over his and his mother's letter on the table between them, he did.

Five notes up. Five notes down.

It really was awful.

She didn't let go.
