The second morning, they were hungry in a way none of them had ever been hungry before.

Romeo had been hungry on bases. Everyone who grew up a barracks child had: the long week before a supply run, the winter the convoy got stuck at the Ridge, the nights their mother came back from the line too tired to cook and the two of them ate dry bread in the dark and pretended it was a feast. That was ordinary hunger. It sat in your stomach and complained.

This was different. This sat behind his eyes. It made the gully walls lean. It made every sound too loud, the brook, the wind in the gorse, Susan's breathing beside him on the bracken. When he stood up, the Hollin tipped sideways like the deck of a boat, and he had to put a hand on the red rock and wait for it to stop.

Dimitri was worse.

He was sitting where he had sat all night, with his back against the overhang and his knees drawn up and his hands jammed in his armpits, and his face had gone a colour Romeo did not like. Not blue anymore. Grey. The grey of ash in a grate that's been cold for a day. His eyes were open. He was watching the brook. He didn't seem to be seeing it.

Juliette was crouched in front of him, talking to him very quietly. Romeo couldn't hear what she was saying. He didn't need to. He knew the tone. It was the one she'd used on him when he was nine and wouldn't eat.

"He needs to be warm," said Susan. She was sitting up in her grain sack, her hair a wild dark tangle, her face cut from the hail. "Romeo. He needs to be warm and he needs to eat, and he's the one person here who could make himself warm in a second, and he won't."

"He's right not to."

"I know he's right." Her voice was flat. "That's the problem."

---

They held a council of war on the bracken, the four of them, in grain sacks, with Sergeant on a rock above them like the chairman of a board.

"Food first," said Juliette. "Then warmth. Then everything else."

"There's nothing up here," said Romeo. "I've been listening all night. Mice. Pigeons. A hare. Sheep, but they're a long way down, and they belong to someone. Nothing we can catch with our hands."

"Can you ask one to come to us?"

He knew what she meant. He'd known she would ask. He looked at the brook.

"I could," he said. "I could go into the hare and walk it right down the gully and into your hands. It'd trust me the whole way. It'd be happy." He paused. "And then I'd be in it when you broke its neck."

Nobody said anything.

"I won't," said Romeo. "I'm sorry. I'll do almost anything. I'll find you water, and a path, and a cave, and I'll tell you if there's a soldier coming from three hills away. But I won't walk something into a snare from the inside. I did it once, on a base, when I was eleven, because an older boy dared me. A pigeon." He kept his eyes on the water. "I didn't eat for two days. I could still feel its neck."

Juliette looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and that was the end of it. She never asked twice. She never had.

"Then it has to be me," said Susan.

They all looked at her. She had one of the leather pouches open in her lap, and she was pouring seeds out into her palm and looking at them very carefully, the way you'd look at coins to check they were real.

"I packed for war," she said. "Brand told me to. *Two pouches is a picnic.* So I've got six. Thorns, mostly. Vines. Things that grow fast and grab. But I put other things in too, because I'm a de Ricci, and we were taught that a person should always be able to feed herself." She held up her palm. Small pale seeds, flat and round. "Field peas. And these are bitter beans; Nonna grows them along the edge of the kitchen garden. And these..." a pinch of tiny dark specks "...are cress. Two days, normally. On a windowsill."

"And not normally?"

"Not normally, an hour. Maybe less." She looked at Juliette. "It's a flora signature. It'll be in the cache. When we come out of the dropout, the Choir will know that Susan de Ricci grew a patch of peas in the Hollin on the second morning."

Juliette was quiet for a moment.

"They already know we came into the Hollin," she said slowly. "They'll have it the moment we sync. River, fields, barn, gully. That's done." She looked at Dimitri. "What they mustn't get is a still flame. That's the bell they built the whole thing to ring. A girl growing peas is just a girl growing peas." She turned back to Susan. "Do it. Small. Don't burn yourself."

---

Romeo had watched Susan grow things before. In the Kiln, in a burst, in two seconds: a cage of vines exploding out of the dirt round Hollis Brand's knees. That was a weapon. That was fast and hard and done before you'd understood it.

This was not that.

She knelt on the flat earth at the back of the shelf, where the overhang had kept the ground dry, and cleared a patch of it with her hands. She pressed the seeds in one at a time. Not thrown. Placed. Each one with the tip of her finger, in a neat row, at a neat depth, the way her grandmother must have done it in a kitchen garden a hundred times while a small girl watched. Then she put both her hands flat on the earth either side of the row, and closed her eyes, and breathed.

Nothing happened for a long time.

Then the earth in the row cracked. A hair-thin line. A green thread came up through it, folded over on itself like a bent finger. Then another. Then all of them, all along the row, unfolding, opening, reaching up into the grey morning light with a sound Romeo had never heard a plant make, a very faint, very fine crackle, like frost forming on a window in reverse.

He felt it, a little. Not the plants; he had nothing to say to plants, and plants had less to say to him. But the mice in the bracken felt it. Every small life on the slope turned its head towards the back of the shelf, curious, the way animals turn towards a sound that isn't danger and isn't food and isn't anything they have a word for.

The pea shoots climbed. Susan opened one eye and, without looking, flicked a hand at a scatter of dead hawthorn twigs by the rock. They rose off the ground and slid, neat as soldiers, into the earth beside each plant. The tendrils found them and wrapped round them and climbed.

Flowers. White and small and folded, like moths. Then gone, and in their place, thin green pods, swelling.

Susan's nose was bleeding. A thin bright line, running down over her lip. She didn't stop. Her hands stayed flat on the earth. Her face was white and set and completely calm.

"Susan," said Juliette.

"Nearly."

"Susan."

"*Nearly.*"

The bitter beans came up beside the peas, darker, broader-leaved. The cress came up in a furred green carpet at the end of the row, so thick you could have lain your face on it.

Then Susan took her hands off the earth, and sat back on her heels, and wiped her lip with the back of her wrist, and looked at the row with an expression Romeo had never seen on her face before. Not pride, exactly. Something quieter.

"There," she said. "Nonna would say the spacing's wrong."

Romeo looked at the girl in the grain sack, with blood on her chin and earth on her hands and a row of peas behind her in the cold grey light, in a gully at the edge of the world.

"Marry me," he said.

She threw a pea pod at him. It hit him in the eye. He ate it anyway.

---

They ate everything. Raw. Pods and all. The bitter beans were so bitter they made Juliette's eyes water, and she ate hers without a word, because she was Juliette. The cress tasted of pepper and cold water and green. Romeo would have said, a week ago, that he would rather starve than eat raw cress in a ditch for breakfast. He had been an idiot a week ago.

Dimitri ate slowly, with his hands shaking so badly that Juliette shelled his peas for him. He let her. That was how Romeo knew how bad it was.

Afterwards, some of the colour came back into the fire boy's face. Not much. Enough.

"Thank you," Dimitri said to Susan.

"Thank Brand," said Susan. "Two pouches is a picnic."

---

Romeo went scouting at noon.

Not on foot. Juliette wanted them to stay in the gully until they knew what was above it, and she was right; the walls of the Hollin were steep and high and gorse-covered, and anyone on the open hillside above them would be visible for miles. So he climbed to the top of the overhang, where the rock leaned out over the brook, and lay down flat on it in the thin sun, and closed his eyes, and went looking for eyes that weren't his.

Sergeant first. Always Sergeant first. The crow went up for him without complaint, which was how Romeo knew Sergeant was worried too, and showed him the Hollin from above: a long red wound in green-brown hills, twisting up and west into higher, barer country. Moorland. Rock. Heather. And beyond it, ridge after ridge going grey and then blue with distance, until the last of them was only a line on the sky. The Reach. All of it. More land than he had ever seen at once.

There were two masts. One back on Corley Hill, the way they'd come, its red light blinking steadily now the storm had gone. And one ahead, far ahead, on a high bald ridge to the north-west, where the Hollin's head came out onto the moor. Small. Barely a pin. But there.

The Hollin would carry them to within a mile of it.

He came back down into himself and lay there on the warm rock for a moment, thinking.

Then, because he was there, and because the Reach was all round him, and because he wanted to, he tried something he had not tried in nine years.

He reached for something native.

Not the hill-cat. He wasn't stupid. Something small. On the rock face below the overhang, in a crack where the sun hit, there was a life he had felt all morning without being able to place it: quick, cold, and very strange. He reached for it the way he'd reach for a mouse, gently, a door opened a crack.

It was like putting his hand into a stream and finding the water was the wrong shape.

Earth animals had minds he could read, even when they were nothing like his. Hunger, fear, warmth, food, the dark, the light. A rat wanted. A dog loved. A crow judged. He understood all of it, because somewhere very far back, before the Landing, before anybody could remember, people and rats and dogs and crows had grown up beside each other, and had learned the shapes of each other's heads.

This thing had never met a person. Not in a million years. It didn't want, or love, or judge. It *counted*. That was the nearest word he had. The sun on the rock: one. The crack, safe: two. The shadow of the crow on the stone, moving: three, three, *three*. A little cold flat ledger of a mind, adding up the world and finding it mostly in order.

And then it counted him.

*Four*, it thought. And then nothing, for a long, long moment. As if four were a number it had never needed before, and didn't like.

It pulled away. Not with fear. With a kind of clean, cold, absolute refusal, the way a door closes in a house with nobody in it.

Romeo opened his eyes. He lay on the rock and looked at the crack below him, where something small and grey-green, with too many legs and a flat blind-looking head, was sliding back into the dark.

"Well," he said to Sergeant, who had landed beside him. "That's me told."

The crow clicked. It sounded like *I could have told you that*.

But Romeo lay there for a while longer anyway, in the sun, with his heart going quick, and did not feel told at all. He felt the way he had at nine, at the edge of a gully, looking down at a grey-and-gold shape in the scrub. Small and stupid and lit up from the inside.

There was a whole country out here full of minds that had never met a person. And he was the only one in the world who could knock on their doors.

---

It was Susan who said it, in the end. In the afternoon, while Dimitri slept, finally, curled on the bracken with Juliette's grain sack over his own.

"We can't keep doing this," she said.

She was sitting with her back against the rock and her knees up, with the envelope in her hands, not opening it. Just holding it.

"Doing what?" said Romeo.

"This." She touched the back of her own head, behind her ear. "Every hour, it's writing. Where we are. What we eat. How fast our hearts are going. Every plant I grow. And the minute we walk out of this valley into sight of a mast, it all goes. And then it starts again." Her voice was very level. "As long as we've got these in our heads, we're on a lead, Romeo. A long one. But a lead."

Juliette, sitting by Dimitri, didn't look up. But Romeo saw her shoulders go still.

"You know what the answer is," said Susan. "We all know."

"Say it, then," said Juliette quietly.

"Corran's scar. Behind his ear." Susan looked at her. "Ferro's. Bett Harrow's son's, one day, maybe, if the Ridge goes the way it's going. *Hollows.* They burned theirs out and the Choir never heard them again."

The brook roared.

"Do you know what a hollow is?" said Juliette. Her voice was very calm. "Really? Not on the walls, in the Fourth. Out here. No ration. No water allocation. No medicine; the clinics run off the seed. No pay. No travel passes. No vote. You can't walk into a town without the gate knowing there's a hole where a person should be. You can't go home." She looked across at Susan. "Ever. Your grandmother. The estate. The lemon tree. You'd be a hole in the world, Susan. Forever."

"I know."

"And the burning itself. Ferro used a hot needle. Corran said it hurt more than anything he'd ever felt. People die of it. People burn too deep."

On the bracken, Dimitri had opened his eyes.

Romeo saw it. Juliette didn't, for a moment; she was looking at Susan. But Romeo saw him lying there with his eyes open, grey-faced, listening. And then he saw Dimitri lift one hand out of the sack, slowly, and look at it.

"I could do it," said Dimitri.

They all turned.

"Clean," he said. His voice was rough. "Ferro said so. In the pumping station. She looked at his seed and said it had been burned with fire, from the inside, and that it was neat. Even. Hardly any scorch." He turned his hand over. "A still flame doesn't burn flesh. It burns omicrons. And a seed sits right in the middle of them." He swallowed. "I think I could reach in and burn the seed and nothing else. I think that's what he did. To himself. On the hill."

Nobody spoke.

"But that's a still flame," said Romeo slowly. "In four heads. In the cache."

"For one second," said Dimitri. "And then there'd be no cache. There'd be no seed to sync. It'd write the last thing it ever wrote, and then it would melt." He looked at Juliette. "*They're still down there.* That was the last thing his ever wrote. One minute before."

Juliette was looking at him. Romeo had seen a great many expressions on his sister's face in eighteen years. He had never seen this one. It was the look of someone standing at the edge of very deep water, the same as on the roof the night after the trial hall. Except this time, she didn't look like she was going to jump. She looked like someone who had just watched somebody else step off.

"Not yet," she said.

"Juliette..."

"Not yet." Her voice cracked very slightly. She made it stop. "You can barely hold your hands still. You haven't been warm in a day and a half. If you're going to reach into my brother's head with fire, Dimitri Gale, you're going to do it when you can light a candle without shaking." She held his eyes. "Not before."

Dimitri looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, and put his hand back inside the sack, and closed his eyes.

Susan looked down at the envelope in her lap. Romeo looked at the brook.

Nobody said *your brother's head*, back to her. But Romeo heard it. He thought she'd meant him to.

---

The second night was colder than the first.

The sky had cleared completely. The stars came out over the Hollin hard and white and sharp as broken glass, and the cold came down out of them like water poured into a bowl. It came into the bracken. It came into the grain sacks. It came into Romeo's bones and sat there, and he lay on the shelf with his knees pulled up and his teeth chattering and felt, beside him, Susan shaking too.

And Dimitri.

Romeo didn't have to look to know. He could hear it. The fire boy's breath, too fast and too shallow. The tiny knocking sound of his teeth. Juliette was lying against his back, wrapped round him, and Romeo could feel the air round the two of them moving, very slowly, very carefully, as she tried to hold a pocket of warmth against the night. It wasn't enough. Air could hold warmth. It couldn't make it.

"Jules," he said quietly.

"I know."

"He can't do another night like this."

"I *know*, Romeo."

He lay there in the dark for a moment longer.

Then he sat up, and put his hands flat on the cold rock of the shelf, and reached out into the night.

Not far. Not to the hill-cat; he had felt it again at dusk, high up, a long way off, and had made himself look away. To the slope above the gully, where something had been moving about on the heather since sunset. Not native. He knew them the moment he touched them, the way you know a voice in the next room: stupid, stubborn, warm, and enormously, magnificently selfish.

Goats.

Wild ones. Half a dozen. Escaped from some farm on the plain a generation back and gone feral, the way goats did, because goats would go feral at the first opportunity and regard it as a promotion. They were bedded down in a hollow among the rocks above the overhang, packed together in a steaming heap, chewing.

He didn't go in. He didn't need to. He just opened the door a crack, the way he had with the farm dogs, and let them feel him on the other side of it. And then he did the one thing that always worked on goats, which was to suggest, very gently, that there was a sheltered spot just below them, out of the wind, with dry bracken and four warm bodies in it, and that it was clearly theirs and somebody else was in it.

There was a pause.

Then a great deal of clattering on the rock above.

They came down over the lip of the overhang one after another in a scramble of hooves and loose stones, and stood on the shelf in the starlight looking extremely offended. A big brown billy with a broken horn. Three nannies. Two kids, half-grown. They smelled tremendous. They looked at the four humans on their bracken with expressions of outraged entitlement.

Then the billy, after a long moment's thought, walked across the shelf, stepped over Susan, and lay down, with a grunt, on top of Dimitri's feet.

The nannies followed. Then the kids. In under a minute, the four of them were buried in goat. A warm, heavy, breathing, chewing, unbelievably smelly heap, with Dimitri somewhere at the bottom of it and Juliette's face sticking out of one end looking more astonished than Romeo had ever seen her.

"Romeo," she said.

"Mm."

"There is a goat on my head."

"I know. Her name's... actually I don't know her name. Goats don't do names. She thinks she's in charge."

"She is in charge."

"That's the spirit."

From somewhere under the heap, very muffled, came a sound Romeo didn't recognise at first. Then he did. It was Dimitri, laughing. Shaking with it. Helpless, cracked, half-frozen laughter, under a pile of goats, at the bottom of a gully at the edge of the world.

And under the laughing, slowly, the shaking began to stop.

Susan was laughing too. Silently, with her face pressed into a nanny's flank, her shoulders going. Juliette wasn't laughing. Juliette was lying very still with a goat on her head. But the air round her had gone loose and easy, and Romeo, who knew every one of her faces, could feel the shape of this one in the dark, and it was the rare one. The one that meant somebody had surprised her into being happy.

He lay down at the edge of the heap, with his back against a warm brown kid who sighed and shifted and went back to chewing. Sergeant came down off the rock and settled on the billy's broken horn, as if he'd always meant to. And the four of them lay there, buried in goat, under the hard bright stars, and for the first time since the canal, Romeo let himself feel warm.

---

He woke because the goats went still.

Not asleep. Still. The way the mice had gone still the first night, and the pigeons, and the hare. Every animal in the heap, all at once, rigid. The billy's head up. The kids pressed tight against their mothers. The breathing stopped.

Romeo opened his eyes.

It was standing at the edge of the shelf.

The moon had come up while he slept, a thin bright paring of it over the eastern wall of the gully, and in its light the hill-cat was grey and gold, the way he remembered. Big as a man. Bigger. Long and low and heavy-shouldered, with a short thick tail that hung very still, and a broad flat head, and ears tufted with black. Its eyes caught the moonlight and gave it back, green-gold, like two lamps.

It was not looking at the goats.

It was not looking at Romeo.

It was looking at Dimitri.

The fire boy was asleep, at last, at the bottom of the heap, with his face turned up to the stars and Juliette's arm across his chest. There was nothing burning in him. Nothing lit. He was just a cold young man asleep under a pile of goats. And the hill-cat stood at the edge of the shelf and looked at him, with its great head lowered and its lamp eyes very wide, and didn't move.

Romeo didn't move either.

He could feel it. He didn't have to reach. It was right there, three metres away, close enough that he could smell it, rank and wild and hot. A mind like a furnace with the door open. Huge. Hungry. Old in a way that had nothing to do with years. A mind that had never been told what to do in its life.

And under the hunger, very faint, something he had not expected. Something that, in a smaller animal, he would have called curiosity.

It was drawn. He understood that slowly, the way you understand a word in a language you only half speak. It hadn't come for the goats. It hadn't come for the smell of people. It had come because something in this gully was wrong, in a way it had never felt before. Something that had been burning, two nights ago, at the bottom of the world, and had gone out. Something it could feel the way it felt the sun on a rock, or the storm coming off the hills.

*The fire on the hill*, thought Romeo. *Every animal in the Reach is afraid of it.*

*And comes back to it, when it's cold.*

He had said that on a roof, weeks ago, to his crow. As a joke, mostly. A clever thing to say at sunset.

He had not known it was true.

The hill-cat took one step forward onto the shelf. Its paw came down on the rock without a sound.

Romeo stood up.

He did it slowly. Very slowly. He stood up out of the warm edge of the goat heap, into the cold, and stepped sideways, one step, so that he was standing between the cat and the sleeping boy. He did not reach. He did not open the door. He did not, though every part of him wanted to so badly his hands shook, go in.

He just stood there. A barracks kid in a grain sack, with a crow on his shoulder, in the moonlight.

And he talked to it. Out loud. The way you'd talk to anybody.

"Hello," he said quietly. "I know. I know what you came for. I felt it too. It's all right. He's just cold." He swallowed. "He's ours. You can't have him. But he's all right."

The hill-cat looked at him.

For the first time, its great lamp eyes left Dimitri and came to Romeo's face, and stayed there. And he felt it look. Not into him; he hadn't let it in, and it hadn't tried. Just at him. At the small strange thing standing in the way. Weighing it. Counting it, almost, the way the cold flat thing in the rock had counted him at noon.

He held still and let it.

On his shoulder, Sergeant's claws had closed on him through the sacking. Hard. Not to hold him back, this time. Just to hold on.

The hill-cat breathed out, once, a long rough gust of hot air that steamed in the moonlight.

Then it turned, unhurried, as if it had simply finished looking at something and lost interest, and walked back off the edge of the shelf, and up the slope, and into the heather, and was gone.

Romeo stood there in the cold for a long time, shaking.

When he finally turned round, his sister was awake. She had not moved. She was still lying with her arm across Dimitri's chest, with a goat on her legs, and she was looking at him over the heap, in the moonlight, with her hawk's eyes very wide.

She had seen all of it.

She didn't say anything. She didn't ask whether he'd gone in. She knew he hadn't. He could see her knowing it.

She just held his eyes for a long moment. And then, very slightly, she nodded.

The way their mother used to nod, at the edge of a war, when one of them had done something hard and right and nobody else had seen.

Romeo lay back down at the edge of the heap, against the warm brown kid, who sighed and went back to chewing. Sergeant settled on his chest. The goats, one by one, let their breath out and went loose again.

Above the gully, very far away, high up on the moor, a hill-cat coughed once at the stars. Then the Reach was quiet.

Romeo lay awake for a long time afterwards, looking up at the sky.

He had stood three metres from the thing he'd wanted most in the world since he was nine years old, and he hadn't reached for it.

He had thought, all his life, that not reaching would feel like losing.

It didn't. It felt like the first true thing he'd ever done.
