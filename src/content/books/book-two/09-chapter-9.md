The wall run was older than the Academy.

It had been the base's curtain wall once, back when Haven still had walls worth defending: a long, high, crooked rampart of grey stone along the northern edge of the parade ground, forty feet at its tallest point, crumbling at the top, patched a hundred times over three centuries with whatever stone had been to hand. Somewhere along the way, some commander whose name nobody remembered had looked at it and decided that a wall nobody could defend was still a wall somebody could climb. And so for two hundred years every cadet at the Academy had climbed it. Up the face. Along the broken top, where the stones were loose and the wind came straight off the sea. Down the far end on a rope. Then round, and up again.

It was the first thing you did as a first-year. The first week. Everyone fell off it.

Juliette was at the foot of it at the fifth bell on Restday, in the dark, in the cold, with her breath smoking, when she heard the footsteps.

She didn't turn round. She was listening to the air, the way she always did before a run: the wind along the top of the wall, gusting from the north-east, strongest at the third buttress where the stone dipped; the pigeons roosting in the cracks halfway up, a dozen small warm sleeping weights; and now, coming across the parade ground behind her, a pair of small feet trying very hard to be quiet and failing.

"You're late, Okoye."

"It's the fifth bell, Captain."

"The fifth bell is when you start. Late is anything after the fourth." Juliette turned round. "Somebody said that to a friend of mine once. He's never been late since."

Wren Okoye stood on the frosted flagstones in her first-year grey, with her too-short hair and her scarred eyebrow and her chin up, and her hands shoved into her armpits for warmth. She looked up at the wall. In the dark it looked like a cliff.

"How many times did you fall off it?" she said. "The first time."

"Eleven."

"I've fallen off it fourteen."

"Then you're ahead of me." Juliette pointed at the first handholds. "Go on. I'll be behind you."

---

Wren fell off at the third buttress.

She went up the face fast and hard, much too fast, scrabbling, using her arms when she should have used her legs, and when she reached the top she stood up on the broken stones and the wind off the sea hit her from the side and she threw her air at it. Juliette felt her do it. A big, blunt, panicked shove of air against the gust, the way you'd put your shoulder to a door, and the gust and the shove met in the middle and tore apart into nothing, and Wren lost her footing in the chaos of it and went over the inside edge onto the practice mats with a thump and a word that first-years were not supposed to know.

Juliette came along the top of the wall after her, light and easy, letting the gust slide past her, and dropped down onto the mats beside her.

"Fifteen," she said.

Wren lay on her back on the mat, staring up at the dark sky, breathing hard.

"I pushed it," she said. "The wind. It was going to knock me off, so I pushed it."

"I know. I felt it."

"It didn't work."

"No."

"Why not?" The girl sat up, furious. "I'm strong. Instructor Fenn says I'm one of the strongest first-years he's ever had. I can knock a second-year off his feet from ten paces. Why can't I stand on a wall?"

Juliette sat down on the mat beside her. Not too close. The way you'd sit beside a bird.

"Because you can't push the sea," she said. "That wind's been coming off the water for a million years. It's bigger than you. It's bigger than me. It's bigger than anyone. You throw your air at it and it just goes round you and laughs." She looked up at the top of the wall, at the dip by the third buttress where the gusts were worst. "You don't fight the wind on the wall run, Okoye. You listen to it. You hear when it's coming, and how hard, and from where. And then you step out of its way before it gets there."

"I can't hear it."

"Not yet."

"Instructor Fenn says some people never do. He says listening's a gift you've either got or you haven't."

"Instructor Fenn," said Juliette, "is a very good water wielder and a very bad teacher of air. He's never had to listen to anything in his life; water just goes downhill." She held out her hand between them, palm up, open to the wind, the way Brennick once had, so the girl could see how still it stayed while the air moved across it. "Close your eyes."

Wren looked at the hand. Then at Juliette. Then she closed her eyes.

"The pigeons," said Juliette quietly. "Halfway up the wall. In the cracks. There are twelve of them. Some are asleep. Some are waking up. One of them, somewhere, is about to turn round on its ledge, because the wind's changing and it's getting cold on that side." She waited. "Don't push. Don't reach. Don't try. Just sit there and let the air come to you. And tell me which one turns."

They sat there on the mat in the dark for a long time. The wind came over the top of the wall, cold. Juliette felt it go round them both. Felt, beside her, the girl trying not to try, and failing, and trying again.

She had sat exactly like this, on this exact mat, four years ago, with Hal Brennick's big scarred hand held out beside her, and his slow voice in the dark saying *the pigeons, Vasquez. Twelve of them. Tell me which one turns.* It had taken her four months to hear one. She had cried with fury every single night of those four months, in the Long Dormitory, with her face in the pillow so nobody would know.

She was not going to tell Wren Okoye that.

"Left," said Wren suddenly.

Juliette went still.

"Left," the girl said again. Her eyes were screwed shut. Her voice was very small. "Third crack from the end. It sort of... the air went funny. Just there. Like when you move in bed and the blanket moves too." Her eyes opened. "Was that it? Was that one?"

Juliette didn't answer for a moment.

It had been the third crack from the end. On the left. A grey pigeon, half-awake, shifting on its ledge, turning its back to the wind.

"Once is luck," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she meant. "Do it again next Restday. And the one after. Ten times in a row, and it isn't luck anymore."

Wren stared at her. And then something happened to the girl's face that Juliette had not seen on it in the whole week she'd been trailing behind her with the kit bag. It broke open. Completely. Like a door flung wide.

She grinned. A huge, wild, delighted fourteen-year-old's grin, with the scarred eyebrow lifted and her whole small furious face lit up with it.

"Again," she said. "Can we go again? Up the wall?"

Juliette stood. She brushed the frost off her knees.

"Sixteen," she said. "Let's see if you can make it to twenty."

---

They did the wall nine more times before the sixth bell, Juliette climbing a few holds behind her every time, close enough to catch the worst of the falls and far enough not to. Wren fell off it six of them. On the last run she made it all the way along the top, past the third buttress, wobbling, white-faced, with her arms out like a tightrope walker and her eyes half shut, and she stepped left at exactly the moment the gust came, and it went past her. Not round her. Past.

She reached the rope at the far end and slid down it and stood at the bottom, shaking, and looked back up at the wall as if she had never seen it before.

Juliette came down after her and stood beside her.

"Twenty-one," said Wren. "I fell off twenty-one times."

"And got to the end once."

"Once."

"I took four months to do it once," said Juliette, before she could stop herself.

Wren looked up at her. Then very carefully, very deliberately, the way you'd set down something fragile, she didn't say anything at all.

They walked back across the parade ground together in the grey dawn light. The base was very quiet. It was Restday: no assembly, no inspection, no duty roster. Most of the cadets would sleep until the eighth bell and then pour out of the gate into the city with their leave passes, to the canal markets and the cheap taverns of the Second District and the pastry shops on Lantern Street, and not come back until the last bell.

"Captain," said Wren, halfway across. "Can I ask a question?"

"You've been asking questions all week."

"This one's different."

Juliette heard it in her voice. She heard it in the air round the girl, too: a small tight knot of nerves.

"Ask."

"In the first-years' dorm," said Wren carefully, "there's a rumour. About you. And Cadet Gale." She stared straight ahead. "Someone's cousin's friend saw you on the canal towpath. At night. Holding hands." A pause. "Everyone says it can't be true. Because of Article Twelve. Because you're the captain who enforces it."

Juliette kept walking.

She felt her heart do something, and she made it stop. She felt the air around her go very still, and she let it. Across the parade ground, the duty officer's window was dark. Somewhere above them, in every head on the base, the Choir hummed softly, drawing its neat little lines.

*I'm a captain. I enforce it.*

"Is it true?" said Wren.

They reached the steps of the north wing. Juliette stopped at the bottom of them and turned round.

The girl was looking up at her. Not with gossip in her face. Not with the greedy delight the second-years got when they had something on somebody. With something else. Something careful, and steady, and very young.

"Why do you want to know?" said Juliette.

Wren thought about it. Properly. Juliette watched her do it.

"Because if it's true," said the girl slowly, "then you broke a rule you believe in. And you're still the best captain at the Academy." She swallowed. "And I'd like to know if that's allowed. To be both."

Juliette looked at her for a long time.

She thought about a cellar, and the air turning slowly round a coin of gold fire. About a dark ballroom, and a hand held out palm up. About a voice in a stone hall saying *you chose to*, and her own voice answering *I don't know if I can ever do it again*.

She thought about the chair in the corridor. About Petra under the beam, and what she would have done if Dimitri had been under it instead.

"I don't know if it's allowed," she said. "I'm still finding out."

Wren nodded, slowly, as if that were a perfectly reasonable answer to the question she'd asked. As if, perhaps, it was the only honest one anyone had ever given her.

"I won't tell anyone," she said. "That you said that."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because you didn't tell anyone what you heard through the review room door," said Juliette, "and you could have dined out on that for a month." She turned and went up the steps. "Go and sleep, Okoye. Twenty-one falls. You've earned it."

---

Restday on a restricted squad was a very long day.

Romeo was, as Juliette had known he would be, absolutely unbearable about it.

He was confined to barracks after the last bell, which on Restday meant nothing, because nobody left after the last bell on Restday anyway. But he had decided to treat it as a great personal tragedy, and he spent the whole morning lying full length on the squad room bench with Sergeant on his chest, reciting, in a voice of profound suffering, everything he would have been doing if he were free.

"The canal market," he said to the ceiling. "The good pastry stall. The one with the cinnamon knots. Biscuit, sitting on the steps of the chapel, waiting for me. The towpath at dusk."

"You're not confined in the daytime," said Susan, without looking up from her slate. "You could go to the canal market right now."

"With an instructor present. Logged. In daylight." Romeo shuddered. "Have you ever tried to buy a cinnamon knot with Instructor Fenn watching? He looks at the pastry like it's a disciplinary matter."

Susan was not allowed to leave the base at all except on Assembly sitting days, and only with an escort; there was no sitting until midweek. Dimitri was not allowed to leave without an officer above the rank of cadet captain, and there were no officers above the rank of cadet captain who wanted to spend their Restday walking the most-watched cadet on the continent round the canal markets. So the four of them, who on any other Restday would have scattered across Haven by the eighth bell, were all stuck on base together, in the squad room, with the door open and Sergeant and a plate of the jam pastries that Ordo made only on Restdays and only for people he pretended not to like.

Juliette found, somewhat to her surprise, that she did not mind.

She sat at the head of the table with a blank report form in front of her and a pen in her hand. Romeo complained. Susan read. Dimitri sat at the window with his elbows on the sill, looking out at the empty parade ground, and every so often he held his hand out flat and a coin of gold fire bloomed above his palm, perfectly still, and he looked at it for a long moment and closed his hand and it went out. He'd been doing it all week. Small fires. Quiet ones. Like a man checking a lock.

"You know that's against the rule," said Romeo from the bench, without opening his eyes. "No fire outside the Kiln."

"It's not at a person."

"Sergeant's a person," said Romeo. "Legally. He's got paperwork. Look at him, he's traumatised." On Romeo's chest, Sergeant, who had not opened an eye all morning, went on very pointedly not opening one. "See? Can't even look at it. You've scarred him, Gale. I hope you're proud."

Dimitri laughed. It was the first time Juliette had heard him laugh in days. He closed his hand, and the coin of fire went out, and he looked across the room at her. She looked back. Then down at the report form. Then up again.

Romeo, on the bench, opened one eye and looked at both of them and then very ostentatiously closed it again.

"Nobody in this room," he announced to the ceiling, "has the faintest idea why two of the people in this room keep looking at each other. Total mystery. Baffling. I may write a paper."

"Shut up, Romeo," said Juliette and Susan together.

Sergeant, on Romeo's chest, made a dry, contented clicking sound, and settled his feathers, and went to sleep.

---

At noon, Squad Four came past the open door on their way to the gate.

Cassius Dray was at the front, tall and red-haired, in his Restday civilian coat, with his squad of five strung out behind him laughing at something. He slowed when he saw them. He leaned in the doorway, very casually, with one shoulder against the frame, and looked round the room at the four of them: Romeo on the bench, Susan with her slate, Dimitri at the window, and Juliette at the head of the table with her blank report.

"Squad Nine," he said. "Spending Restday together. How sweet." He smiled. "I hear Colonel Thorne's put you all on a lead."

"Have a good day in the city, Dray," said Juliette.

"Oh, I will. I'll bring you back a cinnamon knot, Vasquez. You can eat it in front of your instructor." His eyes went to Dimitri. Rested there a moment too long. "And Gale. Still not allowed out without a minder? That must be hard. For the continent's favourite candle."

The air in the room went still. Juliette felt it happen. She didn't decide it. She just felt every breath in the squad room go very quiet and very cold, and Dray, in the doorway, felt it too; she saw it in his face, the flicker of a man who has stepped onto ice and heard it creak.

"Goodbye, Captain Dray," she said.

He held her eyes for a moment longer. Then he pushed off the door frame and smiled again, a little less easily, and went.

The air in the room came back.

"Thank you," said Dimitri quietly, from the window.

"I didn't do anything."

"You did," said Dimitri. "I felt it."

Romeo, on the bench, had opened both his eyes. He was looking at the empty doorway with an expression Juliette knew very well. It was the one he got when he was trying to work out what kind of animal someone was.

"He's been past this door four times this week," he said slowly. "He never used to come down this corridor at all."

"He's jealous," said Susan. "He's come second to us all year."

"Maybe," said Romeo. He didn't close his eyes again.

---

She wrote the report in the afternoon, when the others had gone down to the Kiln for an hour so Dimitri could practise distance with Brand watching from the gallery.

It was harder than she had expected.

Not the facts. The facts were easy. *Monday: squad on patrol rotation, Second and Third Districts, eight hours, no incidents. Tuesday: training, Kiln and element yards, per individual plans posted.* She could write facts in her sleep. She had been writing them since she was sixteen.

It was what to leave out.

She sat with her pen over the paper and thought about the pumping station. About an old woman with a lens over one eye, and a machine built out of a dozen other machines, and a seed pulsing under Dimitri's palm like a heart. About four words on a cracked slate. *They're still down there.*

She thought about Colonel Thorne, who had carried her mother's body back across the Ridge, and who had read every one of her reviews twice, and who had said: *I'm interested in what they do when they think they aren't being watched.*

She thought about the inquiry, two years ago. The long table. The careful questions. *Did you lose sight of the target, Cadet Vasquez?* And her own voice, perfectly steady, answering *yes, sir. In the rain.*

She had told herself, for two years, that it was the only lie she would ever tell the EDF. That everything after it, every corner and every bell and every honest report, made up for it.

She looked down at the report form. At the empty space under *Wednesday*.

Wednesday was the pumping station.

She wrote: *Wednesday: Captain Vasquez and Cadet Gale, welfare visit, Fourth District, two hours. Cadet Vasquez R., fauna practice, Fourth Canal towpath, evening.*

Every word of it true.

She sat and looked at it for a long time.

Then she signed it, and folded it, and put it in an envelope addressed to Colonel M. Thorne, Command Towers, and set it on the corner of the desk. And sat there with her hands flat on the table, and felt, for the first time, what it was actually like to do what she had told Dimitri on the roof all those weeks ago.

*There's a difference between a lie and leaving something out. You'll learn it.*

She had thought, when she said it, that the difference was a comfort.

It wasn't. It was just a narrower place to stand.

---

Dimitri found her on the training roof at dusk.

She heard him on the stairs. She always did. He wasn't trying to be quiet; he never was. He came up onto the roof and stopped a little way off, and didn't come any closer. He had his hands in his pockets.

"Brand said I held it at twenty-six metres," he said. "Personal best."

"Good."

"Romeo says you went up the wall with the first-year at the fifth bell."

"She fell off twenty-one times."

"And?"

"And made it to the end once." Juliette was looking out over the parade ground, at the lamps coming on in the dormitory windows. "She heard a pigeon turn. Third crack from the end. First time."

Dimitri was quiet. Then: "That's how you learned. Isn't it. The pigeons."

She turned her head and looked at him.

"You told me," he said. "In the cellar. When you were telling me everything. You said a lieutenant used to make you sit on a mat in the dark and listen to the pigeons until you could hear which one turned. You said it took you four months." He hesitated. "You probably don't remember. There was a lot going on."

She hadn't remembered. She didn't remember saying it at all.

She looked at him, standing a careful distance away on the roof with his hands in his pockets, in the cold, remembering a thing she had said in the dark weeks ago that she hadn't even known she'd said.

"There's a rumour," she said. "In the first-years' dorm. About us."

He went very still.

"Someone saw us on the towpath," she said. "The night of the seed. When I took your hand."

"I know. I was there." His voice was very quiet. "Is that bad?"

"Article Twelve," said Juliette.

"I know what it says."

"I'm your captain, Dimitri."

"I know."

"If it goes in front of the Board, it's not just a demerit. It's a captain and a cadet in her own squad. In her own chain of command." She heard her voice, flat and certain, the way she had recited it to Wren in the corridor by the duty chair. "They'd take my bar. Or move you. To another squad. Under another captain." She paused. "Probably Dray."

"I'd rather the stones."

"So would I." She almost smiled. It didn't last. "But that's not the worst of it."

He waited.

"The worst of it," said Juliette, "is that if they moved you, it would be *right*. That's the part I can't get past. The rule's not stupid. It's there because of beams." She looked out at the dark. "Because of Varra. Because one day I might be standing over you and somebody else, and I'd have one second to choose. And I'd choose wrong. I know I would. I'd choose you every time."

The wind came over the roof. She didn't touch it.

"And the only way to make sure I never have to make that choice," she said, "is to send you to someone else. And then I'm not there. And you're in a burning hall, or under a hill, or in front of the Assembly in nineteen days, and somebody else is standing behind you. Somebody who doesn't know that if you lose it, the air won't work." Her voice cracked, very slightly, on the last word. She hated it. "So I can either break the rule I believe in, or keep it and not be there when it matters."

Dimitri didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he took his hands out of his pockets, and came across the roof, not quickly, and stopped beside her at the parapet. Not touching. A careful distance. The same distance she'd sat from Wren on the mat.

"You don't have to be the one who catches me," he said. "You're not a fire blanket, Juliette."

"That's not what I..."

"It is. A bit." He looked out at the parade ground. "Everyone keeps telling me what I'm for. A weapon. A monster. A story. A cure. My mother wrote me a whole letter asking me not to let anyone make me into something." He was quiet. "I'm not going to do it to you either."

She didn't trust herself to answer.

"So I'm not going to ask you to break your rule," said Dimitri. "And I'm not going to ask you to keep it. That's yours." A pause. "I'm just going to stand here. On the roof. A captain and a cadet in her squad, discussing his distance work. For as long as you want. And when you've decided, you can tell me."

The lamps burned in the dormitory windows across the parade ground. Somewhere below, the duty cadet was walking the corridor of the fourth-year halls, past the chair, the way duty cadets had walked it for two hundred years.

Juliette stood beside him at the parapet and didn't decide.

After a while, without either of them doing anything at all about it, the air between them went warm.

---

She went to Varga's office at the last bell.

She hadn't planned to. She'd put the report under Thorne's door, the way he'd asked, and turned to go back to her quarters, and instead found herself walking the other way along the command corridor, past the review room with its thin door, to the end, where there was a light under Varga's.

She knocked.

"Come."

The commander was sitting at her desk with the lamp lit and the window behind her black. She was not working. There was nothing on the desk in front of her. She was just sitting, with her hands folded, the way she sat in every review and every meeting and, Juliette was beginning to suspect, in every empty room.

She did not look surprised.

"Captain," she said.

"Ma'am."

"Sit, if you like."

Juliette stayed standing.

"Colonel Thorne restricted my squad," she said, "and you let him."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Varga looked at her for a long moment.

"Because he was right," she said. "Your squad has been walking in and out of my gate like a family going to market. One of them visits hollows. One of them sits in the Assembly. One of them is the most watched cadet on Eta, and the Assembly sits on him in nineteen days, and there are people in this city with silver ships on their collars who would very much like to see him do something foolish before then." Her voice was dry. "Colonel Thorne has given you a reason to stay where I can see you, and I can protect you, and nobody can say I'm doing it. I'd have done it myself, if he hadn't. He saved me the trouble."

Juliette stood very still.

That was an answer. It was even, she thought, probably true.

It was not the question she had come to ask.

She thought about the pumping station. The cracked slate. *Look after my bell. You owe me one.* She thought about Halloran, and the Shore Order, and an envelope inside Susan's jacket that hadn't been opened in three hundred years. About four words written on a hill one minute before a man burned his own seed out.

She could ask all of it. Right now. In this room.

She opened her mouth.

"Did he ask you?" she said. "Idris Kane. Before he went. Did he ask you to look after Dimitri?"

The desk lamp buzzed faintly.

Varga did not move. Her hands stayed folded. Her face stayed still. But Juliette, who was the air, and who was listening, heard something happen in the room. A breath. Very small. Taken, and held.

"Yes," said Varga.

"You never told him."

"No."

"Why not?"

Varga looked at her for a long time.

"Because a boy who knows he's a debt," she said quietly, "spends his life wondering whether anyone is kind to him for his own sake. I didn't want that for him." A pause. "I've had nineteen years to decide whether that was the right choice, captain. I still haven't." Her grey eyes held Juliette's. "How did you know to ask?"

Juliette didn't answer.

Varga waited. Then, slowly, something that was almost a smile touched the corner of her mouth. Not a happy one.

"No," she said. "Of course. You won't tell me." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the empty desk. "Is there anything else you'd like to ask me, Captain Vasquez?"

And there it was.

The door. Standing open. Everything behind it. Halloran. The Order. The Undercroft. *They're still down there.* Varga would answer, Juliette thought. Or she wouldn't. Either way, once it was asked, it could not be unasked, and the commander would know exactly how much her captain knew, and Juliette would know exactly what her commander chose to say about it.

She thought about Dimitri on the roof. *That's yours.*

She thought about a narrow place to stand.

"No, ma'am," she said.

Varga looked at her for a very long time.

"Good," said the commander softly. "The things you don't ask me, I never have to lie to you about." She turned her face to the black window. "Goodnight, captain."

Juliette went out, and closed the door, and stood in the dark corridor with her back against it.

She stood there for a long time, listening. To the base, asleep around her. To the duty cadet's footsteps in the far wing. To the wind coming off the sea along the top of the old wall, gusting at the third buttress, where a small grey pigeon in the third crack from the end had turned its back to the cold.

And behind the door, in the empty office, to Commander Ilse Varga, sitting perfectly still at her desk, letting out the breath she had been holding for nineteen years.
