---

The crow came to her window two hours after the last bell.

Juliette was awake. She was usually awake at that hour now. She lay on her back on the narrow bed in her quarters with her hands folded on her stomach and listened to the base sleep, the way she had listened every night since the review: three hundred cadets breathing in their beds, the duty cadet's footsteps on the far wing, the wind off the sea gusting at the third buttress of the old wall. Her report to Thorne sat in its envelope on the corner of her desk. Her squad sat in her head, four small warm weights she could feel without trying. Romeo, confined, asleep at last, in the fourth-year halls. Susan, very still, in a room of four. Dimitri in the late bloomers' wing at the end of the east corridor, restless, turning over.

And then something hit the glass.

Not hard. A tap. Then another. Then three in quick succession, sharp and deliberate, like a knuckle on a door.

She was on her feet before the third. She crossed the room in two steps and pulled the curtain back.

Sergeant was on her windowsill.

He was huge in the dark, his feathers puffed against the cold, his one white wing feather catching the light from the parade ground lamps. He looked at her through the glass with his flat black eyes. Then he tapped again. Once. Hard.

She opened the window. The cold came in. The crow did not come in with it. He stayed on the sill, and leaned forward, and took the cuff of her sleeve in his beak, and pulled.

"Sergeant," she said. "What..."

He let go of her sleeve. He hopped to the very edge of the sill. He looked back at her over his shoulder, and then out across the parade ground, towards the far side of the base, where the Command Towers stood dark against the sky: three tall grey shafts of stone at the western end of the parade ground, where High Command kept its offices and almost nobody under the rank of colonel ever went.

Then back at her.

Then at the towers.

Then he dropped off the sill, spread his wings, and was gone into the dark. She heard him land somewhere below. She heard him wait.

Juliette stood at the open window in the cold, in her shirt and her bare feet, and felt her heart do something she did not let it do.

Romeo was confined to barracks. Romeo could not go anywhere. But Romeo had a crow who went everywhere, and nobody stopped a crow, and nobody looked at one.

And Romeo had sent him to her.

She dressed in the dark in under a minute. Boots. Grey. No jacket; a jacket rustled. She left her sidearm in the locker, because the Choir logged every sidearm that left a captain's quarters after the last bell, and took the knife instead, because the Choir did not. She opened her door. The corridor was empty and lit low. She listened, and heard nobody, and went.

---

It was a long way across the base at night, if you wanted nobody to see you.

She did not go across the parade ground. Nobody went across the parade ground after the last bell; the duty officer's window looked straight out onto it, and every flagstone was lit. She went along the back of the north wing instead, under the windows, where the old wall ran close, and the gap between them was full of shadow. Then through the kitchen yard, past Ordo's bins. Then along the inside of the western wall, keeping low, keeping slow, letting the air flow round her instead of pushing through it, so that she made no more ripple in the night than a moth.

Sergeant led. A black shape ahead of her in the dark, flying short distances, landing on a wall or a drainpipe or a bin, waiting till she caught up, and flying on. Never far. Never making a sound.

At the foot of the tallest Command Tower he stopped. He landed on an iron drainpipe that ran up the side of the tower all the way to the roof, and looked at her. Then he looked up.

Juliette looked up.

The tower was dark, all the way up. All except one window, very near the top, on the fifth floor, where a light was burning behind a drawn blind. Electric light: the hard white light of the command offices, not the yellow of a lamp. And beside the window, set into the stone, a square iron grille. A ventilation shaft. The old towers had dozens of them, from the days before the network, when the rooms had needed to breathe.

She understood.

The crow couldn't hear what was being said in that room. Neither could Romeo. Nobody could, from outside. Not through stone and glass and a drawn blind, five floors up.

Nobody except someone who could hear the air.

"Good bird," she breathed.

Sergeant clicked once, very quietly, as if to say *obviously*, and flew up and away into the dark to wait on the roof.

Juliette put her hands on the drainpipe, and climbed.

---

She had climbed the wall run nine hundred times. The tower was easier. The drainpipe was old and solid and bolted to the stone every two metres, and the wind on this side of the tower was off the land, gentle, and she let it lift her a little on every pull. At the fourth floor she stopped, and pressed herself against the stone, and listened. Nobody below. Nobody on the parade ground. The duty officer's window, far away, dark.

She went on up. At the fifth floor, she stopped level with the iron grille, a metre to the side of the lit window, and hooked one arm round the drainpipe, and put her face close to the cold iron bars, and closed her eyes.

And listened.

It wasn't like hearing. It had never been like hearing. Hal Brennick had said it was like putting your hand flat on a table and feeling someone walk across the floor upstairs. The air in the room behind the grille moved, the way air always moved round people: when they breathed, when they shifted in their chairs, when they spoke. Voices pushed the air in waves, very small and very fast. And the waves came down the shaft, and through the grille, and into her.

She had done this exactly once before. In a stone hall, with a column of still fire in front of her and a voice in it that wasn't there. It had nearly split her head in half.

This was easier. This was only people.

Three of them.

"...not a request, General. It is a fact."

A woman's voice. Small and dry and precise. Juliette had heard it from the gallery of the Assembly through Susan's eyes, in Susan's careful retelling. She had heard it on every screen in Haven, opening sessions. She had never heard it like this. Tired, and very cold.

Speaker Annelise Halloran.

"Tell me the facts, then." A man. Big and slow and certain of himself. "Because what I've been told so far sounds a great deal like panic."

General Bram Hask. Defence Committee. The man who had wanted Dimitri registered as a weapon.

"Then listen," said the Speaker. "The dispatches came in from the Ridge at the ninth bell. Riots in three oasis towns in the Sere. Thel. Okkar. Sennamar. A Zetan crowd burned the human trading post at Okkar to the ground and the garrison only just got out. The war party in the Sere council is calling for the truce to be torn up. They have a name for him now. Did you know that? They call him *Kain-sereth*. The fire that was sent. And half of them think he was sent to finish what the last one started. To burn the desert to the bedrock."

"Then the Ambassador..."

"The Ambassador bowed to him. In front of every camera on the continent. And as of this evening, the Ambassador's own council is saying he bowed because he was afraid." A pause. "Sath is losing his seat. If he loses it, the hardliners take the Sere. And four Zetan regiments are already moving east towards the Ridge, General. Not in a month. Now. Colonel Thorne's own reports."

A third voice. Low and flat and very controlled.

"Four regiments confirmed," said Colonel Marcus Thorne. "Possibly six. The line at Post Four is reporting campfires on the far side of the Ridge for the first time since the truce."

Post Four. Juliette's hand tightened on the drainpipe. *Kesh Ridge Line, Post Four.* Eleven letters in her drawer.

"And what does the Sere want?" said Hask.

"Proof," said the Speaker. "That the boy is not a weapon. That we will not use him. That he will never stand on the Ridge with his hands raised." A long pause. "Sath has sent me a private message. He says if the boy is still able to light a fire at the turn of the month, he cannot hold his council. And there will be war."

The room was silent.

"Then we put him in a cell," said Hask. "Marr's motion. The Zetans can visit him if they like."

"A cell is not proof. A cell has a door. A cell has a boy in it who can melt the door." The Speaker's voice did not rise. "No, General. There is only one kind of proof the Sere will accept. And there is a procedure for it."

The air in the room went still.

Juliette felt it. Even through the shaft, even five floors up in the dark, she felt the room behind the grille go quiet in a way she had felt only a few times in her life. The quiet of people who already knew what was about to be said, and did not want it said.

"Integrity," said Colonel Thorne.

"Integrity," said the Speaker.

---

Juliette knew very little about the Choir Integrity Office. Nobody did. It was a door on the ground floor of the Assembly's network building, Susan had said, with no plate on it. People were reassigned there. Some came back. Magda Ferro had said: *they came back very quiet. If they came back.*

"Explain," said Hask. "For those of us who don't sit on Antiquities."

"The Integrity Office maintains the Choir," said the Speaker. "All of it. Including the seeds. And a seed is not only a listener, General. It is bound into the nerves. Into the marrow. It sits exactly where the omicrons sit." A pause. "Integrity has a procedure. It is very old. It is very rarely used. It uses the seed to reach the bound omicrons in a wielder's body and... unbind them. All of them. Permanently."

"You mean burn out his gift."

"I mean quiet it. The way you would quiet a bell by filling it with sand." The Speaker's voice was very tired. "He will live. He will be a perfectly ordinary young man. He will never light so much as a candle again. And we will be able to show the Sere a boy who can't."

Juliette's arm round the drainpipe had gone rigid.

"Is it safe?" said Hask.

The silence went on too long.

"It has been performed eleven times," said the Speaker. "In three hundred years."

"And?"

"And seven of them survived."

The wind came round the tower. Juliette did not move.

"Madam Speaker." Thorne. Very low. "He's a cadet."

"He is a war, Colonel."

"He's eighteen years old and he has done nothing but what he was ordered. He saved a woman's life in a burning chapel. He sat his trial in the open. He has not once, not *once*, raised his fire at a person." Thorne's voice did not rise either. But it shook, very slightly, at the bottom. "I have read every report his captain has filed. I know exactly what that squad does and doesn't do. You are proposing to take a boy with a four-in-ten chance of dying and put him on a table because the desert is frightened of a story."

"I am proposing," said Speaker Halloran, "to save ten thousand soldiers on the Ridge, and possibly the coast. I do not like it any more than you. I have sat in this office for eleven years trying to make sure that this choice would never have to be made by anyone. I failed." A pause. "The boy will be collected at the sixth bell. From his quarters. Quietly. By Integrity staff, with an escort of my own guard, not EDF. Commander Varga will be informed afterwards."

"Afterwards," said Thorne.

"Afterwards. Because if she is informed beforehand, Colonel, you and I both know exactly what she will do. And we cannot afford for her to do it."

Another silence.

"And his squad?" said Hask.

"Will be reassigned. Separately. To the frontier garrisons. By the end of the week." The Speaker's voice was flat. "The de Ricci girl to the southern basin, where her grandmother can see her. The Vasquez boy to the Reach outposts. And the captain to the Ridge."

Juliette shut her eyes.

"Post Four," said Colonel Thorne quietly. "You're sending her to Post Four."

"It has an instructor she trusts, I'm told. It seemed kind."

The air in the room shifted. A chair, pushed back. Footsteps.

"Then I want it in writing," said Thorne. "The order. With your name on it. Not mine."

"You'll have it by the fifth bell."

"Good," said the colonel, and the door opened, and closed, and he was gone.

---

Juliette came down the drainpipe without remembering doing it.

She was at the bottom, in the dark, with her back against the cold stone of the tower and her hands shaking, when Sergeant dropped out of the sky and landed on her shoulder. He had never landed on her shoulder before. Only Romeo's. He gripped hard, through her shirt, and leaned his head against the side of her neck, and stayed there.

She stood very still.

*The sixth bell.* Four hours.

She thought about Varga. About going straight to the end of the command corridor and knocking on that door. *Is there anything else you'd like to ask me, captain?* She thought about what Varga would do. What she *could* do. Overrule the Speaker of the Assembly? Defy the Shore Order's own keeper, with Zetan regiments moving to the Ridge?

She thought about Varga, sitting perfectly still in an empty office, letting nineteen years of breath out of her lungs. And she did not know. She did not know which way the heron would strike.

She thought about Thorne. *I want it in writing. With your name on it. Not mine.* He had argued. He had argued harder than she had ever heard anyone argue in a room with a Speaker. And then he had asked for the order in writing, so that when it was carried out it would not be his.

And then he had walked out. And he had not, as far as Juliette could tell, gone anywhere to warn anyone.

She could not trust any of them. Not Varga. Not Thorne. Not Okafor or Lisk with her boiled sweets. Not the bells or the corners or the reports, or the Board, or the Articles. All of it, everything, every single thing she had built her life on since she was nine years old, sat at the bottom of a chain of command that ended in a tired woman in a fifth-floor room deciding to fill a boy with sand.

And under the Speaker, older than the Speaker, was an envelope in Susan's jacket that nobody had opened in three hundred years.

*When I know who I'm working for,* she had said. *Or when I stop caring.*

She still didn't know who she was working for.

She found she had stopped caring.

"Sergeant," she said. "Get Romeo. Tell him to get dressed and come to the squad room. Quietly."

The crow lifted off her shoulder and was gone.

---

Susan was awake when Juliette came into the fourth-years' room. She was sitting up in her bunk in the dark, fully dressed, with her boots on, as if she had known.

The three other girls in the room slept on.

Juliette crouched by the bunk.

"The envelope," she breathed.

Susan looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached inside her shirt, where she had kept it against her skin every night since her grandmother had given it to her, and took it out.

"Here?"

"Squad room. Two minutes."

Susan nodded once and got up without a sound. She had learned, Juliette noticed, a great deal about moving quietly in the last two months.

---

Dimitri was awake. Of course he was.

He was sitting on the edge of his bed in the dark in the late bloomers' wing, fully dressed, with his boots on and his hands between his knees, when she opened the door. He looked up. He didn't say anything. He just looked at her face, and whatever was in it, he read all of it.

"How long?" he said.

"Three hours and forty minutes."

"For what?"

"Squad room," said Juliette. "I'll tell you there. All of you. Together."

He stood up without another word.

---

The squad room was dark. Juliette didn't turn on the light. Romeo was already there, sitting on the bench with Sergeant on his knee and no joke anywhere in his face. Susan came in behind her. Dimitri closed the door. Juliette told them, fast and flat, standing at the head of the table: the tower, the grille, the three voices. The riots in the Sere. *Kain-sereth.* The regiments on the Ridge. The procedure. Eleven times in three hundred years; seven survived. The sixth bell.

Nobody spoke when she finished. Dimitri had put his hand, without thinking, to the back of his own head, behind his ear.

Then she lit the stub of a candle from the drawer, because a candle didn't register on the base's power log and its light would show nothing from the parade ground. She set it on the table among the four of them. It flickered, restless, the way all fire on Psi flickered.

Susan laid the envelope down beside it.

*Beneath.*

"Are you sure?" said Susan.

"No."

Susan almost smiled. Then she picked up the envelope, and put her thumb under the dark green wax, and broke a seal that had not been broken in three hundred years.

It cracked like a twig.

Inside there were four sheets of thick old paper, folded together, brittle at the creases. The handwriting on them was careful and upright and very old, the ink gone brown. Some of the words were spelled strangely. Some were words Juliette didn't know. At the top of the first sheet, in larger letters, was a heading.

*THE ORDER OF THE SHORE, as agreed by the Founding Houses in Council, and sealed, and given into the keeping of each, that it may be kept and not read.*

Susan began to read aloud. Very quietly. Her voice did not shake.

Most of it was what Nonna had said. The twenty-two houses. The records brought to Council. The agreement that the coast should be taught as empty, *for the peace of the people and the keeping of the bargain*. The charters. The silence. The seats.

Then, on the third sheet, the handwriting changed. Became smaller. Tighter. As if the person writing it had been afraid of it.

*And be it known to the Keepers of this Order, and to them alone, the terms of the bargain made upon the shore at the coming of the ships, as the old records give them.*

*That the shore was given to the people of the ships by those who were before, who went up into the mountains, save some who stayed beneath, and keep the quiet places, and listen.*

*That the people of the shore shall keep the shore, and grow upon it, and be many, and want for nothing that the shore can give.*

*That should there ever be kindled among the people of the shore a still flame, which is the fire of those who were before, it is theirs and not ours. And the people of the shore shall deliver it beneath, to those who keep the quiet places. Or quench it. Before the year has turned.*

*And should the people of the shore fail in this, then the bargain is broken. And what was given shall be taken back.*

Susan stopped reading.

The candle flickered between them.

"Before the year has turned," said Juliette.

Her voice sounded strange to her.

"There's more," said Susan. Her face had gone very white. She turned to the last sheet. "It's a different hand again. Later. It says... it's an addition. Signed by the Keeper of the Order. Two hundred years ago. When the Choir was made."

She read it.

*That the Council, fearing the bargain might be broken in ignorance, has caused to be made a listening in every head, that it may be known at once if a still flame is kindled anywhere upon the shore. And that the listening shall be kept, and maintained, and never set aside, so long as the bargain stands.*

The squad room was utterly silent.

Juliette sat there with her hands flat on the table and looked at the brown words in the candlelight, and understood.

*A listening in every head.*

The hum behind her thoughts. Every day of her life. The time, her vitals, the map, the soft green certainty of who she was. Every human on Eta, seeded at three days old. Not for safety. Not for rationing. Not for crime, or water, or medicine.

For *him.*

The Choir had been built to find Dimitri. Two hundred years ago. Before he was born. Before his father was born. It had been listening for him all this time, in every head on the coast, waiting for a still flame to be lit anywhere, by anyone, so that the Keeper of the Order could deliver it beneath, or quench it, before the year turned.

It had never heard his father. She understood that too, all at once, the way you understand the shape of a room when a lamp comes on. Idris Kane's fire had been wild and ordinary when he wore the EDF's grey; it only went still after the Spine. And by then he had already burned the listening out of his own head on a hilltop, and walked north, off the edge of the shore, where the Order's reach ended. To the Choir, he was a hole in the world. Rumours. A Zetan story told at an oasis.

Dimitri was not a rumour. Dimitri was seeded, and on the shore, and had lit a still flame in a stone hall in front of every camera on the continent.

And tonight, the Keeper of the Order had chosen.

"We can't stay," said Susan. Very quietly.

"No."

"Not on base. Not in Haven. Not anywhere with a relay." Susan's hands were shaking now, on the old paper. "Juliette, it doesn't matter where he goes. If the Choir was built to hear him, it'll hear him. Every time he lights a fire. Every time his heart..." She stopped. "He's got a seed. We've all got seeds. They'll be able to follow us anywhere on the coast."

"Not anywhere."

Susan looked up.

"The Choir goes silent in the oldest places," said Juliette. "Ferro said so. Idris made a map." She looked at the candle. "There's a dead zone under this city that the Choir has never been able to hear. Not once in two hundred years."

"The Undercroft."

"The Undercroft. And the old drains run out of it under the whole of the Fourth District, all the way to the outfalls on the edge of the city. Past the walls. Romeo's rats know every one." Juliette was already standing. "Past the outfalls, it's farmland. Then the hills. Then the Reach." She folded the four old sheets carefully, along their three-hundred-year-old creases, and put them back in their envelope, and handed it to Susan. "Valleys too deep for the signal. Dropouts everywhere. Not dead zones. But not Haven either. Somewhere to breathe."

"And then?"

"I don't know," said Juliette. "I don't know, Susan. I just know he isn't going to be on a table at the sixth bell."

Susan looked at her for a long time in the candlelight.

Then she put the envelope back inside her shirt, against her skin. And stood up. And said, in her clear, level, de Ricci voice:

"My grandmother is going to be furious."

"Do you want to stay?"

"No," said Susan. "I want her to be furious with me in person. Someday."

By the window, Dimitri hadn't moved. He was staring at the bare patch of table where the envelope had lain, as if the words were still there, and his hand had gone back to the place behind his ear.

"It was built for me," he said.

"For anyone like you," said Juliette.

"There isn't anyone like me." His voice was very quiet. "There's me and there's him." He took his hand away from his head and looked at it. "Every day of my life. It was listening for this."

"Then let's not let it hear," said Romeo.

They all looked at him. He shrugged, very slightly, with Sergeant shifting on his knee.

"I've been confined to barracks for a week," he said. "I'm extremely ready to leave."

Juliette blew out the candle.

"Kitchen yard," she said. "A quarter past the third bell. Bring only what you can carry and climb with."

---

They met at the kitchen yard at a quarter past the third bell.

Romeo was there first, with Sergeant on his shoulder and a canvas bag over the other, and a face that had nothing in it at all. No joke. No grin. He looked at his sister as she came out of the dark and said only: "He told me. Sergeant. I saw the car through him, at the tenth bell. The Speaker's car. At the towers. I couldn't go. So I sent him to you."

"I know."

"Was I right to?"

Juliette looked at her twin brother in the dark. At the crow on his shoulder. At the bag he had packed without being asked.

"You were right," she said.

Susan came next, with her seed pouches on her belt, all six of them, full, and the envelope against her skin. Then Dimitri, last, out of the dark of the late bloomers' wing, with nothing in his hands at all. He stopped beside Juliette. Not touching her. Close.

"The kitchen gate," said Romeo. "Ordo's bins. Then the drain behind the motor pool. It runs under the wall and out to the canal. Biscuit's down at the bottom with three of Orla's rats. They'll take us to the hatch."

"The Choir will see us go," said Susan. "All four of us. Out of the base. At the third bell."

"Yes," said Juliette. "For about twenty minutes. Down the towpath. Into the Fourth. And then, at grid four-eleven-nine, all four of us go dark at once." She looked at them. "And by the time anyone looks at the log and understands what they're seeing, we'll be under the city."

Nobody said anything.

It was very quiet in the kitchen yard. The base slept around them. Three hundred cadets in their beds. The duty cadet on his chair in the corridor between the wings. The bells silent in their tower, waiting for the fifth.

Juliette thought about the parade ground at a quarter past five. About standing in front of her squad in the cold with her hands behind her back while the duty officer looked through her. About the captains' table, and Ordo's sugar cubes, and the Long Dormitory, and the wall run, and the stones she knew by their first names.

She thought about Wren Okoye, fourteen, asleep in the Long Dormitory, who would come to the foot of the old wall at the fifth bell on Restday and find nobody there.

"Wait," she said.

She went back across the kitchen yard, fast and silent, to the north wing, to the ground-floor window of the Long Dormitory, which was open a crack, as it always was, because thirty girls breathing made the room too warm. She took the black cloth out of her pocket. The one she tied over her eyes every night. Her mother's lullaby. *Clean the weapon, check the weapon.* She had carried it since she was six.

She tore a strip from the edge of the report she had meant to give Thorne, and wrote on it with the stub of a pencil, in the dark, by feel:

*Pigeons. Ten in a row. Then it isn't luck. J.V.*

She wrapped the cloth round it, and reached through the gap in the window, and laid it on the sill inside, where the first girl awake would find it, and somebody would know whose it was.

Then she went back to the others.

Nobody asked. Romeo looked at her face, and then away.

---

The drain behind the motor pool was narrow and cold and stank. They went through it on their hands and knees, Romeo first, then Susan, then Dimitri, then Juliette last, with her hand on the back of his boot so she'd know where he was in the black. It ran under the wall of the base. She felt it, overhead: three metres of stone, the edge of everything she had ever known, sliding past above her back like the hull of a ship.

Then it opened out onto the canal bank, and the cold air came, and the sky.

The dog was waiting at the bottom. Biscuit, brown and white, one ear up and one down, wagging her whole body at Romeo in silence. And three grey rats, sitting up on the towpath in a row like sentries.

And beyond them, standing very straight on the towpath, with its hands clasped behind its back, a figure in a plain grey coat.

They all stopped.

Observer Teyl did not move. In the darkness its face was almost invisible, only the faint pale gleam of the plates along its jaw, and the clear second eyelids sliding slowly across its eyes, and back.

"Captain Vasquez," it said.

Juliette's hand was on her knife. She didn't remember putting it there.

"Observer."

"I was in the Speaker's car tonight," said Teyl. "On the way to the towers. The Ambassador asked me to deliver his message by hand." Its voice was dry and precise, every word set down like a stone on a path. "I waited outside the room, in the corridor, for a long time. My hearing is very good. You know this."

Nobody breathed.

"I came to the canal," said Teyl, "because I calculated that this is the way a captain who could hear through stone would choose to take her squad out of a base whose gates she could not trust. Through the water. Under the wall." A pause. "I was correct. I am usually correct about you."

"Are you going to stop us?"

The Zetan looked at her for a long time. Its head tilted, very slightly. Listening.

"Your heart is very loud, Captain," it said. "For the first time since I have known you."

"Are you going to stop us?"

"I observe," said Observer Teyl. "It is what I am for." It unclasped its hands from behind its back. "Tonight, I observed nothing on the canal. A dog. Three rats. The water. Nothing else."

It stepped aside.

Juliette didn't move.

"Why?" she said.

Teyl was quiet for a moment.

"Because I carried water to the wounded at the Kesh Ridge for eleven days," it said. "And I would like, if it is possible, never to carry it again." Its eyes went past her, to Dimitri. "And because my people's story says the still flame will either end a war or begin one. And I do not think it will end anything, Captain, on a table in a room with no plate on the door."

The canal was black and still.

"Go," said Teyl. "Quickly. The Choir is still listening."

They went.

Romeo first, with the dog and the rats running ahead. Then Susan. Then Dimitri. Juliette last. As she passed the Zetan on the towpath, she stopped, for half a heartbeat, and put her open hand against her chest, and bowed from the neck. *I show you I carry nothing.*

Teyl looked at her. Then, slowly, it bowed back. Not from the neck. From the waist.

Then it turned and walked away up the towpath towards the bridge, and did not look back, and was gone.

---

The hatch at four-eleven-nine was exactly where it had always been: a round iron lid in the cobbles of a dead-end alley behind the bakery, rusted at the rim, with a ring set into it for lifting. Romeo hauled it up. Below, the iron rungs went down into the dark, and up out of the dark came the smell she remembered: wet stone, and under it, something older, dry and mineral, like the inside of a mountain.

They stood round it in a ring.

In the back of Juliette's head, the Choir hummed. Time. Vitals. Map. Four small green markers, standing in an alley in the Fourth District at a quarter to the fourth bell, which somebody, somewhere, in two hours or three or four, was going to look at and understand.

"Last chance," she said. "For anyone who wants to go back. You can still be in your bunk by the fifth bell. Nobody would ever know you'd been here."

Nobody moved.

"I'm confined to barracks," said Romeo. "I've broken that already. Might as well commit."

"My grandmother told me to keep the family's silence or break it," said Susan. "I've decided."

Dimitri didn't say anything. He just looked at Juliette, in the dark, and she looked back, and that was all.

Romeo went down first. Then Susan. Then Dimitri. Juliette stood alone at the top of the rungs with one hand on the cold iron rim and looked up.

The sky over the Fourth District was clear. More stars than over the rest of the city, because the streetlights here ran on rationed power. Somewhere behind her, across the canal, the base was sleeping. In two hours the fifth bell would ring, the way it had every morning for two hundred years, long before the sun was up, and three hundred cadets would get out of bed before they were ready, because that was the lesson. You would never be ready. Get up anyway.

She would not be there to hear it.

She climbed down into the dark.

At the bottom of the long sloping tunnel, where the floor flattened out and the walls began to curve, the silence came down over all four of them like a hand. The Choir went quiet. The time. The vitals. The map. The soft green certainty of who she was.

Gone.

For the first time in her life, nobody on Psi knew where Juliette Vasquez was.

She stood in the black with her squad around her, breathing, and found, to her great surprise, that she was not afraid of it at all.

"Light," she said.

And Dimitri opened his hand.
