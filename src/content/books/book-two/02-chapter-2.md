The Fourth District was burning by the time they reached the canal bridge.

Not all of it. One street. One building. But from the top of the bridge Juliette could see the column of smoke rising black and greasy out of the narrow streets beyond the dead tram stop, and the orange glow underneath it, and she could taste it on the wind before they were halfway across: wet wood, old paint, and something sharp and oily that caught at the back of her throat.

Dimitri tasted it too. She saw his face.

"Accelerant," he said. "Lamp oil. A lot of it. Poured."

"How do you know?"

"I can feel it." He was running beside her, and his voice was strange, tight. "It's like... it's like someone screaming in a room where everyone else is talking. It's loud and it's wrong and it doesn't care what it eats."

They came down off the bridge into the Fourth District, and the streets were full.

People. Hundreds of them, pouring out of every doorway, every alley, every stair. Old men in their nightclothes. Women with buckets. Children on shoulders. The district fire crew, six people and a single rusted pump cart, already fighting to get through the crush at the bottom of the stone steps. And over everything, the bells, still ringing from the canal towers, and the roar of the fire, and somewhere in the middle of it all, a voice Juliette knew, thin and high and furious, shouting.

"*Sister!* Sister Orla's inside! She went back for the box! *Somebody!*"

The boy. The one who'd carried two cans nearly as big as he was. He was at the top of the steps with his fists clenched, being held back by two men, screaming at the burning door.

And on the walls on either side of the steps, still wet, dripping red down over the gold flames that had been painted there four days ago, a word.

*MONSTER.*

---

The chapel was gone. That was the first thing Juliette knew, the instant she reached the top of the steps. The long low room, the benches, the pillar, the little iron stove: all of it was a single mass of fire, roaring out of the doorway and the cracked windows and the gaps in the old roof. The painted ship over the door had blistered black. The blue of the door was gone.

The second thing she knew, a heartbeat after, was the air.

She let it come to her, the way she had since she was eight years old. Let it tell her everything. The fire was eating it, pulling it in through every gap in the building in a great hot gasping rush, and in that rush, in the shapes it made as it moved, she could read the inside of the building like a map drawn in breath. The main room: open, burning, nothing alive. The back wall, where a curtain had hung over a doorway: still standing. And behind it, a small room, with a little pocket of air in one corner that was moving very slowly, very shallowly, in and out.

Breathing.

"Back room," she said. "Behind the curtain. She's alive. On the floor, I think. Low."

"I can't get through that," said Romeo. He was staring at the doorway, at the solid wall of fire. "Jules, nobody can get through that."

Dimitri stepped past him.

He walked right up to the burning door. So close that Juliette felt the heat of it slam into her own face three paces back, so close that his hair should have caught, his skin should have blistered. It didn't. He stood there in the roar and the light and held out his hand, palm open, towards the fire.

"Dimitri," she said. "The Kiln. The rule..."

"There's no person in it," he said, without turning. "Just fire." And then, quieter: "I don't know if I can do this. I've never done it to fire that isn't mine."

"Then don't..."

He closed his hand.

---

She would see it a hundred times afterwards, in her head, and on the screens, because of course there were screens, there were always screens now; half the Fourth District had a recorder in its pocket. But in the moment, she only saw it once, and she would never quite believe it.

The fire stopped.

Not went out. *Stopped.* The whole roaring orange mass of it, pouring out of the door, froze. Every lick and tongue and leaping shred of it, held still in mid-air, as if someone had paused the world. And then, slowly, it changed colour. The angry, smoky orange went pale. Then gold. Then a deep, clear, steady gold she had seen only twice before: once above his palm in a cellar, and once filling a stone hall from floor to dome.

And then it began to move. Not outward. In.

It came out of the doorway towards him in a long, slow, shining river. Out of the windows. Down off the roof. Out of the burning benches and the burning walls and the burning pillar where his mother had sat. All of it, streaming through the smoke towards his outstretched hand like water running downhill, and pouring into him. Into his palm. His wrist. His chest.

And it was not still in him.

Juliette saw his face change. She saw his whole body jerk, the way a man jerks when he drinks something poisoned. He staggered. His knees bent. The river of fire kept coming, and he kept taking it, and under the gold she could see something else, something muddy and sick and orange, the taste of lamp oil and hate, going into him with it.

"Dimitri!"

"Get her out," he said through his teeth. "I can't... hold it... for long. Get Orla out. *Now.*"

Romeo was already moving.

He went in through the doorway with the last of the fire still pouring past him, and Juliette saw him do the thing he had done in the Kiln that morning: the wrong kind of movement, low and fast and four-legged, a hound's speed borrowed and worn. Behind him came Susan, white-faced, with her hands out in front of her. Something groaned inside the building. Something heavy. A beam, or a piece of the roof, and Juliette heard Susan cry out with effort, and the groaning stopped.

Juliette stayed where she was.

She stood two paces behind Dimitri, with every bit of air she had gathered round her, ready. Ready for what, she didn't know. Ready to try. Ready to pull everything she had and find out, again, that it did nothing.

It didn't come to that.

Romeo came out of the smoke with Sister Orla in his arms, small and grey and limp, her blue shawl scorched black at the edges. Susan was behind him, holding a burning section of roof beam up over both their heads with her hands outstretched and her face screwed tight, and the instant they were clear of the doorway she let it go, and it fell, and the last of the fire in it streamed off it like water and into Dimitri's hand.

And then there was no more fire.

The chapel stood there in the smoke, black and dripping and gutted. Dimitri stood in front of it with his hand still out.

Then his knees went, and Juliette caught him.

---

He was burning up. Not on the outside. His skin was cool, almost cold. But underneath it, when she put her good hand flat on his chest, she could feel it. A heat like an oven with the door shut. Moving. Churning. Not still.

"It's wrong," he said. His eyes were half closed. "It's not mine. It's... it doesn't fit. It tastes like oil. Juliette, it tastes like the person who poured it."

"Then let it go."

"Where?"

She looked round. The street. The crowd. Hundreds of faces. The steps. The wet red word on the wall.

"Up," she said. "Straight up. Nobody's up."

He lifted his hand towards the sky, very slowly, as if it weighed a great deal. And the fire came out of him.

Not gold. Not still. A thick, ugly, roaring column of dirty orange, shot through with black, howling up out of his palm into the grey morning sky like something escaping. It went up fifty metres. A hundred. The whole street lit orange. The crowd screamed and fell back. And at the very top it seemed to hang for a moment, writhing, and then simply came apart into smoke, and drifted away on the wind over the rooftops of the Fourth District, and was gone.

Dimitri sagged against her. She held him.

His skin was warm again. Normal warm. His breathing, slowly, came down.

"Better," he whispered.

"Better," she agreed.

---

It was Susan who saw the man.

Juliette was kneeling in the street with Dimitri's head against her shoulder, and Romeo was crouched beside Sister Orla on the cobbles with two of the women from the crowd, and the old woman was coughing, which meant she was breathing, and Juliette was letting herself feel, for one second, something that might have been relief.

Then Susan's hand closed on her shoulder.

"Juliette. On the roof. The laundry."

Juliette looked up.

There was a man on the flat roof of the laundry across the street. The same roof she had lain on with Dimitri in the dark, a lifetime ago, watching a blue door. He was crouched by the parapet, in a dark coat with the collar turned up, with a recorder in his hand pointed down at the street. Watching. Filming.

And when he saw her looking, he stood up, and turned to run.

"Stay with him," Juliette said to Susan. "Romeo, Orla."

She went.

---

She went up the laundry's fire escape in three breaths, with the air at her back pushing her up each flight like a hand. Her braced arm screamed. She ignored it. She came over the top of the parapet onto the roof and the man was already at the far end, at the edge, gathering himself to jump the gap to the next building.

She didn't think. She reached.

She took the air out from under his feet. Just a little. Just enough. A sudden thin patch of nothing under his boots as he pushed off, so that his jump came out short and low and wrong, and instead of landing on the next roof he hit the edge of it chest-first and hung there, scrabbling.

And then he turned his head and looked back at her, and Juliette felt the air on the roof go mad.

It came at her like a wall. A great flat blind slam of wind, out of nowhere, all at once, so hard it lifted her off her feet and threw her backwards across the roof into a water tank. She hit it with her bad arm. The world went white.

Air.

He was an air wielder.

She lay on the gravel of the roof with the breath knocked out of her and the white slowly clearing from her eyes, and she heard him haul himself up onto the next roof, panting, and turn round. She heard him gathering it again. Another wall. Bigger. She could feel it building, the air across both rooftops being dragged together, heaped up, like water behind a dam.

And she could feel something else, too.

He was loud.

Not his voice. His air. The way he moved it. Everything he did with it was a shove. A slam. A big, blunt, heavy push, the way a child pushes a door. He was strong. Stronger than her, maybe, in raw load; that first wall had been enormous. But he was holding the air the way you hold a rope you can't see. Blind. All grip and no feel. She could feel exactly where the wall was building, where it was thickest, where the edges were frayed and weak. She could hear it.

He couldn't.

He had no idea where she was. He was gathering the air to throw at the place where he'd last seen her fall. He wasn't listening to it. He'd never learned to listen to it. He was all hammer and no hand.

*Your gift is a very large hammer,* Hollis Brand had said that morning, standing over Dimitri in the dust. *It is not a hand.*

Juliette got up, very quietly. She moved three steps to the left, along the parapet, light, slow, letting the air flow round her instead of pushing it, so that she made no more ripple than a moth.

The second wall came. It hit the water tank where she'd been lying and crumpled it like paper.

And she was already over the gap.

She landed behind him. He felt that; even a deaf man feels a body land on a roof. He started to turn. She didn't give him time. She did the thing she'd done in a dark ballroom and an alley in the rain and a hundred training fights: she took a single thin thread of air and pushed it, very precisely, very hard, against the side of his knee, at exactly the moment his weight came onto it.

He went down. She was on him. Her good arm across his throat. Her knee in his back. And the air around his head, very gently, very deliberately, beginning to thin.

Not all the way. Not like Varra.

Just enough for him to feel it.

He went still.

"Who sent you?" she said into his ear.

He laughed. It came out wheezing, against the gravel. "Nobody *sends* us, captain. We come because we're called. Same as your fire boy."

"Who's *we*?"

She turned his head with her knee. His coat had fallen open. Pinned on the inside of the collar, where you'd only see it if you were looking, was a small enamel badge. A ship, silver, settling on a green shore. Under it, three words, in neat letters.

*THE SHORE WAITED.*

She knew it. Every child in Haven knew it. It was the picture from the schoolroom wall, the first lesson before letters. The silver ships, the empty coast. *And the land was empty, and the land was waiting, and the land was ours.*

"Shorekeepers," she said.

"Shorekeepers," said the man, and smiled against the gravel. "We keep the true story, captain. The one they taught you in school, before the desert people and the chapel women and the Assembly cowards started telling you different. The shore was empty. The shore was ours. We didn't bow to anybody to get it." His voice dropped, venomous. "And we will not kneel to some Zetan fairy tale with a lighter in his hand."

Juliette looked at the badge for a long moment.

"You burned a chapel," she said. "With an old woman inside."

"We burned a shrine," he said. "To a monster. She shouldn't have gone back in."

The air around his head went thin.

She felt it happen. She didn't decide it. It just happened, the way it had happened in an alley in the rain: her anger reached out, all on its own, and took hold of the air around his face, and began, very slowly, to pull. His eyes went wide. His mouth opened. Nothing came in.

And there it was. The clean cold space. The swept room. The quiet where the guilt should be.

*Nobody up here,* it said. *Nobody watching. Except the Choir, and the Choir only sees your heart, and your heart is very, very calm.*

The man was making a sound now. A thin, high, whistling sound. His heels drummed on the gravel.

*He burned a chapel with an old woman inside. He'd have burned Dimitri if he could. He deserves it.*

She heard it in her own voice, in her own head, perfectly reasonable.

And under it, very faint, she heard something else. Dimitri's voice, in a cellar, in the dark, with a little gold flame between them. *I don't think there's nothing in there, Juliette. I think there's so much you had to close the door on it to keep standing up.*

She let go.

The air rushed back into the man's lungs with a sound like a sob. He lay there on the roof gasping, heaving, his face against the gravel, and she knelt on his back and looked down at her own hand, at the fingers of her good hand, which were shaking.

They had never shaken after Varra.

She watched them shake, and did not know whether to be frightened or glad.

---

Varga's soldiers took him away twenty minutes later, along with two others the crowd had caught running from the back of the laundry with empty oil cans. Juliette came down the fire escape slowly. Her arm was very bad again. She didn't care.

The street had changed while she was on the roof.

The crowd was still there. Hundreds of people, packed into the narrow street in front of the black wet shell of the chapel. But they weren't running anymore, or shouting. They were quiet. And they were all looking at the same thing.

Dimitri was sitting on the bottom step, with a blanket round his shoulders that someone had given him and a cup of water in his hands that someone else had given him. Sister Orla was sitting beside him, wrapped in another blanket, her face streaked with soot, her shawl ruined, leaning against his arm. Romeo and Susan stood on either side of them like guards.

And the people of the Fourth District were kneeling.

Not all of them. Not even most. But dozens. Old men in their nightclothes. Women with buckets still in their hands. The boy who had screamed at the door, kneeling on the wet cobbles with his hands open on his knees and tears on his face. And on the wall beside the steps, over the dripping red word, somebody was already painting. A girl, maybe twelve, with a pot of yellow paint and a brush, carefully, painstakingly covering *MONSTER* with a single gold teardrop flame.

Juliette stopped at the edge of the crowd.

She had seen soldiers look at a commander like that. She had seen people look at a priest like that, at the Sown altars, at the Wayfarer services on the canal. She had never seen anybody look at a person like that. Not a frightened eighteen-year-old cadet with soot on his face and a blanket round his shoulders, who was staring down at the cup in his hands and very clearly wished they would all stop.

She was supposed to feel proud, she thought. Or relieved. Or something.

What she felt was cold, all the way through. Because she knew what a city did with a symbol. It built it up, and up, and up, until it was big enough to set on fire.

*Monster* on one wall. A gold flame on the other. And a boy on the steps in between, who was neither.

---

Sister Orla caught her hand as she came up the steps.

The old woman's grip was surprisingly strong. Her eyes, in her sooty, creased face, were sharp and clear and kind.

"Captain," she said hoarsely. "Your young man walked into my fire and drank it."

"He's not my..." Juliette stopped. "Yes, Sister. He did."

"I went back for this." Orla's other hand came out from under the blanket. In it was a small wooden box, the size of a book, its lid scorched and blistered, its corners black. She held it out, not to Juliette, but to Dimitri, beside her. "Your mother left it with me, boy. A long time ago. Before you were born. She said if you ever came back to the chapel, and asked about your father, I was to give it to you." She coughed. "You came back. You never asked. You just sat on her bench and listened to the singing."

Dimitri looked at the box. He didn't take it.

"What's in it?" he said.

"I never looked," said Sister Orla. "I promised her." She pressed it into his hands. "I nearly died for it this morning. So I would be grateful if you'd open it somewhere quiet. When you're ready. And not in front of half the Fourth District."

Dimitri held the box in both hands. His face was very still. Juliette, beside him, could feel the air around him, and under the stillness it was trembling.

"Thank you," he said. "Sister. For going back."

"I've known you since you were a lump in your mother's belly, Dimitri Gale." Sister Orla patted his knee. "Of course I went back."

Around them, in the wet grey street, the people of the Fourth District knelt in silence. And on the wall by the steps, the twelve-year-old girl with the pot of yellow paint stepped back, and looked at her flame, and was satisfied.

It was a little crooked. It didn't flicker at all.
