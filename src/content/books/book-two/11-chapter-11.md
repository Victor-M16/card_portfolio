The first thing Romeo lost was the rats.

Three of Orla's rats had come down the hatch with them, scrabbling down the rungs ahead of his boots, and all the way down he had been holding the three of them lightly in his head, the way you hold a hand in a crowd: three small hot minds, busy and greedy and very unhappy about the drop.

Then Dimitri opened his hand, and a coin of gold fire bloomed above his palm, and the rats went out like lamps.

Not dead. He could hear them at his feet, scrabbling on the stone. But the threads between him and them had simply gone. He reached for the nearest of them and found nothing, the way you reach for the bottom stair in the dark and it isn't there. It was like trying to hear through a wall of wet wool.

"Jules," he said.

"I know." His sister's voice was very quiet. "Me too. I can't feel the air."

Romeo looked at the flame.

It sat above Dimitri's palm without moving. Not a flicker. Not a lean. Every other fire Romeo had ever seen, every candle and stove and lamp on Psi, twitched and fretted like a dog in its sleep. This one didn't. It just sat there, gold and patient, and drank.

Somebody had explained it to them weeks ago, Pell or Teyl, with jars and needles and a great deal of muttering: the still flame didn't burn air. It burned omicrons. Romeo had thought at the time that it was an interesting fact. He had not understood that it meant this. That standing next to the fire boy with the fire lit was like standing next to a waterfall and trying to hear a mouse.

"Sorry," said Dimitri.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Romeo turned slowly on the spot. "Hold it up. I've never actually seen this place with my own eyes."

That was true. He had been in the Undercroft a hundred times, but never as himself. He had been in it as rats, who did not care about walls, and who saw the world as a thing made of smells and edges and the warm places where food had once been dropped. Rats remembered the Undercroft as the place where nothing lived. They went through it fast and never stopped.

Now he stood in it, and he saw why.

It was a round chamber, wider than the squad room, and much higher, with a domed roof lost in the dark above the reach of the flame. The floor was worn smooth, dipping towards the middle the way a step dips after a thousand years of feet, except that it had not been feet; it had been water, once, long ago, running in and out of the four tunnels that opened off it like the points of a compass. The air was dry. It smelled of stone. Not wet stone, like the drains, but something older underneath it, mineral and clean, like the inside of a mountain that had never been opened.

And the walls.

Romeo had seen Mira's drawing, on the table in the squad room, with Dimitri's hands shaking beside it. He had thought he knew what to expect.

The drawing had been beautiful. The walls were not beautiful. They were too big for that. The ships came down out of a sky of carved stars, and the small figures spilled out of them carrying bundles and children, and on the shore the tall ones waited in a long line with their hands raised and a flame in every hand, and every one of the tall ones was three times the height of a man and worn so smooth by time that their faces were almost gone. Almost. In the gold light Romeo could see where the eyes had been. They were all looking down. At the people coming off the ships. At the people who were, he supposed, him.

"Huh," he said.

Nobody said anything else. Susan had her hand flat over her shirt, where the envelope was. Juliette was looking at the second wall, where the tall ones walked away into the mountains. Dimitri was looking at the fourth.

Romeo didn't look at the fourth. He had seen it in the drawing. A lone figure on a ridge with his hands raised and a column of still fire going up off the top of the stone, and he had decided, on the night Dimitri opened the box, that it was not his business to look at that one for very long. Some things belonged to a person.

He looked at Dimitri instead.

The fire boy was standing very straight in the middle of the oldest room on the coast, with a still flame floating over his hand, and he looked about fourteen. Romeo had worked out what Dimitri Gale was weeks ago, on a roof at sunset, and been very pleased with himself about it. Not an animal at all. The fire on the hill. The thing every creature in the Reach was afraid of, and came back to anyway, when it was cold.

He had not, at the time, thought about what it would actually be like to stand beside the fire on the hill, underground, in the dark, and feel every small life he was holding go quiet around it.

Animals were frightened of fire. All of them, every one he had ever been inside. He had known that his whole life. He was only now finding out what it meant to be standing next to one.

Juliette, at least, had never been hard. She had been a hawk since they were six years old. She was being one now, at the wall, watching everything.

"Right," said his sister, and turned round from the wall. "Listen. All of you. This is the last fire until we're somewhere safe."

Dimitri's head came up.

"Down here, it doesn't matter. Nothing hears down here. But the drains beyond this room aren't the Undercroft. They're just deep." Juliette looked round at the four tunnels. "The Choir might come back in places. Faint. If it does, and you've got that lit, then for one second, somewhere in the Assembly's network office, every bell they've got rings at once." She held his eyes. "That's what it was built for. Two hundred years of listening for exactly that. Don't give it the one thing it's waiting for."

Dimitri looked at the flame on his palm.

"How do we see?"

"We don't." Juliette looked at her brother. "Romeo does."

Romeo sighed, enormously, because somebody had to.

"Three hundred cadets at the Academy," he said, "and every one of them gets told on their first day that the fauna wielder is the useless one. *Can he set a wall on fire? No. Can he lift a lorry? No. Can he talk to a pigeon? Incredible.*" He crouched, and felt the rats nose at his boots, warm, and nothing else. "And then the one time in history the whole squad has to escape through a sewer in the pitch dark, guess who's in charge."

"Romeo."

"I'm just saying. I'd like it on record."

"There isn't a record. That's the point."

"Then I'd like it on *your* record, Jules. In your head. Forever." He nodded at Dimitri. "Go on. Put it out."

Dimitri closed his hand.

The dark came down like a lid. Total. Not the dark of a room at night, where your eyes find a window after a while. The dark of being underground, of having no light anywhere at all. Romeo heard Susan's breath catch. He heard his own heart.

And then, like a door opening in his chest, the rats came back.

---

He let two of them be. He went into the third, the boldest, a scarred grey doe who bit.

Rats were easy. He had been doing rats since he was five years old, on a base at the edge of somewhere, lonely, with a crust in his hand. You didn't have to coax a rat. You just had to be interesting to it, and a boy with a pocket was very interesting indeed. He felt her mind open round him like a small warm room, crowded and busy and full of opinions about the dark, and he settled into it. Not all the way. Never all the way. He kept one hand on his own feelings, the way he'd kept his hand on Sergeant's feathers on the towpath, and let only the edges of her come into him.

Her whiskers. Her nose. That was all he wanted.

The dark lit up.

Not with light. With *shape*. The air in the chamber turned into a map, each breath of it carrying something: the stone, the four tunnels, the faint draught from the north one where the air moved because somewhere, a long way off, there was an opening. The old water smell from the west tunnel. Under it, fainter, the thing Compline wanted most in the world, which was food, which was the outfalls, which was the edge of the city where the drains came out into the river and there were always things washed down to eat.

*West,* said the doe, in the way rats said things, which was not in words but in a great, greedy, forward-leaning *want*.

"West," said Romeo. "The left-hand tunnel, from where Jules is standing. Hand on the shoulder in front. Don't let go. If you let go, say so, out loud, and stand still, and I'll come back for you." He paused. "Susan, you're behind me. Then Dimitri. Then Jules."

"Why am I last?" said Juliette.

"Because you're the only one who can feel if something's following us, and because you'll complain the whole way, and I'd rather have it behind me."

He felt Susan's hand settle on his shoulder. Light and cool and very steady, the way she did everything. He felt the chain form behind her, hand to shoulder to shoulder, four people in the dark, and he put the rat down on the floor of the tunnel and let her go ahead of him on the end of a thread, and walked.

---

He would remember the drains for the rest of his life, and none of it would be pictures.

He would remember the smells. The dry clean stone of the Undercroft giving way, step by step, to the wet brick of the old drains: moss and old iron and the cold green breath of water that had been standing still for a hundred years. He would remember the floor changing under his boots from smooth to rough to slime. He would remember the doe, always a little ahead, a hot bright point in his head, pulling.

And he would remember the sounds. Water dripping. Their breathing. Susan's soft "step down" passed back along the line every time he said it. Dimitri's boots, heavy and careful. And from the back, very quietly, at regular intervals, his sister.

"Romeo."

"Mm."

"Are you sure this is west?"

"No, Jules. I'm taking us to the Zetan border by way of the sea. It's a scenic route."

"I'm only asking because the air's coming from behind us now."

He stopped. Compline stopped too, a little way ahead, her nose working. Juliette was right; the draught had shifted. But the rat wasn't worried. The rat was certain. Rats were not often wrong about where the food was.

"The air's wrong," he said. "The rat's not. Trust the rat."

"I don't trust the rat."

"Then trust me trusting the rat."

A pause in the dark.

"Fine," said his sister, and he grinned, because he couldn't help it, and walked on.

The tunnels went on and on. They branched and joined. Some were brick, low and round, so that he had to stoop and pass the warning back. Some were older, cut straight into grey rock, the walls marked with the faint ridged lines of water levels that had risen and fallen for centuries. Compline knew them all. She knew which ones flooded. She knew which ones ended at a grating. She knew one that went down a long slope into black water that smelled of nothing, and she would not go near it, and Romeo did not ask her why.

Once, at a fork, she stopped dead.

He felt it in her before he understood it: not fear, exactly. Something flatter than fear. A refusal, the way a dog will sometimes refuse to walk past a particular house on a particular street and nobody ever knows why. She sat back on her haunches at the mouth of the right-hand passage and did not move, and when he nudged her, gently, she turned her whole body away from it and pressed herself against the left-hand wall.

"What?" said Susan behind him.

"Nothing. She doesn't like that one."

"Why not?"

Romeo looked into the right-hand passage, which he could not see, through a rat who would not look at it. The stone there felt different under his fingertips: smoother, colder, older. The air coming out of it was very still. It did not smell of anything at all.

"No idea," he said. "Rats have opinions."

They took the left.

---

It was the hum that frightened him.

They had been walking for what felt like an hour, though there was no way to know, when he heard it. Not with his ears. In the back of his head, the place where it had always lived, where for his whole life there had been a soft constant presence like the sea heard from a house on the cliff: time, vitals, map, the faint green certainty of being himself. It had gone silent at the bottom of the hatch. He had not missed it. He had not expected to feel, as he climbed up a short brick slope behind a rat, a tiny thread of it come back.

A flicker. Like a lamp lit in a window very far away.

*A quarter to the fifth bell. Vitals: elevated. Position: Fourth District, grid...*

"Back," said Juliette. Sharp. "Back down. Now."

The whole line went backwards down the slope at once, stumbling, Dimitri's heel on Susan's toe, Susan's hand tight on Romeo's shoulder, and at the bottom, in the old stone, the thread snapped, and the silence came down again.

They stood there breathing.

"How long?" said Dimitri. His voice was not steady.

"Two seconds," said Juliette. "Three." She was quiet a moment. "Long enough."

Romeo knew what she meant. He'd seen enough Choir logs over his sister's shoulder to know how they looked. Four green markers, gone dark at grid four-eleven-nine. And then, an hour later and a few hundred metres west, a little flicker: four markers, for three seconds, under the Fourth District, moving away from the hatch. Somebody would see it. Somebody, sooner or later, would sit at a screen and draw a line from one point to the other and know exactly which way the line went.

"They'd have guessed west anyway," said Susan.

"Guessing isn't knowing." Juliette's voice was flat. Then she breathed out, and it went less flat. "It doesn't matter. It's done. Romeo, can she find a way that stays deep?"

He asked.

The doe, who did not know what the Choir was and would not have cared if she had, considered the question in her own terms, which were *the cold stone* and *the warm brick*, and turned round, and led them back the way they had come, and down a side passage none of them had noticed, and on.

It took a long time. They went lower. The brick gave way again to the old grey rock, and the air got colder, and the floor got wetter, until they were walking ankle-deep through still water that came up through Romeo's boots and into his socks and made him think, very clearly and with great longing, of Ordo's stew.

Except he didn't think of stew.

He thought of the *bins*. Ordo's bins, behind the kitchen yard. The good one on the end, where the bread crusts went. The way the lid didn't quite close. The smell of it on a warm evening, sour and sweet and wonderful.

He stopped walking.

"Romeo?" said Susan.

He stood in the water in the dark, with his own mouth full of spit, thinking about the inside of a kitchen bin as though it were the best place on Psi.

"Nothing," he said. "Sorry. I'm fine."

He pulled back. Not out of the rat; he still needed her. But away from her, a little. He made himself think about his own hands. His own feet, cold and wet in his own boots. His own name. He felt Compline's hunger slide back down to where it belonged, a small hot point ahead of him in the dark, and not a thing he wanted himself.

*It's like wearing someone else's coat,* he had told Dimitri, once, grinning. *If you leave it on too long, it starts to fit.*

He hadn't been grinning on the inside.

Susan's hand had tightened on his shoulder. She didn't say anything. But it stayed tight, after that, for a long time.

---

They heard the sixth bell under the Third District.

It came down a shaft. An old vertical drain, with a grating somewhere far above, and a little grey square of something that was not quite light. Down it, through the stone, faint and thin and very far away, came the sound of a bell.

Then another. Then all of them.

Haven had a hundred bell towers. The Assembly's, the chapels', the old harbour bell, the tram depots. And one of them, a long way east, over a canal and a wall and a parade ground, was the bell on the north tower of the Academy, which had rung the sixth bell every morning for two hundred years, and which Romeo had heard every single morning of his life since he was fourteen, and which he would have known anywhere, by its crack.

They all stood still in the water and listened to it ring six times.

Nobody said what they were all thinking. Romeo thought it anyway. He couldn't stop himself. A corridor, lit low, at the end of the east wing. Footsteps, more than a duty cadet's. A door with a card on it in the late bloomers' wing. People who were not EDF, in grey that was not EDF grey, opening it. An empty bed. The blanket thrown back.

And three more doors, after. His own, in the fourth-year halls, with his bed made, because Juliette had made it for him while he was packing. She had made it to inspection standard. Corners and everything. He hoped somebody from Integrity noticed. He hoped they ran a white glove along the frame and found it clean.

The bells stopped. Their echoes went on a little longer in the shaft, and then those stopped too.

In the dark behind him, he heard Dimitri breathe out.

"Thank you," said the fire boy. Very low. Not to anyone in particular. To all of them.

"Don't," said Romeo. "Honestly. I'm only here because they confined me to barracks and the barracks were boring." He found the doe again, a little way ahead, waiting. "Also you owe me about nine cinnamon knots. I'm keeping a list."

Dimitri laughed. It wasn't much of a laugh. It shook. But it was one.

"Come on," said Juliette, from the back. "Walk."

They walked.

---

The outfall, when they reached it, was a round iron grating set across the end of the tunnel like the bars of a cage, and behind it, the world was grey.

The silence held, even here. The doe had brought them the deep way, through the old grey rock, right to the end; the brick of the city's newer drains ran somewhere above them, and the Choir with it, and down here at the very lip of the stone there was still nothing in Romeo's head but himself.

Romeo stood with his hands on the cold bars and looked out. He could see. That was the first thing. Not much. A wide shallow river, slow and brown, the colour of strong tea. Reeds. A mud bank. Willows on the far side, bent over the water. And over all of it, the flat grey light of a morning that had not yet decided whether to bother.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. It also hurt.

He hadn't expected that. He blinked against it, and blinked again, and the light kept coming in, much too bright, much too wide, from everywhere at once. His eyes watered. Some old part of him, very small and very low down, wanted to turn round and go back into the dark where things made sense.

*Ah,* he thought. *Still wearing it.*

He crouched down in the water inside the grating and held his hand out flat on the wet ledge, and the doe climbed onto it, and he lifted her up in front of his face. She looked at him with her black bead eyes, whiskers going.

"Thank you," he said. "Really. You were magnificent. Go home. Tell Orla we're all right." He thought about it. "Tell her not to tell anyone. Not even the dog."

He let her go.

It was like setting down something he'd been carrying uphill. He felt her mind slide out of him, all of her, the hunger and the cold-stone-warm-brick and the great greedy wanting, and he felt the gap she left, and he stayed very still and let himself fill it back up with himself. The other two came out of the dark to join her. The three of them sat in a row on the slimy ledge for a moment, looking at him. Then they turned and ran back up the tunnel into the dark, and were gone.

He stood up.

The light still hurt. He kept his eyes narrowed against it. Behind him he heard the others coming up, one after another, into the grey: Susan with her hand over her eyes; Dimitri, pale and huge in the doorway of the tunnel; Juliette last, already looking past him at the grating, at the hinges, at the bank beyond.

"Can we open it?" she said.

"It's rusted to the stone. Susan could..."

Something black dropped out of the sky and landed on the grating a foot from his face.

Romeo's whole body went cold.

His stomach turned over. His heart slammed against his ribs. Every hair on the back of his neck stood up, and some deep, small, screaming part of him was already moving, already turning, already looking for the dark, the gap, the hole, anywhere, anywhere, *a crow, a crow, it's a crow, it'll have your eyes*...

He was halfway back down the tunnel before he knew he'd moved.

He stopped. He stood in the water with his back against the wall and his hands flat against the stone, shaking, and looked back towards the grating.

Sergeant sat on the iron bars in the grey morning light. Huge and black and ruffled, with his one white wing feather. Looking at him.

Just looking.

"Romeo?" Juliette had her hand on her knife.

"It's fine." His voice came out very strange. He made it come out less strange. "It's fine. It's just him. I'm fine. I just..." He laughed. It didn't work. "Rats don't like crows. Turns out."

Nobody laughed with him.

He made himself walk back up the tunnel. One step. Then another. He made himself come right up to the grating, close, until he was face to face with the bird through the bars, and he made himself hold his hand out, palm up, the way he had a thousand times since he was nine years old.

Sergeant looked at the hand for a long moment.

Then the crow leaned forward, very slowly, and pressed the top of his head into Romeo's palm. Not pecking. Not clicking. Just leaning. The way he'd leaned into Juliette's neck at the foot of the Command Tower.

Romeo stood there with his hand on his own bird and waited for the fear to go.

It went. Slowly. Like water draining out of sand. By the time it was gone, Susan had her palm flat against the rusted hinge of the grating and her eyes closed and her jaw tight, and the hinge was groaning, and Juliette was watching him instead of the hinge, with an expression he knew very well.

It was the one she had worn at nine years old, at the top of a gully at the edge of a war, just before she punched him in the face.

"How long did that last?" she said quietly.

"Not long."

"How long, Romeo?"

"I don't know, Jules. A minute. It's gone." He met her eyes. "It's gone. Really. They always go."

She looked at him for a long time. She didn't say *take it off before you come home.* She didn't need to. He could hear her not saying it.

The hinge broke.

---

They didn't go out. Not yet.

"Look," said Romeo, and went into Sergeant.

It was different with the crow. It always was. Sergeant didn't open up for him like a warm room; Sergeant *allowed* him, the way a senior officer allows a cadet to sit at the end of the table. Clever and cold and dry and full of opinions, most of them about Romeo. He felt the bird's contempt for the river, for the mud, for the four soaked humans standing in a sewer pipe, and under the contempt, steady as stone, something that had no word in crow and that Romeo had never once needed to name.

He didn't take the eyes. He asked. Sergeant considered the request, decided it was beneath him, and did it anyway.

The crow went up.

The world fell away beneath them. The river, a brown ribbon winding west through flat green country. The western edge of Haven behind it, low grey roofs and chimneys and the dark mouths of a dozen outfalls in the riverbank, one of which had four people in it. Fields, out to the west, in long strips: wheat stubble, cabbages, a dark square of something under plastic. Farmhouses with smoke going up from them, straight, then bending, all at once, all in the same direction. Hedges. A road. Beyond all of it, a long way off, a low grey line of hills, and on the nearest of them, a thin dark mast with a small red light blinking at the top.

And beyond the hills, filling half the sky, the storm.

It was enormous. A wall of cloud the colour of a bruise, coming east off the Reach, rolling over the hills like something poured. The top of it was white and boiling and caught the first of the sun. The bottom of it was black. Under it, the hills had already gone grey and soft, the way things go when rain is falling on them.

Sergeant did not like it. Sergeant made his opinion of it known by turning, sharply, and beating back down towards the river, and Romeo let him, because he had seen enough.

Then he looked at the fields again. Properly. With a crow's eyes, which were very good at noticing which things on the ground were doing something unusual.

The cows were lying down. Every herd, in every field, all of them, chewing, with their backs to the west. The rooks were going home, in long black lines towards the trees, at a time of morning when rooks should have been going out. A fox was crossing an open field in daylight without bothering to hide, fast, head low, going towards the river. The swallows were so low over the stubble they were nearly touching it.

He came back into himself, in the pipe, blinking. The light still stung. It stung less.

"Storm," he said. "Big one. Off the Reach. Coming this way." He pointed with his chin, west, at nothing they could see from inside the outfall. "Every animal out there knows. The cows are all lying down."

"I felt it at the hatch," said Juliette. "Far off. A pressure. I wasn't sure." She had her head tilted, listening to the air outside the grating, and now that she was listening to it, he could see her face change. "It's dropping. Fast. It'll be here within the bell."

"Then we go now," said Susan. "Before it hits. We get as far as we can."

"No," said Juliette.

They all looked at her.

She was still listening. But her eyes had gone somewhere else, somewhere Romeo had seen them go before, in briefings and in the dark under bridges, when she was holding a whole street in her head at once.

"There's a relay on the hills," she said. "Romeo, you saw it. A mast with a red light."

"Yes."

"Every big westerly takes the plains relays down. Every single one. It happens twice a year. The ration office complains about it in every report; half the farms out here lose the Choir for an afternoon and their water meters stop counting and the Assembly has to estimate." She looked at the grey light beyond the bars. "That's why there's still a relay on the hill and not a dozen. Nobody's ever spent the money. Nobody needs to watch cabbages that closely."

Romeo understood. He felt it go through him like cold water.

"A dropout," he said.

"A dropout. The whole plain. For as long as the storm sits on it." Juliette's jaw was very tight. "Our seeds will keep writing. Everything we do, every step, all of it, it'll sit behind our ears and wait. And when the relays come back, it'll all go up at once, and anyone watching will see exactly where we went." She looked at them, one at a time. "But not *while* we're going. Not live. They'll see where we were. Hours late. And by then, we'll be in the hills."

"If we're fast," said Dimitri.

"If we're fast."

"And if the storm doesn't kill us," said Susan, quite calmly.

"And if the storm doesn't kill us," agreed Juliette.

From somewhere under their feet, very faintly, there was a sound. A long hollow gurgle, like a throat clearing. Romeo looked down. The water round his boots, which had been still for the whole of the last hour, was moving. Very slowly. Towards the grating. Out of the tunnel.

"Jules," he said.

"I know," she said. "It's raining on the hills already. The drains will fill before the storm gets here." She looked at the moving water, then at the grey sky beyond the bars, then at her brother. "We wait at the mouth. Right at the edge. When the relay light goes out, we go. Not before."

"And if the water comes first?"

"Then we go anyway," said Juliette, "and we run."

---

They waited a few metres back from the broken grating, where the old stone still held the silence round them, crouched in the pipe while the water rose round their ankles and then their shins and the sky went from grey to yellow to a dark green Romeo had never seen a sky go before.

Sergeant sat on top of the pipe outside and said nothing at all.

Then the wind came.

It came first as a shiver over the river: every reed on the far bank bowing at once, as if something had walked past them. Then the willows thrashed. Then it hit the outfall and howled through the grating behind them, and Romeo felt his sister go still beside him in a way he recognised, the stillness of somebody listening to something very big. The air was screaming all round them and she sat in the middle of it, her eyes half shut, and did not push it, and did not fight it, and let it go round.

The rain came after the wind. Not drops. A wall. It came down the river towards them like a grey curtain being drawn across a window, and hit, and the world vanished.

Romeo went back into Sergeant, just for a second, just the eyes, and the crow, who was furious about the rain and about being asked, lifted off the pipe and fought his way up a few metres into it and looked west.

The hills had gone. The fields had gone. There was nothing out there but grey and water and wind.

And through it, faint, high up, a little red light on a mast, blinking.

Blinking.

Blinking.

Gone.

Juliette was already standing up.

"Go," she said.

They went out over the lip of the pipe into the open, and in the back of Romeo's head, the hum came back. Just for a moment. The Choir found them, faint and thin, *a little after the seventh bell, vitals elevated, position*, and then something happened to it. It went wrong. The voice in his head broke up like a voice down a bad line. It stuttered. And then it went flat and small and grey, a quiet he had never heard before, a quiet that wasn't silence: the sound of a seed with nobody to talk to, talking to itself.

---

Afterwards, he could never remember all of the crossing. Pieces of it.

The river first, chest-deep at the edge and colder than anything had ever been, Dimitri hauling Susan up the far bank by the wrist. The mud. Then the fields, mile after mile of them in the rain, the stubble stabbing through his trousers and the furrows full of water that came over his boots. The wind, which tried every few seconds to knock them flat, and which went round Juliette, and round whoever was closest to Juliette, because she was holding a small calm space in the middle of the gale like somebody holding an umbrella, and trying very hard not to look as if she was doing it, because she knew, the same as he did, that every bit of it was being written down behind her ear.

The hail, for ten minutes. White stones the size of peas, bouncing knee-high off the ground, cutting their faces and their hands. Susan, crouched under a hedge, quite calm, with blood running down from her eyebrow, saying "I'm fine, keep going."

Lightning. Once, very close: a white crack and a blow of sound in the same instant, and an oak tree two fields away splitting open like a loaf. He felt every animal for a mile go flat. He went flat too.

And Dimitri.

Dimitri was cold. They were all cold. But Dimitri was cold in a way the rest of them weren't, a way that looked wrong on him, like seeing a bird on the ground. He walked with his arms wrapped round himself and his teeth chattering and his lips going grey, and his hands, which had been warm every single time Romeo had ever touched them, were blue.

"You could," said Romeo, at a gate, shouting over the rain. "Just a bit. Just your hands. Nobody's listening."

"Everyone's listening," said Dimitri. "Later." He shook his head, water flying off his hair. "Juliette's right. It's all being written down. If there's a still flame on the plain at the seventh bell, they'll know it was me, and they'll know exactly where." His teeth were chattering so hard he could barely get the words out. "I'm not giving it to them."

Romeo looked at him. The warmest boy on Eta, standing in a field in a storm, turning blue, because he wouldn't light his own hands.

*The fire on the hill,* he thought. *Going blue in a field, because he won't light his own hands.*

It was the bravest stupid thing he had ever seen.

---

The farm dogs found them at the second hedge.

There were three of them. Big rough-coated sheepdogs, black and white, coming out of the rain across the field at a dead run, barking, the way farm dogs came at anything that came near their farm. Juliette's hand went to her knife.

"Don't," said Romeo. "Please. Just... let me."

He stepped out in front of the others, into the open, and went down onto one knee in the mud, and held out both his hands.

It was almost embarrassing. Farm dogs were the easiest animals on Psi. They had been bred for it, for a thousand generations, back past the Landing to wherever dogs had come from in the first place. All they wanted in the world was a job and someone to tell them they were doing it well. He didn't even have to go in. He just opened the door a crack and let them feel him on the other side of it: calm, friendly, an alpha who wasn't angry, someone who knew the rules.

The first dog skidded to a halt a metre from him. The barking stopped. Her ears went back. Her tail went down, then up, then started to wag.

"Hello," said Romeo. "Hello, darling. Good girl. *Very* good girl. Excellent work. Top marks. You saw us coming a mile off."

The other two arrived. Within half a minute he had three wet sheepdogs pressed against his chest, licking his face, and he was laughing, actually laughing, in the rain, because there was nothing in their minds at all but warmth and approval and the great uncomplicated joy of having been told they were good.

He didn't go into them. He didn't need to. And he would remember, later, how much he'd wanted to.

"Is there a barn?" said Juliette.

"There's a barn," said Romeo. "They want to show it to us. They're very proud of it."

---

The barn was stone-built and dark and smelled of hay and cows and old harness leather, and the rain roared on its tin roof like applause, and there was a woman in it, milking.

They all stopped in the doorway.

She was sitting on a three-legged stool with her forehead pressed against the flank of a red cow and her hands moving steadily under it, and a pail between her knees, and she did not stop when they came in. She was broad and solid, in an oilskin coat and a man's flat cap, with grey hair escaping from under it and forearms like a docker's. She looked round at them once, over her shoulder. Four soaked strangers dripping in her doorway, with her own three dogs fawning round their knees. One of them bleeding from the eyebrow. One of them blue.

She looked back at the cow.

"Shut the door," she said. "You're letting the weather in."

Romeo shut the door.

The woman went on milking. The rain roared. Nobody said anything. The cow shifted her weight and blew out through her nose, and turned her big head round to look at them too, mildly, chewing, and decided they were none of her business. Up in the rafters, Sergeant, who had come in through a gap in the roof, settled on a beam and shook himself, and went very still, the way he did when he wanted to be mistaken for part of the building.

Romeo looked at the woman's back.

*A goose,* he thought.

It came to him at once, the way the right animal always did. Big and broad and absolutely certain of her own patch of ground. Unbothered by anything until it came too close, and then not unbothered at all. Geese were the best guards on any farm on the coast. Better than dogs. Dogs could be talked to. Geese couldn't. They saw a stranger and they told the whole world.

Which was either very good news, he thought, or very bad.

"There's sacks in the corner," said the woman, without turning round. "Dry ones. Get your wet things off and put them on. The tall one first. He looks like a drowned calf."

Dimitri hesitated.

"Go on, lad. I've seen worse. I've got sons."

He went. They all did. In the dark corner of the barn, between the hay bales, they peeled off their soaked shirts and wrapped themselves in hessian grain sacks that smelled of barley and mice, and sat in a row on a bale, shivering, while the woman finished the cow and stood up and carried the pail to the door and set it down and came back.

She stood in front of them with her hands on her hips. She looked at Susan's eyebrow. She looked at Juliette's face. She looked, for a very long time, at Dimitri.

Then she reached into the deep pocket of her oilskin and took out half a loaf of bread wrapped in a cloth, and a lump of hard yellow cheese, and handed them to Juliette.

"Bett Harrow," she said. "This is Harrow's. That's Corley Hill behind." She jerked her head west. "Storm'll sit on us till about the ninth bell. Always does, the big ones. Then it goes over to the coast and the sun comes out like nothing happened." A pause. "And the relay on Corley comes back first. Before any of the plain ones. Always has. It's on the high ground." She didn't look at any of them in particular when she said it. "If I was going west, and I didn't fancy being on the plain when Corley came back, I'd go up the Hollin. Brook runs down out of the hills behind the top field. There's a gully. Gets deep fast. Goes all the way up into the Reach." She sniffed. "Cows won't go near it. Too steep."

Nobody said anything.

Juliette broke the bread into four pieces, carefully, and handed them out. Romeo had never tasted anything as good in his life. He knew it was because he was starving and frozen and had spent three hours as a rat. He didn't care.

"Thank you," said Susan, in her clear de Ricci voice. Even in a grain sack she sounded like she was in a drawing room.

Bett Harrow looked at her.

"You're a de Ricci," she said.

Susan went very still.

"Don't fret. I've no seed in my eyes. I'd know that voice anywhere, that's all. Your grandmother's on the screen every year at the harvest blessing." The farmer's mouth went thin. "We had a well, here. Up till my mother's time. Then the Assembly said the plain's water was to be rationed, same as the basin, and they capped it, and put a meter on the pump. Four hours a day." She shrugged. "Not your family's doing. Not out here. I know that. Just funny, that's all. A de Ricci in my barn, drinking my milk."

Susan didn't look away. Romeo watched her not look away. He thought, not for the first time, that a cat could be very brave when it decided to be.

"It isn't funny," said Susan quietly. "But thank you for the bread."

Bett Harrow grunted. It might have been approval. Then she turned, finally, back to Dimitri.

"And you," she said, "are the one off the screens."

Dimitri didn't move. Romeo felt Juliette, beside him on the bale, go as still as a held breath.

"My eldest is on the Ridge," said the farmer. "Post Six. Corporal. Been there two years. Writes every month." She was looking at Dimitri the way she'd looked at the cow: steadily, without any hurry at all. "His last letter said the Zetans were lighting fires on the far side. First time since the truce. Said everybody on the line was talking about you. Half of them think you're the reason the fires are lit. The other half think you're the reason they'll go out again." She paused. "I haven't decided which half I'm in."

The rain roared on the roof.

"Nor have I," said Dimitri.

Bett Harrow looked at him for a moment longer. Then something in her broad face shifted, very slightly. Not softened. Just shifted. Like a goose settling its feathers.

"Well," she said. "That's honest." She picked up the empty pail. "Your shirts won't dry. Take the sacks. I'll not miss four." She went to the door, and stopped there with her hand on it, and looked back at them over her shoulder. "I didn't see anything this morning. Cows were lying down. Dogs were barking at the weather. That's all."

She went out into the rain, and shut the door behind her.

---

They left a quarter of an hour later, by the back of the barn, the way the dogs showed them.

Romeo went last. At the corner of the barn he stopped and looked back at the farmhouse, low and grey in the rain, with smoke from its chimney blown flat across the yard. The youngest of the three dogs had followed them to the corner and was sitting there in the mud with her tongue out, watching him go, and on an impulse he didn't examine, he reached out, very lightly, and borrowed her eyes.

Just for a second. Just to look.

The farmhouse kitchen window. Yellow light behind it; oil lamps, not electric. And Bett Harrow standing at the window in her oilskin, with the pail on the table behind her, looking out at the rain.

She had her hand at the back of her head. Behind her ear.

He let the dog go.

He stood there for a moment in the rain. A woman with a hand behind her ear could be sending a message. She could be sending it to her son at Post Six, or to the ration office about her meter, or to the nearest Choir post, or to nobody, because there was nobody on the plain to send it to right now. A woman with a hand behind her ear could also have a headache. He had a headache. Everybody he knew had a headache.

He caught up with the others at the top field.

"Jules," he said, and told her.

His sister listened without stopping. When he'd finished, she was quiet for three steps.

"Then walk faster," she said.

---

They reached the Hollin at a quarter to the ninth bell.

It started as nothing. A brook at the bottom of a field, brown and roaring with storm water, too wide to jump. Then it cut down into the ground. Then down further, into a gully with steep sides of red earth and gorse, where the wind couldn't reach and the rain came straight down and the noise of the water filled everything. They followed it up. The gully got deeper. The sides got steeper and rockier, with ferns growing out of the cracks, and hawthorns bent over the top like hands.

Behind them, the storm was moving off east.

Romeo felt it go. He felt the sheep in the fields below get to their feet, one by one, and shake themselves. He felt the rooks come out of the trees. He felt a hare, somewhere on the slope above them, sit up in its form and put its ears up into the thinning rain.

And at the top of the gully, just as they climbed round a great wet shoulder of rock into the place where the walls closed in overhead, he went into Sergeant one last time and looked back.

Corley Hill. The thin dark mast on its top.

The little red light, blinking.

Blinking.

He felt the hum in the back of his head stir. Faint. A thread of it, reaching for him, *a quarter to the ninth...*

And then the rock closed round them, and the gully turned, and it was gone. Not silent, the way the Undercroft was silent. Just thin. Small and grey and quiet. A seed talking to itself in a valley too deep for anybody to hear it.

Nobody said anything for a long time. They just stood there in the bottom of the Hollin with the brook roaring past their feet, wrapped in Bett Harrow's grain sacks, soaked and filthy and bleeding and blue, and breathed.

"Everything we did this morning," said Susan at last. She had her hand behind her own ear. "It's all in there. Waiting."

"Yes," said Juliette.

"The barn. The farmer. All of it."

"Not what anybody said. Just where we were. And how long." Juliette looked up at the strip of sky between the gully walls, which was going, slowly, from grey to a thin washed blue. "It'll sync the moment we come out of a valley anywhere near a relay. And then they'll have the whole morning. River, fields, barn, here."

"So they'll know where we went in," said Dimitri.

"They'll know where we went in." His sister's voice was quite steady. "They won't know where we come out."

Susan laughed. A small, tired, startled sound. They all looked at her.

"I was just thinking," she said. She had one of her seed pouches in her hand, turning it over and over. "I've got six of these. Full. I've been carrying them all night, and I haven't used one, because if I grow anything, it's a signature. It goes in the cache. It tells them exactly where I was and exactly what I did." She held the pouch up. "Two kinds of seeds. One in my pocket and one in my head. And both of them tell on me."

Romeo grinned. It hurt his face, which was cut from the hail. He did it anyway.

"Write that down," he said. "That's good. That's a poster."

---

They went on up the Hollin until the afternoon, and then they stopped, because they couldn't go on.

There was an overhang. A great slab of red rock leaning out from the gully wall over a flat shelf of dry earth, high enough above the brook that the flood didn't reach it. Somebody had used it before; there was a ring of blackened stones in the middle of it, very old, with grass growing between them. A shepherd, maybe. Somebody from before the Choir.

Nobody looked at the ring of stones for very long.

They made beds out of bracken that Susan cut with Juliette's knife, by hand, the slow way, with her mouth set. They ate the last of Bett Harrow's cheese. They put their wet clothes on the rocks to dry in the thin sun, which came, briefly, and went. And then the sun went down behind the high ground to the west, and the gully filled up with blue shadow, and the cold came.

Dimitri sat with his back against the rock and his knees drawn up and his blue hands jammed in his armpits, and did not light anything.

Nobody asked him to.

At some point in the dusk, Juliette got up from her bracken and went and sat down beside him. Not touching. A careful distance. Then, after a while, less careful. Romeo saw the air between them stir, and go still, and stay still, and he looked very hard at the brook for some time.

Susan was asleep almost before she lay down, curled tight in her grain sack with her seed pouches under her head and the envelope inside her shirt. A cat, he thought. Even asleep. The expensive kind, put out in the rain, and furious about it, and not going to show you.

He took the first watch. He sat at the edge of the shelf with his legs hanging over the drop and Sergeant on his knee, and looked out at the dark.

The Hollin went on up ahead of them into the hills, a black cut in a black slope, under more stars than he had seen since he was nine. No city glow. No lamps. Nothing for miles in any direction but rock and gorse and the sound of water. He could feel the life of the valley all round him, faint and small, the way you can feel the warmth of a room with your eyes shut: field mice in the bracken, a roosting pigeon somewhere on the rock face, the hare from that morning, or another one. Sheep, far below. All of them Earth animals. Soft. Easy. All of them the kind that wanted to be fed.

And then, from somewhere up the gully in the dark, a sound.

Deep. Rough. Short. Like a saw drawn once through wet wood.

Every small life in the valley went still at once. The mice. The pigeon. The hare. Romeo felt them all freeze, the way he'd felt every animal on the plain go flat when the lightning hit, and he froze with them, and his heart, which had not done this in nine years, started to pound.

The sound came again. Further off. Higher up. Then nothing.

He knew it. He would have known it anywhere. He had heard it once, at the edge of a war, from the bottom of a gully, with his hand on a dying paw.

A hill-cat.

He sat very still on the edge of the shelf and did not reach.

He wanted to. He wanted to so badly his hands shook. He could feel the shape of where it must be, up there in the dark, the way you feel the shape of a fire with your eyes closed: something big and hot and alive at the very edge of everything he could sense, a mind that had never been told what to do in its life, that didn't know what a wall was, that had no seed in its head and no bell to wake it and no list of years it owed to anybody. It was right there. He could have touched it. Just to see. Just for a second. Just the edge.

*It's like wearing someone else's coat.*

On his knee, Sergeant's claws closed. Hard. Through the cloth of his trousers, into the skin. Not enough to draw blood. Enough.

Romeo looked down at him.

The crow was looking up. Flat black eyes in the starlight. Not clicking. Not pecking. Just holding on, the way he had held on to Juliette's shoulder at the foot of the tower. The way you'd hold a rail on a ship.

"I know," said Romeo, very quietly. "I know. I'm not going anywhere."

He put his hands in his coat pockets and kept them there.

He had told Dimitri, on a roof, a long time ago, that there were two ways his life could go. Seven years, and a licence, and a warehouse full of grain and rats. Or the Reach, and finding out what he was.

He had never really believed in the second one. It had been a thing you said on a roof, the way you said you'd sail to the far lands one day, or beat Juliette at cards, or tell Varga what you really thought of her.

Now he was sitting in it. A deserter. With no seed that anyone could hear, and no warehouse, and no seven years, and a hill-cat somewhere above him in the dark, coughing at the stars.

The Reach was his country. He had always half known that.

He just hadn't known, until tonight, that the frightening part wasn't how wild it was.

It was how much it felt like home.
