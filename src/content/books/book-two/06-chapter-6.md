On paper, they were visiting Sister Orla.

It was even true. She was staying in a room above a cobbler's on the Fourth Canal, two streets from the burned chapel, while the boys from the district rebuilt the Long Voyage's roof. Juliette had filed it that afternoon: *Captain Vasquez and Cadet Gale, welfare visit, F. District, two hours.* The kind of thing nobody would look at twice. And they did visit. Orla gave them tea and a crust for Biscuit and told Dimitri he looked thin, and held his hand for a long time without saying anything, and then said, "Go on, then. Whatever it is you're really here for. Go and do it."

So they went.

Out through the cobbler's back door. Along the towpath in the dark. Down the culvert steps beside the old pumping station, where the thin stream of water ran out into the canal, and where Corran Vell was waiting for them with a lamp.

He looked at Juliette for a long moment. She looked back. The last time they'd been this close, he had been standing over her in the steam with water coiled round his wrist, and she had been on the floor of the dye works with her knife gone.

"Captain," said Corran.

"Vell."

"Are you going to arrest me?"

"Not tonight."

Corran nodded slowly, as if that were a perfectly reasonable answer. Then he looked at Dimitri, and something in his face eased.

"Thank you for Orla," he said. "I wanted to say it to your face."

"You don't have to."

"Yes, I do." Corran turned and held the lamp up to the dark mouth of the culvert. "She's waiting. She's been waiting since the beast-speaker left. I don't think she's slept."

---

Magda Ferro had not slept. She was sitting at a long workbench under a cluster of hanging lamps, with her magnifying lens strapped over her good eye and her blind eye drifting, and in front of her was a machine.

Dimitri had never seen anything like it. It was the size of a bread oven, built out of brass and glass and parts that had clearly been taken from other machines, a dozen other machines, and bolted together with more cleverness than care. Wires ran out of it in every direction. In the centre of it, under a dome of thick glass, was a small brass cradle, lined with something soft. Empty. Waiting.

On the shelves along the walls, in their jars and tins and paper twists, the hundreds of dead seeds watched.

"The still flame," said Magda Ferro, without looking up. "Come here. Let me see your hands."

He went. He held them out. She took them in her clawed old fingers and turned them over, palms up, and looked at them through her lens for a long time, the way you'd look at a rare insect.

"Ordinary," she said finally, sounding almost disappointed. "Perfectly ordinary hands. Calluses from the stick. Nothing to show for it at all." She let go. "His were the same, you know. Before he went north. You'd never have known."

She picked up the seed from a dish beside her, using a pair of fine brass tweezers, and laid it in the little cradle under the glass dome.

"Here's what happens," she said. "The filament wakes with warmth. *His* warmth, or the closest thing to it. So you put your hand on the dome, here, flat, and you give it what you gave it the other night. Not fire. Not flame. Just warmth. Steady. As steady as you can. As if you were keeping a baby bird warm in your palm." Her mouth twisted. "And then we wait. The cache will start to talk to my machine. Slowly. It's been asleep for nineteen years and it's been badly burned, and it'll be confused. It'll take time. An hour. Maybe more." She looked up at him. "Can you hold steady for an hour, boy?"

Dimitri thought about the Kiln. About walking away from a flame on a stone post, step by step, feeling the rope thin.

"I don't know," he said. "I'll try."

"Good answer." She waved at a stool. "Sit."

---

He sat. He put his hand flat on the glass dome.

And he gave it warmth.

It was harder than fire. Fire was easy now; fire wanted to come. Warmth without fire was like trying to hum without opening your mouth. He had to find the place under his ribs where the fire lived, and open the door just a crack, just enough to let a little heat out, and not one breath more. And then hold it there. Steady. Not too hot. Not too cold. Not flickering.

Under his palm, the glass grew warm. Then warmer. He felt it go through the glass, down into the cradle, into the little black lump of melted metal.

For a long time, nothing happened.

Juliette sat on a crate by the wall with her arms folded, watching him. Corran stood by the door, watching the culvert. Romeo had stayed above, on the towpath, with Sergeant and three rats posted at either end of the canal. *If anyone comes,* he'd said, *you'll know before they've finished thinking about it.*

The machine hummed. The lamps hissed. The dead seeds watched from their shelves.

And then, under his hand, something answered.

It was the same as the night in the squad room. A pulse. One single beat of warmth, from inside the seed, pushing back against his palm. Then, a moment later, another. Then another. Slow. Steady. Like a heart, very far away, beating in its sleep.

Magda Ferro sat up straight on her stool. On the side of her machine, a row of small glass bulbs had begun to flicker. Green. Amber. Green.

"There," she breathed. "There you are. Oh, there you are, you clever thing. Keep going, boy. Don't stop. Don't you dare stop."

Dimitri didn't stop.

It was the strangest hour of his life.

The pulse under his palm didn't speed up or slow down. It just went on, steady, warm, patient, a heartbeat that wasn't his and wasn't anybody's anymore. And the longer he held it, the more he felt it. Not just warmth. Something else. A kind of pull, very faint, like a current under still water. As if the seed were leaning towards him. As if it recognised something.

He thought of Pell's lecture. *Bound omicrons tune themselves to a note.*

He thought of his father's voice in the trial hall fire. *Fire talks to fire, if you know how.*

He thought: *this is the closest I have ever been to him.*

And then he made himself stop thinking, because his hand had started to get hotter, and the old woman had hissed at him, and he breathed, in, out, the way Juliette had taught him, and brought it back down.

Across the room, Juliette uncrossed her arms and leaned forward on the crate. She didn't say anything. She just watched him. And the air around him, very gently, began to move. Slowly. Steadily. In and out. Like breath.

He held on.

---

"Positions first," said Magda Ferro, at the end of the hour. "They're the easiest. They always come through first."

She had a slate wired into the machine, an old one, cracked across one corner, and on it, slowly, line by line, text was appearing. She read it aloud in her dry cracked voice, squinting through her lens.

"Seven days. That's what's left. Seven days of cache, and the last one ends at the second bell after midnight on the night he burned it. On the hill." She ran a finger down the slate. "Look at this. He's busy. Southern garrison, the de Ricci estate. Over and over. That's his posting. Then here. And here. Three times in seven days." She tapped. "Haven. Fourth District. Grid reference 4-11-9. And then he drops off."

"Drops off?" said Juliette.

"Goes dark." Magda Ferro looked up. "Two hours. Four. Then he comes back, same reference. You know what 4-11-9 is, captain?"

Juliette's face was very still. "The old maintenance hatch under Fourth District. The way into the Undercroft."

"The Undercroft." Ferro nodded slowly. "Three times in his last week. Going down into the oldest dead zone on the coast. Coming back up hours later." She looked at Dimitri. "Your mother's drawing. Was that from one of these trips?"

"She said he took her once."

"Then once, she was with him, and twice, he went alone." Ferro turned back to her slate. "And then here. Day five. Not Haven. Not the estate." She went very quiet. "Assembly district. The network office."

"Your office," said Corran from the door.

"My office." She stared at the slate. "Third floor. Corner desk." Her voice had gone strange. "He came back. Two years after he first came to me with his map and his questions, he came back. I don't remember it. I must not have been there." She shook her head. "Or I don't want to remember."

"What came after positions?" said Juliette.

The old woman blinked, and looked down, and the lens magnified her eye huge and wet.

"Sent messages," she said. "They're coming now. Slower. Hold on, boy. Nearly there."

---

There were four.

The first was a report.

Magda Ferro read it aloud, line by line, as it crawled across the cracked slate. It was formal. Clipped. Written in the dry language of the EDF, addressed to a member of the Assembly's Committee on Antiquities and the Coastal Record, and it said, in the plainest possible words, that Captain Idris Kane of the southern garrison wished to report the discovery of carvings of pre-Landing origin in the natural dead zone beneath Haven's Fourth District. Carvings depicting non-human inhabitants of the coast at the time of the Landing. Carvings that contradicted the Assembly's official account of the Empty Shore. He requested authority to survey the site fully, and the other natural dead zones on the coast, a list of which he attached.

It was signed *I. Kane, Capt.*

It was addressed to *Councillor A. Halloran.*

Nobody in the pumping station moved.

"Halloran," said Juliette very quietly. "*Annelise* Halloran?"

"There was only ever one," said Magda Ferro. "She sat on Antiquities before she was Speaker. Everybody knows that." She swallowed. "There's a reply. It's the second message. It came in the same day. Short."

She read it.

*Captain. Thank you for your report. You will be aware that all records relating to pre-Landing artefacts are sealed under the Shore Order of the founding Assembly, and that their discovery, description or discussion is restricted to members of this committee. Your request is denied. You will make no further visits to the site in question. You will make no copies. You will report to the Choir Integrity Office on the first day of next month for reassignment. A.H.*

"The Shore Order," said Juliette. Her voice was flat and cold. "I've never heard of it."

"No," said Magda Ferro. "Nobody has. That's rather the point of it." She stared at the slate. "*Nobody is allowed to know.* I told him that, two years before. I didn't know why. I only knew that there were things the network wasn't allowed to look at, and if you asked about them, you got moved. And people who got moved to Choir Integrity..." She stopped.

"What?" said Dimitri. His hand was still on the dome. It was very hard, suddenly, to keep it steady.

"They came back very quiet," said Magda Ferro. "If they came back."

The pulse under his palm beat, and beat.

"He didn't go," said Juliette slowly. "To Integrity. He burned his seed out first."

"On the hill. Six days later." Ferro's finger moved down the slate. "The third message. Day six. One line."

She read it, and then she didn't say anything for a moment. Then she turned the slate round on the bench so that Dimitri could see it himself.

It was addressed to *Lt. I. Varga.*

*Ilse. I'm going north. Don't come after me; you'll have to sign the warrant, and I'd rather it was you than anyone. There is a girl in the de Ricci kitchens who sings. If anything happens to me, look after my bell. You owe me one. I.*

Dimitri stared at the words.

*You owe me one.*

Halden Ford. The grain store. The beam. The fire opening like a curtain.

Varga, in the back of a car on the coast road, looking at her own hands. *When your file came across my desk, I thought: here is a debt I can actually pay.*

She hadn't told him that his father had asked her to.

"She knew," he said. "Nineteen years. She knew he'd asked."

"She looked after Mira?" said Juliette.

"No. Aurelio did that." Dimitri swallowed. "Varga waited. For me. Until I walked into her command." He looked at the slate. "She's been paying it the whole time."

The pulse under his hand beat. Slow. Patient. Far away.

"And the fourth?" said Corran from the door.

Magda Ferro turned the slate back round. Her face, behind the lens, had gone very strange.

"The fourth wasn't sent," she said. "It was drafted. Composed. Held. He never released it." She frowned at the slate. "It's the last thing in the cache. Timestamp: second bell after midnight. On the hill." She swallowed. "About one minute before the seed stops."

"What does it say?"

She didn't answer. She just turned the slate round again.

It wasn't addressed to anyone. There was no name at the top. Just four words, composed on a hilltop in the dark, the last thing Idris Kane had thought into the Choir before he took his own fire to the inside of his own skull.

*They're still down there.*

---

Dimitri's hand came off the glass.

He didn't mean it to. It just did. The warmth went out of it all at once, the way a breath goes out of you when you're hit, and under the dome the seed's slow pulse stuttered, and stopped. The little row of glass bulbs on the side of the machine flickered green, amber, and went dark.

Magda Ferro let out a long, slow breath.

"That's all right," she said quietly. "That's all right. I've got it. It's all on the slate. Every word." She looked at him with her one magnified eye. "You held it an hour and twenty minutes, boy. Do you know how long his own body heat would have kept it talking? About a minute. Then it would have cooked." She shook her head. "You held it like a baby bird."

Dimitri didn't answer. He was staring at the slate. At the four words.

*They're still down there.*

"Down where?" said Corran. "The drains?"

"The Undercroft," said Juliette. She had stood up off the crate. "He went down three times in his last week. On his own. And then he sent a report to the Assembly about the carvings, and the Assembly sealed it, and told him to stop going down, and ordered him to Integrity." Her voice was very quiet. "And six days later he went up a hill and burned his seed out, and the last thing he thought before he did it was *they're still down there.*"

"Who?" said Dimitri. His voice cracked. "Who's still down there? The carvings?"

"I don't think he meant the carvings," said Juliette.

The pumping station was very quiet. Even the machine had stopped humming.

Dimitri thought of the Undercroft. The long sloped tunnel. The round chamber at the bottom, with the floor worn smooth by water that no longer ran. The dry mineral smell, like the inside of a mountain.

The silence. The deep, perfect, total silence in his head, the moment they had walked in. Not a jammer. Nothing anybody had built. The oldest dead zone on the coast.

*Nobody knows why,* Romeo had said. *The stones down there are older than the city.*

And a Zetan medic on a parade ground at sunset. *The ones in the mountains, who were already old when my people were young.*

And Dr Pell, in the warm lecture hall, with his enormous spectacles. *The Muites, if you believe the stories, can do a great many things with omicrons that we can't.*

"The dead zones," Dimitri said slowly. "The natural ones. Nobody knows what makes them."

"No," said Magda Ferro.

"He made a map of all of them. Every one on the coast. And then he went down into the biggest one, three times, and came back up, and asked the Assembly for permission to go into the rest." Dimitri's hands had begun to shake. He pressed them flat on the workbench. "What if they're not *places*? What if they're not old stone, or something in the rock? What if something's doing it? On purpose? Like a jammer. Except alive."

Nobody answered him.

On the shelves along the walls, the hundreds of dead seeds sat in their jars and tins in the lamplight. Every one of them, once, had gone silent in a dead zone. Every one had stopped writing.

"Then the Shore Order isn't about history," said Juliette at last. Her voice was very calm. It was the calm she used when she was more frightened than she had ever been. "It's not about what happened at the Landing. It's not about who was here first."

She looked at the slate. At the four words.

"It's about who never left."

---

They walked back along the canal at the third bell.

Romeo met them at the bridge, with Sergeant on one shoulder and a rat on the other, which he set down on the towpath with a quiet word before they reached him. He took one look at their faces and didn't make a joke. Not one. He just fell into step beside his sister and walked.

At the cobbler's door, Juliette stopped.

"Halloran," she said. "The Speaker. Who gave you thirty days."

"I know," said Dimitri.

"Thirty days to *train*. She said. Thirty days to find out what you are." Juliette's eyes were very dark. "Or thirty days to decide what to do with a boy whose father found something under her city that she sealed nineteen years ago. And who might be able to find it again."

Dimitri thought about the Speaker at her lectern. Small and silver and sharp. Her hands folded, very still. *Like a person holding something heavy very carefully,* Susan had said.

"We need to tell Susan," he said. "She watched her hands."

"We need to tell Varga," said Romeo.

Juliette was quiet for a long time.

"Varga had a message from your father nineteen years ago," she said. "*Look after my bell.* And she didn't tell you. She's had nineteen years, and she didn't tell you any of it until you were sitting in the back of her car." She looked at Dimitri. "I trust her. I think. I think she's been on your side the whole time." A pause. "But I don't know whose side she's on about *this*."

The canal was black and still. Somewhere far off in the Fourth District, a dog barked, once, and then was quiet. Romeo's head turned towards the sound, very slightly, before he caught himself.

Dimitri looked down at the cobbles. At the dark water. At the city all around them, sleeping, with its Choir humming softly in every head, and its maps with their blank corners, and its schoolrooms where children learned before their letters that the shore was empty, and the land was waiting, and the land was theirs.

And under it, under all of it, under the streets and the drains and the oldest stones on the coast, a silence that no one had ever explained.

*They're still down there.*

"I have to go back," he said. "To the Undercroft."

"I know," said Juliette.

"Not to look at the walls."

"I know."

She didn't tell him not to. She didn't tell him it was dangerous, or that the Speaker had sealed it, or that the last man who went down there three times in a week ended up burning his own seed out on a hill and walking into the snow.

She just took his hand, there on the towpath, in the dark, in full view of the Choir and anybody else who cared to look.

"Then we go together," said Juliette Vasquez. "And this time, we bring a lamp."
