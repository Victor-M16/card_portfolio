They argued about the seed for two days.

Not loudly. Juliette didn't argue loudly; she argued the way a river argues with a rock, by simply continuing to be there until the rock gives up. But it went round and round the squad room, in the evenings, with the door locked and the seed wrapped in a handkerchief in the middle of the table like a very small, very dangerous pudding.

Give it to the Assembly's engineers, and the Assembly would know everything that was on it before they did. Including, possibly, things about Varga. About Aurelio. About a captain who had walked through fire at Halden Ford and a lieutenant he'd carried out of it.

Give it to Varga, and it went into the drawer with Susan's report about Matteo. And maybe it came out again. And maybe it didn't.

Keep it, and it was a little black lump of melted metal in a handkerchief that had pulsed once in Dimitri's hand and never again.

"There's another way," Romeo said, on the second evening.

They all looked at him.

"Hollows," he said. "They burn their seeds out. All of them. Every single one, at some point, sat somewhere and dug a little piece of metal out of their own head." He shrugged. "Somebody must know how they work. Better than the Assembly. Somebody who isn't on anybody's books."

"You mean the Ashen," said Susan.

"I mean a boy who carries water to the Fourth District and once nodded at us very politely from a chapel door." Romeo looked at his sister. "Corran knows people. And Corran owes Dimitri, I think. For the chapel. For Orla."

Juliette was quiet for a long time.

"Not you," she said finally. "If anyone goes, it's me."

"You're the captain. You go walking into the Fourth District at night to meet an unregistered wielder and somebody's going to have a recorder, and then you're on every screen in Haven next to a hollow, and Marr has her thirty days wrapped up in a bow." He grinned. "Nobody looks at me, Jules. I'm the funny one. I'm a man with a crow. If anybody films me, it'll be because Sergeant's done something rude."

Sergeant, on the cupboard, clicked in a way that suggested this was likely.

Juliette looked at him for a long moment. Then at Dimitri. Then at the seed.

"Tomorrow night," she said. "And you send me your position every quarter-bell until you're back. If you go dark, I'm coming in."

"Yes, Captain."

"And Romeo." She held his eyes. "Whatever you've been borrowing lately. The hound. Whatever's next. Take it off before you come home."

He grinned at her. He didn't answer.

---

The trouble with finding Corran Vell was that Corran Vell did not exist.

No seed. No Choir. No records. You couldn't look him up. You couldn't send him anything. You couldn't follow a little green marker across a map, because there wasn't one. He was a hole in the world, shaped like a young man with his sleeves rolled up.

But holes have edges. And Romeo knew a great many creatures who were very, very good at noticing edges.

He went down to the Fourth District at dusk, along the canal, with his hands in his pockets and Sergeant on his shoulder, and stopped at the bottom of the stone steps beside the dead tram stop. The Long Voyage was a black shell behind its scorched door. Someone had nailed boards across the windows. Someone else had painted a gold flame on every single board.

There was a dog asleep on the steps.

A stray. Brown and white, lean, with one ear that stood up and one that didn't, curled nose to tail in a patch of last light. Romeo had seen her before. Half the Fourth District fed her. She belonged to everybody and nobody, and she had been at this chapel every day of her life, because Sister Orla always had a crust.

Romeo crouched down beside her and held out his hand.

She woke. She sniffed his fingers. Her tail thumped once on the stone.

"Hello, sweetheart," he said softly. "I need your nose."

And he went in.

---

It was always different, and it was always the same.

With rats, it was small and quick and greedy. With crows, it was clever and cold and full of opinions. With Old Bess at the kennels, it was a soldier's mind: alert, obedient, patient, eager to work.

The stray was none of those.

The stray was *joy*.

It hit him like warm water. A wide, simple, enormous happiness, about everything at once: the last of the sun on the stone, the smell of the canal, the crust Orla had left on the step that morning, the boy who threw sticks on the corner, the woman who scratched behind the one ear that stood up. She was hungry and she didn't mind. She was cold and she didn't mind. She was alive, and that was wonderful, and here was a new person, and that was wonderful too.

And the smells.

Romeo had been inside noses before. Never like this. The world exploded into a thousand layers of scent, all at once, every one of them telling a story. The burned chapel: wet ash and old lamp oil and, under it, the sharp chemical sting of the accelerant, still there, still sour. Orla's smell, faint, on the step: wool and soap and candle wax. A hundred feet that had come down these steps for water. And among them, under them, faint but clear, a smell the dog knew very well and liked very much.

River water. Clean river water. And skin that had been cold and wet a great deal, and a little bit of copper, from pipes.

The water boy. The one who scratched the ear that didn't stand up.

*Where?* Romeo asked. *Where does he go?*

She didn't need asking twice. She was on her feet, nose down, tail up, and she was away along the canal at a trot, and Romeo was going with her. Not following. *Going*. His own legs were moving, somewhere, a long way off, walking quickly along the towpath with a crow on his shoulder. But he wasn't in them, much. He was down low, close to the ground, four feet, nose full of a thousand stories, and the trail was there, bright as a ribbon, and it was wonderful, it was the most wonderful thing, and he could do this forever, he could just stay, just be this, warm and simple and happy and *free*...

Something sharp hit him on the ear.

He yelped. Out loud. In his own voice.

Sergeant had pecked him. Hard. And was now sitting on his shoulder glaring at him with one flat black eye from a distance of two inches.

Romeo stopped walking.

He was standing on the towpath under a bridge, in the near dark, and the stray was a few metres ahead of him, looking back over her shoulder with her tongue out, waiting. He was breathing hard. His hands were shaking.

He had nearly not come back.

Not like the hill-cat. Not that wild, furious, enormous wanting. Something softer, and worse. It hadn't felt like falling. It had felt like going home.

"Thank you," he said to Sergeant, very quietly.

The crow did not dignify that with a response. It simply stayed exactly where it was, two inches from his face, watching, and did not look away for the rest of the walk.

Romeo went back into the dog after that. Carefully. Only a little. Only the nose. He kept one hand on Sergeant's feathers the whole way, like a man holding a rail on a ship.

---

The trail ended at the old pumping station under the Fourth Canal Bridge.

It was a squat brick building half sunk into the canal wall, with iron doors rusted shut and the windows bricked up long ago. The dog stopped at a culvert beside it, a round black mouth in the brickwork where a thin stream of water ran out into the canal, and sat down, and thumped her tail, and looked at Romeo as if to say: *here, obviously.*

"Good girl," said Romeo, and let her go. All of her. He felt her slide out of him like water out of a cupped hand, and she shook herself, ears flapping, and trotted away up the towpath without a backward glance. Somebody, somewhere, would have a crust.

He stood by the culvert for a moment, just being himself. It was colder than it had been. Quieter. Everything smelled of almost nothing.

"You followed a *dog* here," said a voice from inside the culvert.

Corran Vell stepped out into the last grey light.

He was the same as Romeo remembered from the chapel door: thin, young, tired, with his sleeves rolled up and the faint shimmer of water hanging round his wrists like heat haze. But closer, in the cold, Romeo could see the little crescent scar behind his ear, and the way he stood. Loose. Easy. Weight on the balls of his feet. Like something that lived half in the water and half out of it, and was never quite sure which side it preferred.

*Otter*, thought Romeo. *Definitely an otter.*

"She likes you," he said. "She says you scratch the ear that doesn't stand up."

Corran's face did something complicated. "Biscuit. Her name's Biscuit. Orla named her." He glanced up the towpath. "Is she all right? After the fire?"

"She's fine. She's very happy. She's happy about absolutely everything." Romeo rubbed his ear where Sergeant had pecked it. "It's a bit much, honestly."

Corran almost smiled. Then didn't. "You're the other twin. The beast-speaker."

"Romeo."

"I know who you are. Everyone in the Fourth knows who you are. You carried Orla out." Corran's eyes went to the crow on his shoulder, then back. "I'm not going to thank you for that, because she's mine to thank, not yours, and I'll do it to her face. But I owe you. All of you. The fire boy most." He folded his arms. "So. What do you want?"

Romeo took the handkerchief out of his pocket. Unwrapped it. Held it out on his palm.

Corran looked at the little blackened lump for a long moment.

"That's a seed," he said.

"It's Idris Kane's seed."

The water round Corran's wrists stopped moving.

---

The inside of the pumping station was warm and dry and full of the low, steady hum of old machinery that somebody had brought back to life. Lamps hung from the iron rafters. A narrow walkway ran round the walls above the great silent pump wheels. And along one wall, on shelves made of old crates, in rows and rows of glass jars and tin boxes and twists of paper, were seeds.

Hundreds of them. Little black lumps, blistered and melted, each one labelled in tiny handwriting. A name, or a date, or just a number.

"Hollows bring them to her," said Corran quietly. "After. When they've dug them out. Most of us can't stand to keep them, and can't stand to throw them away either. So she keeps them for us." He looked at the shelves. "Mine's up there somewhere. Third row. Number two hundred and eight."

"Who's *she*?"

"Me," said a voice from the walkway above.

She was old. Older than Pell, maybe. Small and bent, in a heavy apron covered in burns, with a magnifying lens strapped over one eye and the other eye milky and blind. Her hair was white and cropped short, and behind her left ear, very old, very faded, was a little crescent scar.

"Magda Ferro," said Corran. "She used to work for the Choir."

"Thirty-one years," said the old woman, coming down the iron steps one at a time, gripping the rail. "Network engineering. Seed design. Third floor of the Assembly's network office, corner desk, by the window." She reached the bottom and squinted at Romeo through her lens. "I built the local cache. The little bit that keeps writing when the line drops. That was mine." A dry laugh. "And then one day I sat at my desk and read what the network was keeping on my sister, and I went home and took a hot needle to my own head. Seventeen years ago." She held out a hand like a claw. "Let me see it."

Romeo put the seed in her palm.

She held it up to the lamp, very close, turning it with her fingertips, and squinted through the lens for a long time.

"Burned with fire," she said. "Not a needle. Not a blade. *Fire.* From the inside. Look at the way the casing's bubbled. Somebody held a flame against it until it melted." She turned it again. "Somebody very good with flame. It's even. Neat. Hardly any scorch on the filament." Her blind eye drifted towards Corran. "Is this real, boy? Is this really his?"

"The beast-speaker says so."

"I say so," said Romeo. "It came from his son. Who got it from his mother. Who got it from him, the week he deserted."

Magda Ferro was silent for a long moment.

"I knew him," she said. "Did you know that? Not well. He came to the network office once. A young captain with a lot of questions. He wanted to know why the Choir went dark in the oldest places. The drains under Haven. Certain hills. Certain ruins. He'd made a map. Every dead zone on the coast that wasn't a jammer, marked in red." She turned the seed in her fingers. "I told him nobody knew. Which was true. And then I told him that nobody was *allowed* to know. Which was also true." She looked at Romeo with her one good eye, magnified huge behind the lens. "Two years later I burned mine out. I've often wondered if that conversation was where it started."

"Can you read it?" said Romeo.

The old woman looked down at the seed in her palm.

"The filament's mostly melted," she said. "Most of the cache will be gone. But this end here," she touched it with the tip of a fingernail, "this end's intact. A few days. Maybe a week." She frowned. "But it's cold. Dead cold. A seed's filament needs warmth to wake. Body warmth, usually. Its own person's warmth. That's how it knows who it belongs to." Her mouth twisted. "And the person this belongs to is somewhere in the snow."

Romeo felt something go very still inside him.

"It pulsed," he said.

She looked up.

"Four nights ago. When his son touched it. One pulse. Like a heartbeat." Romeo swallowed. "Dimitri said: *I think it knew me.*"

Magda Ferro stared at him. Behind her lens, her good eye had gone very wide.

Then, slowly, she began to laugh. A dry, cracked, astonished sound, like old paper tearing.

"Of course it did," she said. "Of course. It doesn't want a body's warmth. It wants *his* warmth. The same warmth. And the boy's fire is his fire." She closed her clawed hand round the seed, very gently, the way you'd close it round a baby bird. "Bring me the boy, beast-speaker. Bring me the still flame, and let him hold this in his hand for an hour, and I'll read you every last thing Idris Kane's head was doing the week before he walked into the Reach."

---

Corran walked him back as far as the bridge.

They didn't talk much. The canal was black and still. Somewhere off in the Fourth, a bell was ringing for the late service at one of the chapels that hadn't burned.

At the foot of the bridge, Corran stopped.

"Beast-speaker," he said. "One more thing. Tell your captain. Tell the fire boy."

Romeo waited.

"There are two Ashen now," said Corran. "There always were, a bit. But since the trial hall, there's two." He looked out at the black water. "There's us. The ones who follow Kane. The water. The chapels. *Don't hurt him.* And then there's the others. The ones who painted *and now it is lit* on the overpass." His mouth tightened. "They used to be small. Loud, but small. Roe's friends. Then everybody saw the fire boy fill a stone room to the roof, and now they're not small anymore. They've got a leader. They call her the Lamplighter. Nobody I know has seen her face."

"What does she want?"

"The same thing the Shorekeepers want," said Corran. "Only backwards. The Shorekeepers think the boy's a monster and want him locked up. The Lamplighter thinks he's a weapon and wants him *pointed*." His eyes came back to Romeo's. "At the Ridge. At the Sere. At the Zetans. She's been telling everyone who'll listen that Kane's son is the answer to the ration. One boy who can burn a desert empty, and Psi is wide."

The wind came off the canal, cold.

"Kane's orders," said Corran, "are *don't hurt him*. Hers are *bring him*." He paused. "I don't know which of those frightens me more."

He turned to go. Then turned back.

"And your leak," he said. "The one who told Roe about the boy. It isn't us. It isn't her, either. We don't need a leak in the EDF. We've got Orla's kitchen window and the whole of the Fourth District." A thin smile. "Whoever's feeding the Assembly, beast-speaker, they're wearing your colours. Not ours."

Then he was gone, down the culvert steps into the dark, and the faint shimmer of water at his wrists went with him.

---

Romeo sent his position to Juliette at the quarter-bell, as promised, from the top of the canal bridge, and two words with it, because anything more would sit in the Choir's logs forever. *Coming home.*

The reply came instantly. *Good. Walk faster.*

He walked faster.

It was full dark now, and cold, and the city was quiet. He went back along the canal towpath, past the boarded chapel with its gold flames, past the bakery, past the dead tram stop. Sergeant rode on his shoulder in silence. Every few minutes the crow turned his head and looked at him. Just looked.

"I know," said Romeo. "I know. I took it off."

Sergeant kept looking.

Romeo got as far as the base gate before he noticed.

He had stopped walking. He was standing in the middle of the road outside the gate, in the lamplight, with his head lifted and turned slightly to one side. Breathing in. Slowly. Deeply. Through his nose.

He could smell the mess kitchen. Ordo's stew, three walls away. He could smell the stables at the back of the motor pool. He could smell, very faintly, on the cold air, a particular kind of soap that he knew, absurdly, was the soap Susan de Ricci used, and that she had walked through this gate an hour ago, and that she had stood just here, by the left-hand post, for a long time, as if waiting.

He could smell all of it. Clear as words on a page.

He had let the dog go. He was sure he had let the dog go. He had felt her leave.

He stood very still in the lamplight with the night air in his nose, and understood that some of her hadn't.

On his shoulder, Sergeant made a single low sound, deep in his throat. Not disgust. Not a click. Something Romeo had never heard the crow make in nine years.

It sounded, almost, like worry.

"It's fine," said Romeo quietly. "It's just a nose. It'll wear off."

And it would. He knew that much. He had felt it before, smaller, after long nights with the rats: a twitch, a craving for crumbs, gone by breakfast. It was not a gift. It was not something he got to keep. It was a stain, the kind you get from leaning too long against wet paint, and every time it lasted a little longer before it faded. That was the part that frightened him. Not what he could smell. How long it took to stop.

He walked through the gate.

He was very careful, all the way across the parade ground, not to sniff the air again. And very careful, when he got to the barracks, not to notice that he could still smell exactly which way Susan had gone.
