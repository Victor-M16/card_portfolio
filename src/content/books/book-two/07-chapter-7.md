They told her in the Kiln, at the fifth bell, because it was the only place on base where nobody else ever came that early.

She stood in the cold among her tacks and her marbles and her river stones and listened to all of it. The pumping station. The old woman with the lens. The seed pulsing under Dimitri's hand like a heart. The four messages. *Councillor A. Halloran.* The Shore Order. *Look after my bell.* And four words in the dark, one minute before a man set fire to the inside of his own head.

*They're still down there.*

When they had finished, nobody said anything for a while. Dimitri looked exhausted. Romeo was being very careful not to sniff the air. Juliette was watching Susan's face.

"Her hands," said Juliette. "In the Assembly. You said they were too still. Like someone holding something heavy."

"Yes."

"Do you think it's this?"

Susan thought about the Speaker at her lectern. Small and silver and sharp. Proposing thirty days in a tired voice, almost apologetically, at the very end of a long day. *She doesn't know what to do with him either,* Nonna had murmured.

Or she knew exactly what to do with him, and didn't want to.

"I don't know," Susan said. "But I know who might." She bent and began, very carefully, to pick her tacks up out of the dirt one by one. "There's a founding Assembly in this story. A secret order. Things that families agreed never to ask about." She dropped the tacks into her pouch. "Chartered families are founding families. That's what a charter *is*."

"Your grandmother," said Romeo.

"My grandmother," said Susan. "But not first."

---

They were holding Matteo in the old stockade at headquarters, a row of stone rooms below the parade ground that had once held deserters and now held, mostly, quiet. He had a cot, a chair, a small barred window at the top of the wall that showed the boots of passing soldiers, and a shelf of books someone had brought him that he hadn't read.

He looked up when the guard let her in, and for a moment his face did something it hadn't done since the cellar. It opened.

Then he saw she was alone, and it closed again.

"Little sister."

"Matteo."

"Come to see the family vault?" He tried to smile. It didn't work. "How's Vesper?"

"Old. Fine. Papa brings her apples." Susan pulled the chair round from the wall and sat down facing him. "She misses you. Bastiano says she looks for you at the stable door."

Matteo looked away, at the little barred window.

They sat there for a while. Boots went past outside. Somewhere above, a sergeant was shouting at a squad.

"I need to ask you something," Susan said. "And I need you to tell me the truth, because I can't tell when you're lying. I was raised not to."

"You took me to a Zetan last time."

"I know. I'm sorry I had to." She meant it. She was surprised how much. "I'm not taking you anywhere now. It's just me."

He looked at her for a long moment.

"Ask," he said.

She folded her hands in her lap. She didn't fiddle with the ring.

"Did Nonna ever tell you stories?" she said. "When you were small. About Psi. About before."

Matteo went perfectly still.

And then, slowly, horribly, he started to laugh.

It wasn't a cruel laugh. That was the worst of it. It was the laugh of a man who has been holding something in his chest for a very long time and has just been asked, by the one person he least expected, to put it down.

"She never told you," he said. "Did she. Not once."

"Told me what?"

"Gods of the Sown." Matteo put his face in his hands, still laughing, and then he wasn't laughing anymore, and he sat there with his face in his hands for a long time, and Susan waited.

When he looked up, his eyes were wet.

"Every night," he said. "Every single night, until I was nine. She'd come up to the nursery after dinner in her green, and sit on the end of my bed, and tell me about the world. About the Landing. About the ships, and the long dark, and the shore. About the tall ones in the mountains, who were old before the Zetans were young." His voice had gone soft. "And about the Quiet Ones."

Susan felt the hair rise on her arms.

"Who are the Quiet Ones?"

"The ones under the hill." Matteo was looking past her now, at nothing, at a nursery a long way away. "That's what she called them. The Quiet Ones, who live in the deep places, in the oldest stone, and drink the noise of the world. That's why it's quiet in the old places, she said. Because they're drinking. Because they're listening. And because they're old and tired and the noise hurts them." He swallowed. "She had a little rhyme. Gods, I haven't thought of it in years. *Hush in the cellar and hush on the hill, the Quiet Ones listen and the Quiet Ones still. Keep the faith and keep the vine, and what's beneath stays under the line.*"

The words dropped into the little stone room like stones into a well.

*They're still down there.*

"She said the Sown remembered," said Matteo. "When everyone else forgot. That's what the faith was for. The shrine on the hill. The living altar. The whole family religion. It wasn't really about being planted. It was about keeping faith with whoever did the planting. With whoever was still underneath." He laughed again, shakily. "I used to lie awake afterwards listening to the house. I was sure I could hear them. Under the cellars. Breathing."

"And when you were nine?"

His face changed.

"When I was nine they tested me," he said. "Properly. The de Ricci way, in the conservatory, with Nonna and Papa and a dozen pots of seedlings. To see how strong I'd be." He held out one hand and looked at it as if it belonged to someone else. "Trace and a half. I made a bean sprout lean, a little. Nonna watched me do it for a long time. And then she said, *well,* and patted my head, and went downstairs."

"Matteo..."

"She never came up again." He said it simply. "Not once. Not for a story. She'd say goodnight at the door. She'd ask if I'd done my lessons. She was kind. She was always kind." His mouth twisted. "But the stories stopped. And then you were born, and you were strong, *two* gifts, a de Ricci miracle, and I waited for her to start telling them to you instead. And she didn't. And I didn't understand why. And I was so *glad*, Susan. I was so glad she hadn't given them to you. Because they were the only thing she'd ever given me that you didn't have."

Susan couldn't speak.

"That's an ugly thing to say to your sister," said Matteo. "I know. I've had a lot of time in here to work out how ugly I am." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "There. That's the truth. You don't need a Zetan for that one."

Susan looked at her brother. At his red-rimmed eyes and his wine-stained shirt and the little barred window behind him. At the boy who had run beside her down the long drive holding Vesper's bridle, shouting *you're doing it, you're doing it*. At the man who had found the real books and asked what else the water could buy.

She thought of Nonna in the Assembly, standing, with a hand on Susan's shoulder, saying *a gift is owed something in return.*

She thought: *and what did you owe him, Nonna? When his gift came small?*

She got up out of the chair.

She knelt down on the cold stone floor in front of her brother's cot, and put her arms round his neck, and held on. He went stiff with surprise. Then, after a long moment, something in him gave, and he put his face against her shoulder, and his whole body shook.

"Thank you," she said into his hair. "For the stories. For telling me."

"They're not much."

"They're everything," said Susan. "Matteo, they're *everything*. You've been carrying them for seventeen years. You're the only one who has them."

She drew back and looked at him.

"I'll come back," she said. "I'll bring Vesper's apples. And you'll tell me the rest of them. All of them. Every one she told you."

Her brother looked at her for a long time, with his face wet and astonished.

"All right," he said.

---

The conservatory at the estate was warm and green and dim, the glass roof misted over against the cold outside. The orchids hung in their baskets. The old fig stood against the far wall, its leaves dark and broad and still.

Nonna Livia was sitting among them in her green, as she was every evening, with her hands folded on her cane.

She did not look round when Susan came in. She did not need to.

"You've been to see your brother," she said.

"Yes."

"And he told you about the stories."

Susan stopped in the middle of the stone path between the orchids.

"You knew he would," she said.

"I hoped he would." Her grandmother's voice was very quiet. "It seemed fairer that you should hear it from him. Everything else in this family, he has had to watch you be given. This one thing, at least, he could give you himself."

Susan stood very still.

"He was nine, Nonna."

"I know."

"You stopped coming up. You never told him why."

"I know."

"He's spent seventeen years thinking you stopped because he wasn't worth telling."

The old woman was silent for a long time. In the green dark, among the plants that knew her, she looked very small. Smaller than Susan had ever seen her.

"I stopped," said Livia de Ricci, "because the stories are not stories. They are a weight. And I looked at a little boy who could barely make a bean sprout lean, and I thought: *he will never be strong enough to carry this.* I thought I was sparing him." Her hands tightened on the head of her cane. "I was wrong. I was not sparing him. I was deciding he was not worth the burden. And a child knows the difference, even if he cannot name it. I have known that for many years, and I have never once said it aloud." She turned her head, finally, and looked at Susan with her clear old eyes. "There. I have said it. I am ninety-one, and I am a de Ricci, and I am not perfect, and I have hurt the people I love by deciding what they could bear." A pause. "You may tell him I said so. I would like him to know."

Susan couldn't answer. She sat down on the low bench across from her grandmother, the way she had on the night of her first dinner home, and looked at her hands.

"And me?" she said, after a while. "Why didn't you tell me? I had two gifts. I was strong. I was everything you wanted."

"Because you were everything I wanted," said Nonna. "And I was afraid of what you would do with it."

---

The old fig against the wall rustled, very faintly, though there was no wind under the glass.

"Tell me about the Shore Order," said Susan.

Her grandmother did not ask how she knew the name. She looked at Susan for a long time. Then she nodded, slowly, as if something had been settled.

"Three hundred years ago, when the coast cities finally joined under one Assembly, there were twenty-two families who held the oldest records on Eta," she said. "Older than the cities. Older than anyone could count. Accounts copied and recopied for longer than there have been names for years, all the way back to the Landing itself. What met the ships when they came down. What was agreed on the beach." She paused. "And what was not. Those twenty-two families brought their records to the first Assembly, and the first Assembly decided what to do with them."

"The founding Assembly."

"The founding Assembly. Twenty-two seats." Nonna smiled very slightly. "The same twenty-two you sat beside last month, on the lowest tier, on the eastern side. The oldest names. The final word."

Susan felt cold all the way through.

"The charters," she said slowly.

"Ah. Now you see it." Her grandmother's voice was gentle. "Every school in Haven teaches that the chartered houses were granted their privileges for founding the Assembly. For uniting the coast. And that is true, as far as it goes." She tapped her cane once on the stone. "But a charter is a contract, child. It has two sides. The Assembly gave us our land, and our water, and our seats, and our children's exemption from service. And in return, the twenty-two founding families agreed to one thing."

"To forget," said Susan.

"To *keep* forgetting. Generation after generation. To build the schools that teach the empty shore. To put our names to the Shore Order, and seal every record of what met us on the beach, and never, ever ask what was still owed." Nonna's mouth tightened. "Our wealth is the price of our silence. Every bottle of de Ricci red. Every drop of water in the basin. Every exemption that kept a de Ricci child out of the war. It's all paid for. With a promise not to look down."

"And the Sown?"

"The Sown is the memory we were allowed to keep." Her grandmother looked round the conservatory, at the plants, at the old fig, at the living green all around them. "Dressed up as a faith, so nobody would mistake it for history. A shrine on a hill. A prayer at the altar. *We keep faith with the ones beneath.* Every de Ricci child has said those words. Very few have ever asked what they mean." She looked at Susan. "The ones who did, I told stories to. At bedtime. Where stories are safe."

Susan sat very still among the orchids.

"And Halloran?" she said. "She's not a chartered house."

"No. The Hallorans were never founders. But somebody outside the twenty-two families has always held the keys to the Order. A keeper. To make sure the families keep their word." Nonna's eyes were sharp. "Antiquities. The committee with the dull name, that nobody wants to sit on. Whoever chairs it, keeps the Order. And whoever keeps the Order has a way of becoming Speaker in the end." A pause. "Annelise Halloran is not a villain, child. Understand that. She is a jailer. She believes, with all her heart, that she is keeping something locked up for our own good."

"Is she?"

Her grandmother didn't answer for a very long time.

"I don't know," she said at last. "Nobody alive knows. That is the whole design of it. We have spent so long not looking that we have forgotten what we were not looking at." She smiled faintly. "Except, it seems, a young captain with a map, nineteen years ago. And now his son."

---

She got up.

It took her a long time. She put both hands on her cane and pushed, and Susan rose to help her, and she waved Susan away, and got up on her own, slowly, the way she did everything now. Then she walked, very carefully, along the stone path between the orchids to the far wall, where the old fig stood.

She put her hand on its trunk.

Susan felt the tree answer her. A slow, deep, rooted warmth, rising through the wood. And then something else. The bark under her grandmother's hand moved. Not much. A seam opened in it, a long narrow split down the grey trunk, like a mouth, and the wood inside it was smooth and pale and dry, and in a little hollow carved into the heart of the tree, wrapped in oiled green cloth, there was something flat.

Nonna Livia took it out.

The fig closed behind her hand. The bark sealed. In a moment, there was no seam at all.

"Every founding family was given one," said her grandmother. "A copy. Sealed. To be kept by the head of the house, and passed down, and never opened. The Order itself. In the words the founders wrote it." She unwrapped the oiled cloth. Inside was a thick envelope of old, old paper, gone brown at the edges, sealed with a disc of dark green wax pressed with a vine and a key. "My mother gave it to me. Her mother gave it to her. None of us has ever broken the seal. I don't believe anyone in any of the twenty-two houses has broken their seal in three hundred years." She held it out. "I am ninety-one. I am tired. And I was wrong about your brother, and I may well have been wrong about this."

Susan looked at the envelope.

"Nonna, I'm not the head of the house."

"Your father has resigned his seat. Your brother is in a cell. And I am sitting in a chair I gave up sixty years ago, because there is no one else." Her grandmother's eyes were very bright. "You watched their hands, child. You saw what Marr wanted. You saw what Halloran was carrying. You took your own brother to a Zetan because you would rather know the truth than keep the peace." She pressed the envelope into Susan's hands. "I have kept this family's silence for seventy years. I am giving it to you. You will decide whether to keep it."

The paper was old and dry and very light. Through it, Susan could feel the shape of folded pages. Several of them.

"And if I open it?" she whispered.

"Then you will be the first de Ricci in three hundred years who knows what we promised," said Livia de Ricci. "And what we were paid to forget." She lowered herself slowly back into her chair among the orchids. "Go on. Take it back to your soldiers. Your fire boy, and your captain, and that ridiculous young man with the crow." A faint, tired smile. "I like him, you know. He told the peacock it was beautiful."

Susan stood in the green dark with the sealed envelope in her hands.

"Nonna," she said. "Will you tell Matteo yourself? That you were wrong?"

The old woman looked at her for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "Tomorrow. I'll take the apples."

---

In the car back to Haven, Susan sat with the envelope on her knees and did not open it.

She watched the dark terraces go by, and the old vines on the gate turning their leaves towards her as she passed, and then the long coast road, and the grey sea.

She thought about twenty-two families, three hundred years ago, sitting round a table with records older than memory, deciding what everyone else would be allowed to know.

She thought about her brother, nine years old, lying awake in the nursery listening for something breathing under the cellars.

She thought about a rhyme.

*Hush in the cellar and hush on the hill,* *the Quiet Ones listen and the Quiet Ones still.* *Keep the faith and keep the vine,* *and what's beneath stays under the line.*

On the envelope on her knees, under the dark green wax, there was a single word written in a hand three hundred years old. She had not noticed it in the conservatory. She noticed it now, in the passing light of the coast road lamps. Faded, brown, careful, the letters of someone who had believed that what they were writing would never, ever be read.

*Beneath.*

Susan de Ricci closed her hands over it, and held it all the way home.
