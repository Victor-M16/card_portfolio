They called it the Kiln.

It had been an artillery yard once, back when the EDF still trusted chemical shells, before the omicrons made them as likely to go off in the breech as in the enemy's lines. A long rectangle of packed earth at the back of headquarters, walled in stone three metres thick, with a single iron gate at one end and a viewing gallery at the other, behind a slab of glass as thick as Dimitri's wrist. For eighty years it had been used to store broken transports. Three weeks ago, on Varga's orders, they had cleared it out, scorched the walls clean, and put a sign on the gate.

*RANGE NINE. AUTHORISED PERSONNEL ONLY.*

Under it, in chalk, somebody had written *THE KILN*. Nobody had rubbed it off. By the end of the first week, even the officers were calling it that.

It was his.

That was the strangest part. For eleven months Dimitri had been a cadet with no gift, training in the general yards with everyone else, learning to march and shoot and strip a rifle and keep his head down. Now he had a whole yard built for him, with walls that could take fire and a gallery where people could watch it safely. Every morning at the sixth bell, he walked through the iron gate, and every morning there were more people behind the glass.

This morning there were four.

Dr Pell, perched on a high stool with a slate on his knees and his spectacles pushed up onto his forehead. Observer Teyl, standing very straight with its hands clasped behind its back, its second eyelids sliding slowly across and back. A medic, because Varga insisted. And a tall, grey, broad-shouldered man in a training vest with no rank on it at all, who was not behind the glass. He was standing in the middle of the yard, waiting, with a wooden practice blade in each hand.

"Gale," said Master-at-Arms Hollis Brand. "You're late."

"It's the sixth bell, sir."

"The sixth bell is when you start. Late is anything after the fifth." Brand tossed him one of the wooden blades. Dimitri caught it, just. "And the rest of you. Out from behind the wall. I can hear you breathing."

From behind the stone buttress by the gate, Juliette stepped out first. Then Romeo, with Sergeant on his shoulder. Then Susan, in a training vest with her hair tied back so tightly it looked painful, and two small leather pouches on her belt that Dimitri had never seen before.

"We were observing," said Romeo.

"You were lurking," said Brand. "Observing is what the Zetan does. Get in here."

---

Hollis Brand did not have a gift.

Dimitri had found that out on the first day, and had not believed it. Brand was fifty-four years old and had served thirty-one years in the EDF, twelve of them at the Kesh Ridge, and in all that time he had never once registered more than a trace. He couldn't stir a breath of air or make a puddle tremble. He was, in the language of the omicron tables, nothing.

On the first day, he had knocked Dimitri flat on his back in four seconds with a wooden stick.

"Your gift," Brand had said, standing over him, "is a very large hammer. It is not a hand. Anybody who only has a hammer thinks every problem is a nail, and gets killed by the first man who brings a knife. Your gift is the last thing you reach for. Not the first. Get up."

Dimitri had got up. He had been getting up for three weeks.

This morning, Brand made them fight him one at a time.

Juliette went first. The brace on her left forearm was black and slim now, not a sling, and she held that arm close, but she moved like it wasn't there. She came at Brand low and fast, and Dimitri, watching, saw for the first time what she did with the air when she fought. Not big things. Small things. A breath of pressure behind her heel on every step, so that she moved half a beat quicker than her body should have allowed. A puff of air at Brand's eyes as she feinted. And something Dimitri couldn't see but could feel, prickling on the back of his neck: the whole yard's air going very still around her, so still that every movement Brand made pushed against it, and she knew where he was going before he got there.

"She hears you," Romeo said quietly, beside him. "The air. Every move you make, it ripples. She's been doing that since she was eight. Used to win every game of hide-and-seek on the base."

Brand was still faster. He was always faster. But it took him forty seconds to put Juliette on the ground, and when he did, he stood back and looked at her with something that, on a less stony face, might have been respect.

"Better," he said. "The arm's slowing your guard. Stop protecting it. Either use it or fight like you've only got one."

"Sir."

"Vasquez. The male one."

Romeo handed Sergeant to Susan, who took the crow as if handed a live grenade, and walked into the middle of the yard.

He didn't attack. He stood there, loose, with the wooden blade held carelessly at his side, and closed his eyes.

And the pigeons came down.

There were always pigeons on the walls of the Kiln. Dozens of them, grey and fat and stupid, roosting in the cracks in the old stone. Dimitri had stopped noticing them. Now they lifted off the walls all at once, in a soft, clattering cloud, and wheeled over the yard, and dropped low, round Brand's head, a whirling grey storm of wings.

Brand didn't even flinch. He swung his blade through the cloud as if it were smoke. But he had to swing it, and while he did, Romeo moved.

Dimitri had seen Romeo spar a hundred times. He was good: quick, loose, clever, always a joke away from a foul. This was different. Romeo came in low, almost on all fours, and he moved wrong. Too fast. Too smooth. A sideways skitter like something with four legs, a duck under Brand's blade that no human spine should bend for, and a lunge from a crouch that covered three metres in a single spring.

Brand caught him anyway. By the collar, in mid-air, and slammed him into the dirt. But Brand's face had changed.

"What was that?" he said.

Romeo lay on his back in the dust, panting, grinning up at the sky. "Hound," he said. "From the kennels. Old Bess. She's got the best reflexes on base. I borrowed them."

"You can borrow reflexes?"

"I can now." Romeo sat up. His eyes were very bright, and he looked, for a moment, not like himself at all. Then he blinked, and he was Romeo again. "Didn't used to. Been practising. A bit at a time. It's like wearing someone else's coat. If you leave it on too long, it starts to fit." He grinned, but Dimitri, who knew about the hill-cat, saw the edge in it. "Don't worry, sir. I take it off before dinner."

Brand looked at him for a long moment.

"See that you do," he said. "De Ricci."

---

Susan walked into the middle of the yard and opened one of the leather pouches on her belt.

She took out a pinch of something small and dark, and held it up on her palm so Brand could see. Seeds. Dozens of them. Then she closed her hand.

"Ready, sir."

Brand came at her.

And Susan threw the seeds.

Not at him. Around him. A flat, scattering spray, flung wide across the dirt, and as they left her hand she did the other thing, the thing she'd told her mother she'd never do at table. Every seed stopped in mid-air. Hung there, for half a heartbeat, in a perfect ring round Brand's feet. Then dropped, very precisely, each one to a different spot on the packed earth, like pins pressed into a map.

And she grew them.

The yard exploded green. Thin, tough, whipping vines burst up out of the dirt in a ring, so fast Dimitri could hear them, a sound like tearing paper, and wrapped themselves round Brand's boots, his knees, the wooden blade in his hand. In two seconds, the master-at-arms was standing in a cage of living green up to his waist.

The yard was very quiet.

Behind the glass, Dr Pell had stood up off his stool.

Brand looked down at the vines round his legs. Then he looked at Susan. Then, with great deliberation, he dropped the wooden blade, took a short steel knife from his belt, and began, calmly, to cut himself free.

It took him eleven seconds.

"Good," he said, stepping out of the wreckage. "Very good. Combined use. Kinetic placement, flora growth. Fast. Neat." He sheathed the knife. "And if I'd been carrying a lamp, or a lighter, or if I'd been standing next to him" he jerked his thumb at Dimitri "you'd have lost the lot in a second. Plants burn, de Ricci. Find out what else you can throw."

Susan's chin went up. "Yes, sir."

"And carry more seeds. Two pouches is a picnic. You're going to war."

Then he turned, and looked at Dimitri, and the yard went quiet in a different way.

"Gale."

---

He lost. He always lost.

He was not allowed to use fire against people. That was the first rule of the Kiln, Varga's rule, written on the wall in white paint by the gate. *NO FIRE AT A PERSON. NOT IN TRAINING. NOT IN ANGER. NOT EVER, WITHOUT ORDERS.* So he fought Brand with the wooden blade, the way he had for three weeks, and he was slower than Juliette and clumsier than Romeo and less clever than Susan, and Brand put him on his back in nineteen seconds.

"Better than yesterday," Brand said. "Yesterday was twelve."

Dimitri lay in the dust and looked up at the sky. And felt it.

He felt it every time he lost. A heat, under his ribs, rising. Not anger. Not quite. Something older than anger. It turned over in him like a big animal in a small cage, and it said, very quietly, *you don't have to lose. You never have to lose again. Just open your hand.*

It had been saying that for three weeks. Ever since the trial hall. Ever since a voice in the fire had said *I'm taking my hand away*.

He breathed. In. Out. The way Juliette had taught him in the Undercroft, a lifetime ago.

The heat went down.

When he opened his eyes, Brand was standing over him, looking down, with an expression Dimitri couldn't read.

"It wanted out just then," Brand said. "Didn't it."

Dimitri didn't answer.

"I've seen your face do that every day for three weeks," said Brand. "Every time I put you down. You get this look. Like a man holding a door shut with his back." He held out his hand. "That's the only thing I can teach you that matters, Gale. Not the stick. That. The door. Every day you hold it shut when it wants to open, you get a little stronger. The day you stop holding it is the day you stop being a soldier and start being a weapon. And weapons don't get to choose who they're pointed at."

Dimitri took his hand. Brand pulled him up.

"Fire drills," said the master-at-arms. "Alone. Everybody else, behind the glass."

---

The fire drills were Dr Pell's.

There were no fire instructors in the EDF. There never had been. Fire wielders above trace were so rare that in three hundred years the EDF had trained exactly four, and all of them had been dead for a century, and none of them had left notes worth reading. So Dr Pell had built the drills himself, from scratch, out of theory and old books and an enormous amount of trial and error, mostly error, mostly on the first week, when Dimitri had melted the iron gate.

They had a new gate now.

"Size," said Dr Pell's voice, through a brass speaking tube from behind the glass. "Coin, please."

Dimitri opened his hand. A coin of gold fire, perfectly still.

"Fist."

He let it grow. A fist. Still.

"Head."

A head. Still. The heat came off it in a wave, and he held it, and it stayed exactly where he put it, exactly the size he wanted, not a flicker, not a tremor.

"Good. Distance. Put it on the post."

There was a stone post at the far end of the Kiln, twenty metres away, scorched black. Dimitri looked at it. He felt the flame in his hand. He felt the distance between them, like a rope he hadn't let out yet.

He let it out.

The flame lifted off his palm and drifted down the yard, slowly, still, a little gold sun floating at head height through the cold morning air, and came to rest on top of the stone post, and sat there.

Behind the glass, he heard Romeo say *oh, show off* very quietly.

"Hold it there," said Dr Pell. "Now walk away from it."

That was the hard one. He turned, and walked towards the gate. Five metres. Ten. Fifteen. With every step the rope between him and the flame got thinner, and harder to hold, and he felt it starting to fray, and he felt the flame starting to want, starting to lean, starting to reach for the stone, the dirt, the air, anything.

At eighteen metres, it began to flicker.

At twenty, it went out.

"Twenty-two yesterday," said Dr Pell. "Eighteen the day before. You're tired. That's fine. That's the point." A pause. "Come in, cadet. Everyone. I want to show you something."

---

Dr Pell had a jar.

It was an ordinary glass specimen jar, the kind he kept beetles in, with a heavy stopper and a little brass dial set into the lid. He put it on the table in the gallery, between the four of them and Teyl, and folded his thin old hands on top of it.

"Three weeks ago," he said, "in the trial hall, Captain Vasquez pulled every breath of air out of the centre of a burning room. And the fire went on burning." He looked at Juliette over his enormous spectacles. "You told the inquiry it burned on nothing."

"Yes, sir."

"Fire," said Dr Pell, "does not burn on nothing. Nothing burns on nothing. That is the first law of everything. If a fire does not eat air, it eats something else." He tapped the brass dial on the jar's lid. "This is an omicron counter. A small one. It tells me how many omicrons are in the air inside this jar. The answer, at the moment, is the same as the air in this room. Ordinary. Psi-normal." He looked at Dimitri. "Cadet. Put a flame in the jar. Coin-sized. Then I'll seal it."

Dimitri looked at the jar. Then at Juliette.

She nodded, very slightly.

He opened his hand over the open mouth of the jar and let a coin of fire drop into it. It sank to the bottom and sat there on the glass, gold and still. Dr Pell pressed the heavy stopper down. A hiss of seal. A click.

The flame burned.

In a sealed jar, with no way for air to get in, it burned exactly as it had in the open. Not a flicker. Not a dimming. A little gold teardrop on the bottom of the glass, perfectly content.

Everyone was looking at the dial.

The needle was moving. Very slowly. Downward.

Dimitri felt his breath catch.

"The air in the jar," said Dr Pell softly, "is the same as it was. The same oxygen. The same everything. Except the omicrons." He watched the needle creep. "Those are going down. Steadily. At a rate proportional, I'd guess, to the size of the flame." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, a little too carefully, and put them back on. "It isn't burning air, cadet. It's burning *them.*"

Nobody spoke.

Beside Dimitri, Juliette had gone very still. She was looking at the jar, but her head was tilted, the way it did when she was listening to the air. And her face, very slowly, was changing.

"It feels wrong," she said quietly. "Near the jar. The air. It feels..." She stopped. Searched for the word. "Thin. Not less air. Less of... something. Like when you go deaf in one ear, a little." She looked up at Dimitri. "I can't hear it properly. Right next to your flame. I can't hear the air."

On the other side of the table, Susan had put her hand on one of the seed pouches on her belt. Dimitri saw her face go pale. She opened the pouch and looked inside.

The seeds at the top of the pouch, the ones nearest the jar, had gone grey.

Not burned. Not dried. Just grey. Dull. Like something with the life taken out of it.

"Doctor," said Romeo carefully. "What happens if he does it big? Not a coin. Like in the trial hall. A whole room."

Dr Pell did not answer for a long time.

Then he reached out and put his old hand flat on the top of the jar, over the brass dial, so that none of them could see the needle anymore.

"I would ask you all," he said very quietly, "not to mention this jar to anyone. Not in the mess. Not in a sent message. Not anywhere the Choir can draw a line round it." He looked round the table, at each of them. Last, at Dimitri. "There are people in this city who already call you a monster, cadet. I would rather they did not find out that you might also be a cure."

"A cure for what?"

Dr Pell looked at him. His eyes, behind the spectacles, were very old and very tired and, Dimitri realised with a cold shock, very frightened.

"For everyone else," he said.

By the window, Observer Teyl had not moved. Its hands were still clasped behind its back. Its head was tilted, and its second eyelids were closed, both of them, clear and still. Listening. Not to the jar. To the room. To five human hearts, all beating very fast.

Then it opened its eyes and took out its slate and began to write, slowly, in curling Zetan script.

Juliette, who could read it, watched over its shoulder. Dimitri watched her face. He saw her read the first line. He saw her jaw tighten.

"What does it say?" he said, later, when they were alone on the stairs.

She didn't answer for a moment.

"It's a heading," she said. "For a new report. Formal register." She looked at him. "*Concerning the still flame, and whether it eats the world.*"

---

The bells started at the ninth hour.

Not the bells of the base. The city's bells. All of them, from the old towers along the canal, ringing the long, rolling, uneven peal that meant only one thing in Haven, and had meant it for three hundred years.

Fire.

Dimitri was in the mess when it started. He was holding a mug of Ordo's tea. And the moment the first bell rang, before anyone in the room had even stood up, before the Choir had pushed a single alert, he felt it.

Far away. Across the city. Past the canal. In the Fourth District.

A fire.

Not a still one. A wild one. Hungry and fast and spreading, eating wood and paint and old cloth, and it was wrong. It tasted wrong, in the back of his throat, the way the chemical charge at the dye works had tasted wrong. Sharp and greasy and deliberate. Somebody had poured something on it first.

And under it, inside it, like a second heartbeat under the first, he could feel a hundred tiny flames he had felt once before. Candles. Dozens of them. Lit all at once, along the walls of a long low room, in a building at the bottom of some stone steps.

He dropped the mug.

Juliette was already on her feet across the table. She had seen his face.

"Where?" she said.

"The chapel," said Dimitri. "The Long Voyage. Somebody's set it on fire." He was already moving. "Orla's inside. Juliette, she's inside, I can feel her candles..."

"Romeo. Susan." Juliette didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to. "Now."

They ran.
