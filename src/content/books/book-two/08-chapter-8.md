The fifth bell at the Haven Academy rang in the dark, the way it had every morning for two hundred years.

Juliette had been awake for it every morning since she was fourteen, and before that, as a barracks child, she had been awake for the fifth bell at four other bases, and every one of them rang it long before the sun was up. Her theory was that somebody, very long ago, had decided that soldiers should always start the day before they were ready. That was the lesson. You would never be ready. Get up anyway.

She was up before it this morning. She usually was. She lay on her back on the narrow bed in her quarters with her hands folded on her stomach and listened to the base wake around her.

Captains had single rooms. That was the first thing you noticed, when you made captain. Not the stripe on your collar or the extra pay, which was very little, or the way the first-years stopped talking when you walked into the mess. It was the door. For two years in the fourth-year halls she had slept in a room of four, and before that, for two years as a first- and second-year, in the Long Dormitory: thirty iron beds in two rows under a vaulted ceiling, thirty footlockers, thirty girls breathing in the dark, and a duty cadet at the door with a lamp. She had liked the Long Dormitory, though she'd never have admitted it. She had liked the sound of thirty people breathing. It meant nobody was alone.

Now she had a door that closed, and a window, and a desk, and nobody breathing but her.

She thought, as she did most mornings at this hour, about Petra.

Petra Lowe had slept in the bed next to hers for two years in the Long Dormitory. Earth wielder, small and freckled and loud, from a fishing family in Varra who had wept with pride when the polite men in grey came for their daughter. Petra had snored. Petra had stolen Juliette's socks. Petra had been the only person at the Academy who wasn't frightened of her, and had told her so, frequently, at volume. *You're not scary, Vasquez. You're just sad and you think it's the same thing.*

Petra had been in the warehouse at the Varra docks when the roof came down.

Juliette lay on her back and let herself think about her for exactly the length of time it took the fifth bell to stop ringing. That was the rule she'd made, two years ago. One bell's worth of Petra, every morning. No more. If she let it be more, she would not get up.

The bell stopped.

She got up.

---

The letter was on her desk where she'd left it the night before. She'd read it four times already. She read it again while she laced her boots.

*Vasquez,*

*The Ridge is cold and the coffee is worse than Ordo's, which I did not think was possible. My squad of lieutenants are mostly idiots but one of them can actually shoot. The Zetans across the line wave at us in the mornings now, since the truce. I don't know whether to wave back. I've started to.*

*I saw you on the screens. The trial hall. You on your knees in front of that fire. I don't know what you were doing and I won't ask. But I recognised the look on your face. It was the one you had at fourteen, the first week, when I put you on the wall run and you fell off it eleven times and got back on it twelve. I told the Academy Board that week that you'd be a captain before you were twenty. They laughed. Tell them I said so.*

*Look after your brother. Eat something green occasionally. Don't let Brand bully you; he only does it to people he likes.*

*H. Brennick, Lt.* *Kesh Ridge Line, Post Four*

She folded it and put it back in the drawer with the others. There were eleven now. One a month, since he'd been reassigned to the Ridge last spring. Ordinary orders. A lieutenant's rotation; every officer at the Academy did two years at a frontier post sooner or later. He'd told her about it in the mess, cheerfully, over a plate of Ordo's eggs. *Somebody's got to stop the Zetans waving at nobody, Vasquez.* And then he had gone, and the instructor who replaced him on the wall run was perfectly competent and perfectly forgettable, and Juliette had gone on getting up at the fifth bell and running the wall and eating Ordo's eggs, and had not once let herself think about how much she missed him.

She thought about it now, for exactly as long as it took to tie her second boot.

Then she went out.

---

"Captain Vasquez?"

The girl was waiting outside her door.

Juliette hadn't heard her. That was the first thing she noticed. She heard almost everyone, almost always; the air in a corridor told her who was coming long before they arrived. But this girl was standing perfectly still against the opposite wall, in the first-years' grey with the single white stripe on the cuff, with her hands at her sides and her chin up, and she wasn't breathing hard, and she wasn't fidgeting, and the air around her was so still it was almost not there.

She was small. Fourteen, Juliette guessed; fifteen at most. Dark-skinned, with her hair cropped very short, the way girls cropped it in their first month when they realised how long it took to dry after the wall run in the rain. She had a scar through her left eyebrow, an old one. And she was holding a folded slip of paper in her right hand as if it were a weapon she was prepared to use.

Juliette recognised her. She had seen her at breakfast, at the first-years' tables, the day Romeo had made Ordo give him a second sugar cube. The girl with the shaved head who'd grinned at Romeo, and ducked her head into her porridge, and been hit by her friends.

She'd let her hair grow a little since then.

"Cadet," said Juliette.

"First-Year Cadet Wren Okoye, Captain." The girl held out the slip of paper. "Shadowing assignment. Major Lisk says I'm yours for the week."

Juliette took the slip. She knew what it said before she read it. Every term, in the fourth week, every first-year was assigned to shadow a cadet captain for five days. To see what a captain did. To learn what it meant to lead. It was one of the Academy's oldest traditions and most of the captains hated it, because a first-year at your heels for five days was like having a very small, very earnest, very loud dog.

Juliette had shadowed Captain Idaline Marsh at fourteen. Marsh had made her carry her kit for five days and spoken to her twice. Juliette had learned more in those five days than in the previous year.

"Why me?" she said.

The girl's chin went up a fraction more. "I asked for you, Captain."

"Nobody asks. It's assigned."

"I asked Major Lisk eleven times." A pause. "She said if I asked a twelfth she'd put me on the stones for a week. So I didn't. And then she assigned me to you anyway."

Juliette looked at her.

At the cropped hair and the scarred eyebrow and the stillness. At the chin. At the way the girl was looking back at her, very steadily, without blinking, as if she had decided long ago that if she blinked first she would lose something she couldn't afford to lose.

*You're not scary, Vasquez. You're just sad and you think it's the same thing.*

"What's your gift, Okoye?"

"Air, Captain."

Of course it was.

"Can you hear it?"

Something flickered in the girl's face. Surprise. Then something that might have been hope, very quickly hidden.

"Sometimes," she said. "Not like you. I've watched you on the training yards. You know where everyone is. I can push it. I can push it really hard. But I can't..." She stopped. "Instructor Fenn says I'm all hammer."

Juliette almost smiled.

"Carry my kit," she said, and handed her the bag. "Keep up. Don't talk unless you've got a question, and if you've got a question, ask it. Don't save it for later. Later's when people die."

Wren Okoye took the bag. It was nearly as big as she was.

"Yes, Captain," she said, and fell in behind her, and did not, as far as Juliette could hear, make a sound.

---

She had questions. Of course she did. She held them for nearly a full minute, all the way down the stairs and across the corridor to the mess, and then they came out in a single breath, as if they'd been pressing against her teeth.

"Captain, is it true you made captain at sixteen?"

"Yes."

"The rules say fourth-year minimum. And eighteen."

"The rules say *ordinarily*. Read the whole sentence." Juliette pushed open the mess doors. The noise hit them like a wave: three hundred cadets, four years, and the clatter of tin plates and Ordo shouting at someone about the porridge. "The Academy Board can promote a cadet early in exceptional circumstances. It's in Article Six. Nobody reads Article Six."

"What were the exceptional circumstances?"

Juliette walked to the captains' table without answering.

It stood at the end of the hall, under the long windows, on a platform one step up from the floor. One step. That was all. But nobody sat at it who hadn't earned the step, and nobody walked past it without lowering their voice. There were twelve cadet captains at the Academy this term. Eight were at breakfast. They looked up as Juliette came in with her shadow trailing her kit. A couple nodded. One, a tall red-haired boy at the far end, gave a slow, deliberate, mocking little salute.

Captain Cassius Dray. Fourth-year. Water. Squad Four. He had been a captain for three months and resented Juliette for every single one of the two years she'd had on him. His squad and hers had been placed first and second in every term's exercises since he'd made captain; first and second, and he had been second every time.

"Vasquez," he said. "I see they've given you a pet. How nice. You'll have something to talk to now your squad's always on leave."

"Dray." Juliette sat down. "I see your water's still going everywhere except where you point it."

The captains' table made a low sound that might, on a less dignified platform, have been laughter. Dray's face went tight. Wren Okoye, standing at Juliette's shoulder with the kit bag, very carefully did not smile.

"Sit," said Juliette, and pointed at the end of the bench. "Shadows sit at the end. Eat. Don't speak to anyone at this table unless they speak to you first."

"Not even Captain Dray?"

"*Especially* not Captain Dray."

The girl sat. Ordo came by, slammed down two plates of eggs and a single sugar cube on Juliette's tray without a word, and walked on. Juliette looked at the sugar cube. Then she broke it in half and put one half on Wren's plate.

The girl stared at it as if it were a medal.

"The exceptional circumstances," Juliette said, quietly, under the noise, "were that I was in a training squad on a live operation at the Varra docks, two years ago, and the operation went wrong, and two of my squad were caught under a building. One of them died. And I chased the wielder who did it into a dead zone and the inquiry decided I'd behaved with conspicuous courage." She picked up her fork. "Three weeks later the Board read Article Six. That's all."

Wren Okoye looked at her for a long moment. She had stopped eating.

"What was her name?" she said. "The one who died."

Juliette's fork stopped halfway to her mouth.

Nobody had asked her that. In two years. Not the inquiry, not the Board, not Varga. They had asked her what happened, and how long, and what she saw, and whether she'd lost sight of the target. Nobody had asked her the name.

"Petra," she said. "Petra Lowe. Earth. Fishing family. She snored."

Wren nodded slowly, as if filing it somewhere important.

"My dad was at the Ridge," she said. "Logistics. Not a wielder. A shell went off in the store when they were unloading. Before the truce." She looked down at her eggs. "They gave my mum a letter. It said *conspicuous courage* too."

They sat there for a moment in the roar of the mess hall, the captain and the first-year, not looking at each other.

Then Juliette ate her eggs, and after a while, so did Wren.

---

She showed her the day.

Not the parts they put in the Academy prospectus. The real ones. The captains' briefing at the half-past-six, in a cold room off the command corridor, where Major Edda Lisk, the Master of Cadets, read out the day's duty roster for the twelve squads in a voice like warm bread, and gave every captain a boiled sweet from a tin on her desk, and remembered every one of their birthdays, and had once, in Juliette's second year, put a fourth-year on the stones for a fortnight for calling a first-year a barracks brat.

"The stones?" Wren whispered afterwards, in the corridor.

"You've not been on them yet?"

"No, Captain."

"You will." Juliette walked faster. "Everybody goes on the stones eventually. The parade ground. Hands and knees, with a brush and a bucket, scrubbing the flagstones from one end to the other. It takes about six hours. Your knees bleed by the third. And at the end, the duty officer walks the whole length of it in white gloves, and if he finds a single mark, you start again." She glanced down. "Three demerits in a term and you lose your leave. Five and you're on the stones. Eight and you're in front of the Board."

"How many have you had?"

"Forty-one."

Wren stopped walking.

"In my first year," said Juliette, without stopping. "I was on the stones nine times. I know every flagstone on that parade ground by its first name. Keep up, Okoye."

She showed her the squad yards, where the twelve squads drilled in rotation, and the posted training plans pinned to the board outside each one: Squad Nine's, in her own tight handwriting, with every hour of every day for the next four weeks filled in, and a column on the right headed *Individual*, and under it four names and four lines. *Gale: control, distance, Brand daily. De Ricci: precision, endurance, non-flora projectiles. Vasquez R.: duration limits, see me. Vasquez J.: arm.*

"Why does it say *see me* next to your brother?" said Wren.

"Because it does."

She showed her the Omicron Theory hall, where Dr Pell was teaching the first-years their second lesson on load and affinity, the same lesson he had taught Juliette four years ago and Romeo beside her and every cadet for forty years before that, with the same jokes in the same places. Wren knew it already. She mouthed the jokes along with him. When Dr Pell asked the hall what an omicron was, Wren's hand went up first, straight as a spear, and Juliette, standing at the back, watched the girl's face as she answered, and recognised the expression exactly. It was the face of somebody who has decided that if she knows everything, nothing can surprise her.

"He's a real doctor?" Wren asked afterwards. "Like a medic?"

"No. Doctor of the Omicron Sciences. Forty years of research. He's never set a bone in his life." Juliette paused. "He once tried to treat a burn with a lecture. Romeo still talks about it."

And she showed her the barracks. The Long Dormitory, empty at this hour, thirty beds made with corners so sharp you could cut yourself on them. The fourth-year halls, rooms of four, two boys' wings and two girls' wings and a long corridor between them with a duty cadet's chair halfway along it.

"Why's there a chair?" said Wren.

Juliette looked at the chair for a moment.

"Article Twelve," she said.

Wren frowned. "What's Article Twelve?"

"You haven't read it yet?"

"I've read Articles One to Nine. Twelve's in next term's reading."

"Then I'll save you a term." Juliette leaned against the corridor wall, by the chair. "Article Twelve says that no cadet shall enter into any romantic attachment with another cadet within the same chain of command. No captain with anyone in their own squad. No cadet with their own instructor. Anything that might compromise the judgement of a soldier in the field." She paused. "Which is why there's a chair."

Wren considered this.

"Does it work?"

Juliette almost laughed.

"No," she said. "It doesn't work at all. You put three hundred eighteen-year-olds in a building and tell them the one thing they're not allowed to do, and suddenly it's the only thing any of them can think about." She looked down the corridor. "Second-years climb out of windows. Fourth-years bribe the duty cadet with sugar. There's a broom cupboard on the third floor of the east wing that has a reputation going back sixty years. Everybody knows. The duty officers know. Major Lisk definitely knows." She shrugged. "Mostly they look the other way, until somebody's stupid enough to get caught in front of somebody who can't."

"So it's allowed."

"No. It's not allowed. It's *survived*. There's a difference." Juliette pushed off the wall. "The rule's there for a reason, Okoye. If I'm out in a dead zone and someone in my squad is under a beam, I need to make the right call. Not the call that keeps the person I love alive. The call that keeps the most people alive." She heard her own voice, flat and certain, reciting it the way she'd recited it to herself for two years. "That's what Article Twelve is for. So a captain never has to choose."

Wren looked at her very steadily.

"And do you agree with it, Captain?"

The corridor was very quiet.

Juliette thought about a cellar under a dye works, and a coin of gold fire floating between them, and the air turning slowly around them like breath. She thought about a ballroom, and a hand held out, palm up, and every candle on the windowsill gasping. She thought about a towpath in the dark, and a hand she had taken in full view of the Choir and anyone else who cared to look.

She thought about the beam at Varra. About Petra.

"I'm a captain," she said finally. "I enforce it."

Wren Okoye, fourteen years old, with her cropped hair and her scarred eyebrow and her stillness, looked at Juliette for a long moment, and very clearly noticed that it was not an answer.

She didn't say so.

*Clever girl*, Juliette thought, and was not sure whether she was pleased or frightened.

---

The review was at the second bell after noon.

It happened every quarter, for every cadet captain: forty minutes in a panelled room on the command corridor, standing at a rail in front of a long table, while three senior officers went through your squad's record line by line and asked you to explain it. It was not a punishment. It was not a trial. It was, the Academy prospectus said, *an opportunity for reflection and mentorship.*

Every captain at the Academy dreaded it more than the stones.

She left Wren on the bench outside the door with the kit bag, and the instruction to sit still and listen to nothing.

"How will I know if it goes well?" said Wren.

"You won't," said Juliette. "Neither will I. That's how you'll know it went normally."

She went in.

Three officers behind the long table. She had stood in front of them seven times before and knew them as well as she knew the flagstones.

In the centre: Commander Ilse Varga, in plain grey, perfectly still, a heron in the shallows. Juliette looked at her and thought, as she had thought every day for a week, of four words on a cracked slate in an old woman's workshop. *Look after my bell.* And thought: *you knew. Nineteen years. And you never said.*

She did not let her face move.

On Varga's left: Major Edda Lisk, Master of Cadets, round and grey-haired in a cardigan over her uniform that was strictly against regulations and that nobody had ever dared mention, with her tin of boiled sweets at her elbow and a pair of half-moon spectacles on a chain. She smiled at Juliette over them as if Juliette were a favourite niece arriving for tea.

And on Varga's right: Colonel Marcus Thorne.

Thorne was High Command's liaison to the Academy. He came down from the command towers once a quarter for the reviews and otherwise stayed as far from cadets as it was possible to stay while still being responsible for them. He was tall and lean and hard-faced, with iron-grey hair and an old burn scar down one side of his neck, and in seven reviews he had never once smiled at Juliette, never once praised her, and never once let her finish a sentence without interrupting it.

She was fairly certain he hated her.

She was fairly certain, too, that he was the reason she had not been offered the instructor's post Brennick had held, last spring, though she'd been the obvious choice and Lisk had told her so. Thorne had read her file, apparently, and said only: *too young. Too much like her mother.* And that had been the end of it.

She had never known what he meant. She had never asked.

"Captain Vasquez," said Varga. "Squad Nine. Quarterly review. Begin with your operational summary."

Juliette began.

She gave it the way she always gave it: flat, clean, short. Patrol rotations completed. Training hours logged. The Tannery Street operation. The dye works. The de Ricci reception. The Fourth District fire. She kept her voice level. She left out everything she had always left out.

Major Lisk made little notes and nodded. Varga said nothing at all.

Colonel Thorne let her get as far as the Fourth District before he cut in.

"Your squad's leave record, captain."

"Sir."

"It's irregular." He turned a page. "Cadet de Ricci. Seven absences from base in the last four weeks. Four of them overnight. Two of them to the de Ricci estate. Three to the Assembly district." He looked up. "Explain."

"Cadet de Ricci is on charter attendance leave, sir. Granted by the Commander. Her family's seat in the Assembly passed to her grandmother last month. Cadet de Ricci is the acting heir. The Assembly requires the heir present at all sittings where the house votes."

"The Assembly requires." Thorne's mouth thinned. "The EDF requires that its cadets be on base and in training. Which requirement do you suppose takes precedence, captain?"

"The Commander's ruling, sir."

A very small pause. Varga did not move.

"And the de Ricci estate? Is the Assembly sitting in a vineyard now?"

"Family matters, sir. Her brother's..."

"I know where her brother is." Thorne turned another page. "I know where all of them are. That's my job. Cadet Vasquez, R.: eleven absences from barracks after the last bell in four weeks. Fourth District. Canal towpath. Old pumping station." He looked up. "Do you know what your brother does at an old pumping station at midnight, captain?"

Juliette kept her face perfectly still. *He's a crow. Nobody looks at him.*

Somebody had looked.

"He trains, sir," she said. "Fauna. The canal has the densest animal population in Haven. Rats, birds, strays. His individual plan specifies duration work. It's posted."

"I've read it. It says *see me.*" Thorne's eyes were very cold. "Have you seen him?"

"Daily, sir."

"And Cadet Gale." Another page. "Welfare visit to the Fourth District last week. Two hours. With his captain." A pause. "Who visits an old woman for two hours, captain?"

"She'd nearly burned to death, sir. And she knew his mother."

Thorne looked at her for a long moment. Then he closed the file and set his hands flat on top of it.

"Here is what I see, captain," he said. "I see a squad that is never where it is supposed to be. I see a dual wielder who walks out of the gate whenever her grandmother sends a note. I see a fauna cadet who spends his nights in the most lawless district in the city. And I see the most-watched cadet on the continent, a boy the Assembly is going to sit in judgement on in three weeks, being escorted around Haven at night by a captain who is, as far as I can tell, accountable to nobody but the Commander." His voice did not rise. "Your squad is the most powerful unit at this Academy, Vasquez. It is also the least disciplined. Those two facts together make me very uneasy."

Juliette said nothing. Her heart was very quiet. She made sure of it.

"Colonel," said Major Lisk gently. "The squad's training scores are the highest in the Academy. By some distance. The de Ricci girl's precision work alone..."

"I've seen the scores, Edda." Thorne didn't look at her. "Scores are what cadets do when they know they're being watched." He looked at Juliette. "I'm interested in what they do when they think they aren't."

The room was very quiet.

"Effective immediately," said Colonel Thorne, "Cadet de Ricci's charter leave is restricted to sitting days of the Assembly only. Escorted, there and back. Cadet Vasquez, R., is confined to barracks after the last bell until further notice. Training on the canal is to take place in daylight, logged, with an instructor present. Cadet Gale does not leave the base without an officer above the rank of cadet captain." He paused. "And the squad's captain will submit a written account of her squad's movements to me personally, every seven days, for the remainder of the term."

Juliette felt the floor tilt very slightly under her.

She looked at Varga.

Varga had not moved. She was sitting perfectly still in the centre of the table with her hands folded, looking at Juliette, and her face showed nothing at all. She did not object. She did not overrule. She let it stand.

*Which requirement takes precedence, captain?*

"Is that understood, Captain Vasquez?" said Thorne.

"Yes, sir."

"Good." He stood. He picked up the file. And then, at the end of the table, he stopped, and looked back at her, and for the first time in eight reviews, something in his hard face shifted. Not softened. Shifted. Like a stone turned over.

"Your mother," he said, "used to look at me exactly like that. Across a map table at the Ridge. Every time I gave her an order she thought was stupid." A pause. "She was usually right."

Juliette stared at him.

"She was also," said Colonel Thorne, "dead within the year. Because she was very brave, and very sure of herself, and did not like to be told where she could and couldn't go." He held her eyes. "Write your reports, captain. Keep your squad where I can see them. I have buried one Vasquez. I would prefer not to make it three."

He walked out.

The door closed behind him. The room was silent.

Major Lisk took off her half-moon spectacles and polished them on her cardigan, and then reached into her tin and held out a boiled sweet across the table.

"He was her lieutenant, you know," she said quietly. "At the Ridge. Before he was a colonel. He carried her body back." She put the sweet down on the edge of the table, near the rail, when Juliette didn't take it. "He's never once said a kind word to you in two years, and he's read every single one of your reviews twice. He's the one who signed your Article Six."

Juliette couldn't speak.

She looked at Varga.

Varga looked back. Still. Grey. Unreadable.

"Dismissed, captain," said the Commander.

---

Wren Okoye was sitting on the bench outside exactly where Juliette had left her, with the kit bag on her knees and her hands folded on top of it and her face blank.

She stood up when Juliette came out.

Juliette walked past her down the corridor without a word. After a moment, she heard the girl fall in behind her. Silently. Keeping up.

They were halfway down the stairs before Wren spoke.

"I wasn't listening, Captain. Like you said."

"Good."

"But the door's very thin."

Juliette stopped on the landing.

The girl stopped too, two steps up, with the kit bag clutched against her chest. She looked at Juliette with her steady unblinking eyes.

"He doesn't hate you," said Wren. "The colonel. I couldn't hear everything. But I could hear his voice when he said the last bit." She hesitated. "My mum sounds like that. When she talks about Dad."

Juliette stood on the landing and looked up at her shadow.

She thought about a man with a burn scar down his neck, carrying her mother's body back across the Ridge. Reading her file twice. Signing her Article Six. Saying *too young, too much like her mother,* and meaning something she had never once, in two years, thought to ask about.

She thought about how much of her life she had spent deciding what people meant, and being wrong.

"Okoye," she said.

"Captain?"

"How many demerits have you got this term?"

The girl's chin went up. "Two."

"Get a third," said Juliette, "and you lose your leave. And then you'll be stuck on base every Restday with nothing to do but the wall run." She turned and went on down the stairs. "Which I do at the fifth bell on Restdays. Alone. If anyone wanted to come and fall off it eleven times."

Behind her, there was a very small silence.

"Yes, Captain," said Wren Okoye, in a voice that was trying very hard not to sound like anything at all.

---

Susan came back at the last bell. Escorted, for the first time: a bored corporal walked her to the barracks door and signed her in and left.

She found Juliette in the squad room, alone, with eleven letters from the Kesh Ridge Line laid out on the table in front of her, unopened, as if she had been about to read them all again and had stopped.

Susan didn't ask about the letters. She sat down across the table, and took the envelope out of the inside of her jacket, and set it in the middle.

Old brown paper. A disc of dark green wax pressed with a vine and a key. One word, faded, in a hand three hundred years old.

*Beneath.*

Juliette looked at it for a long time.

"Thorne's restricted your leave," she said. "Assembly days only. With an escort."

"I know. The corporal told me." Susan's hands were folded on the table. She wasn't fiddling. "He's not wrong, you know. I've been walking in and out of here like it's my mother's house."

"He's not wrong," said Juliette.

They sat in silence.

"Do you want to read it?" said Susan.

Juliette didn't answer at once.

She thought about the Academy prospectus. *An opportunity for reflection and mentorship.* About Article Six, and Article Twelve, and a chair in a corridor that nobody believed in. About forty-one demerits and nine times on the stones. About Petra, who snored. About a lieutenant at the Ridge who had told the Board she'd be a captain before she was twenty, and a colonel who had carried her mother home, and a commander who had sat still and let it all happen and had been sitting still, Juliette now knew, for nineteen years.

She had followed the rules her whole life. All of them. Except once, in the rain.

And every day for a month, she had been finding out how many of the people who made those rules had been keeping secrets, and how many of the secrets had been kind.

"No," she said. "Not yet."

Susan looked at her.

"I've spent my whole life," said Juliette slowly, "trusting that the people above me knew what they were doing. Even when I didn't like them. Especially then." She looked at the envelope. "And today I found out I don't even know which of them hate me and which of them have spent two years making sure I don't die." She was quiet a moment. "If I read that, and it says what I think it might say, then I don't know who I'm working for anymore. And I've got a squad, and a first-year who thinks I know what I'm doing, and a colonel who wants a report on my desk in seven days."

"So when?"

"When I know who I'm working for," said Juliette. "Or when I stop caring."

Susan was quiet for a long time. Then she reached out and picked up the envelope and put it back inside her jacket, against her chest.

"I'll keep it," she said. "Until you're ready. Or until I'm not."

She stood, and went to the door, and stopped there.

"Juliette. The first-year. The one who's been carrying your kit all day." A faint smile. "Romeo says she reminds him of you."

"Romeo should mind his own business."

"He says she's a hawk," said Susan. "A very small one. Still learning which way the wind blows."

She went out.

Juliette sat alone in the squad room with eleven letters and a sugar cube Ordo had left on her tray at lunch that she hadn't eaten. After a while she picked up the top letter and opened it and read it, slowly, all the way through, for the fifth time.

*Tell them I said so.*

Then she got up, and put the letters back in their drawer, and went to find her brother, who was confined to barracks after the last bell until further notice and would, she knew, be absolutely unbearable about it.
